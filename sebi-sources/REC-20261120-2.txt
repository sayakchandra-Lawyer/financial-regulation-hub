Consultation Paper on proposal to review the Additional Disclosure Framework
for Foreign Portfolio Investors

1. OBJECTIVE
1.1. To improve ease of doing business for Foreign Portfolio Investors (FPIs) by

       reviewing the Additional Disclosure Framework to provide a more investor
       friendly regulatory framework, aligned with global standards and thereby making
       India a preferred investment destination.

2. BACKGROUND
2.1. Foreign capital has been an important constituent in India's economic expansion

       especially since the 1991 liberalization reforms. Foreign capital helps in bridging
       the gap between domestic savings and investment requirements and also
       provides vital foreign exchange to support a growing economy.

2.2. However, the opportunities that such foreign capital provides also come with their
       attendant risks. Mitigation of such risks demands that adequate safeguards are
       in place. Identification of beneficial ownership in the financial system is an
       important mandate emerging from the need to prevent, control and combat
       money laundering, terrorism financing and other related threats for the integrity
       of the international financial system.

2.3. Financial Action Task Force (FATF) is the international policy making body
       established in 1989 to lead global action to tackle money laundering, terrorist and
       proliferation financing. Currently, 40 countries/jurisdictions (including India) are
       members of FATF and more than 200 countries and jurisdictions have committed
       to implement the FATF’s standards. FATF Recommendations provide a
       comprehensive framework of measures to help countries tackle illicit financial
       flows. Recommendation 10 requires financial institutions to undertake Client Due
       Diligence (CDD) and identify the Beneficial Owner (BO) of their clients. While
       FATF has provided respective jurisdictions the flexibility to prescribe their own
       thresholds to determine ownership of client based on ownership interest, it has
       recommended that such threshold should not exceed 25%.

                                                       Page 1 of 7
2.4. In India, ‘The Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002’ (PMLA) has been
       enacted to, inter alia, prevent money-laundering. Prevention of Money
       Laundering (Maintenance of Records) Rules, 2005 (PMLR), provides for
       identification of BO of clients on the basis of controlling ownership interest and
       control. In terms of Rule 9(3) of PMLR, the current threshold for controlling
       ownership interest is 10% /15% depending on the legal form of the client.

2.5. For accessing Indian securities market, Foreign Portfolio Investors (FPIs) register
       with SEBI and also enter into an account based relationship with a custodian,
       bank, depository participant and stock broker as a client. Thus, the PMLR
       requirements related to CDD norms including identification of BO as per the
       extant 10%/15% ownership threshold are applicable to FPIs as well.

2.6. A comparative analysis of global practices adopted for identification of BOs
       indicates that several emerging markets which are FATF members adopt a
       relaxed ownership threshold (typically 25%). Further, developed markets such as
       USA, UK, Canada, Luxembourg, Belgium, Portugal, Netherlands, France,
       Germany, Singapore, Japan and Hong Kong also follow a norm of 25% threshold,
       with lower threshold applicable in some jurisdictions only for high-risk entities.
       Thus, going by global standards, India follows a more stringent regime for
       identification of BOs. There may be a need to review the PMLR, 2005 to make it
       consistent with other jurisdictions. However, this is under the jurisdiction of
       Ministry of Finance.

2.7. Over and above the aforementioned requirement of BO identification, SEBI has
       prescribed an Additional Disclosure Framework (ADF)1. ADF, inter alia, provides
       the criteria for identification of FPIs that are required to submit granular details of
       all entities holding any ownership, economic interest, or exercising control in the
       FPI, on a full look through basis, upto the level of all natural persons, without any
       threshold. The criteria provided under ADF are as follows:

2.8. An industry-wide Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) has also been adopted in
       consultation with SEBI, for implementation of the ADF.

3. NEED FOR REVIEW of SEBI’s ADF
3.1. In today’s global financial landscape, capital is highly mobile and responsive.

       Investors have access to a vast universe of markets and securities, and their
       allocation decisions are shaped by a range of factors. For any investee
       jurisdiction, many of these determinants remain exogenous and beyond direct
       control. In such times, any measure that increases the compliance burdens may
       reduce the attractiveness of Indian capital markets for foreign investors.

3.2. As stated earlier, the granular disclosure requirements under ADF have been
       prescribed by SEBI over and above the BO identification requirements specified
       under PMLR. Further, such granular disclosure requirements under the ADF are
       unique to India.

3.3. Such disclosure requirements have to be weighed against the prevailing
       requirements in our own jurisdiction as well as other jurisdictions competing for
       the same pool of global monies. Disclosure requirements that are not in line with
       globally accepted practices could potentially place India at a competitive
       disadvantage vis-à-vis peer jurisdictions with relatively lenient regulatory
       requirements.

       billion) from Indian equity market.

3.5. While there may be several factors behind such FPI outflows, the relatively
       onerous rules regarding BO identification and disclosures required as per ADF
       as compared to most of the FATF compliant countries (both developed and
       emerging) necessitate a review of existing processes and rules.

3.6. Policymaking involves a careful balancing act of minimising both Type I errors
       (over-regulation that stifles innovation and market efficiency) and Type II errors
       (under-regulation that may expose the system to risks). Continuous evaluation of
       policy outcomes, informed by stakeholder feedback and evolving market
       conditions, is therefore essential. Accordingly, regulatory fine-tuning has to be an
       ongoing process.

4. ISSUES UNDER CONSIDERATION
4.1. SEBI has received constant feedback from FPIs and other market participants

       regarding the stringent disclosure requirements especially when no such
       disclosure requirements are present in other competing jurisdictions. It is

                                                       Page 4 of 7
       observed that ADF is giving rise to several practical challenges, including the
       following:

         a) FPIs are required to identify and collect information from significantly larger
             number of underlying investors for their Indian investments as compared to
             investments in other jurisdictions.

         b) A global fund which invests in different countries is forced to set up a different
             fund for its Indian investments as compliance levels are different, resulting in
             additional operational and compliance costs.

         c) In case of pooled investment vehicles such as hedge funds, investor holdings
             may change frequently due to subscriptions and redemptions, necessitating
             repeated identification exercises and periodic updates.

         d) Many investors may not be accustomed to providing information beyond
             what is required in their home jurisdictions, leading to practical difficulties in
             obtaining the requisite information and documentation.

         e) In case of FPIs with complex structures, such as ‘fund of fund’ arrangements
             and segregated portfolios, tracing ownership through multiple layers to
             identify natural persons for the purpose of regulatory compliance can be
             time-consuming and costly.

4.2. Many foreign investors looking to invest in India consider the granular disclosure
       requirements as additional compliance burden. Rather than investing in India and
       be subject to granular disclosures, many investors may instead choose to avoid
       investing in India. Therefore, the disclosure requirements for breach of
       ‘concentration criteria’ have practically become an entry barrier for FPIs looking
       to invest in India. In any case, even if an FPI holds a concentrated position in a
       listed entity, such investment is capped at 10% of the shareholding of the listed
       entity.

4.3. Further, specifically with regard to ‘size criteria’, it may be noted that PN3, inter
       alia, provided that an entity or a citizen of a country which shares land border with
       India, or where the beneficial owner of an investment into India is a citizen of any
       such country, can invest only under the Government route. While the conditions
       prescribed under PN3 were not made applicable to investments made by FPIs,

                                                       Page 5 of 7
       considering the potential for misuse of the FPI route for circumventing the
       stipulations of PN3, SEBI had included the ‘size criteria’ under the ADF.

4.4. It may be noted that, in absence of a clear definition of BO under PN3, the term
       was purported to include any ownership directly or indirectly. Therefore, even a
       small stake by a person from land bordering countries used to come under the
       purview of conditions placed by PN3. Vide Press Note 02 of 2026 (PN2) dated
       March 15, 2026, Government of India reviewed the FDI Policy and modified the
       investment conditions prescribed via PN3. The definition of ‘beneficial owner’ has
       now been clarified to include only those persons who hold more than the
       thresholds prescribed under PMLR.

4.5. As mentioned earlier, FPIs are also required to provide details of the BOs as per
       the thresholds prescribed under PMLR. Therefore, as far as identification of BO
       is concerned, the position under PN3 (as clarified by PN2) is similar to
       requirements already specified for FPIs. Therefore, the additional requirement
       under ADF to identify all entities holding any ownership or economic interest or
       control in the FPI in case of breach of ‘size criteria’, may not be required.

5. PROPOSAL
5.1. Considering the challenges faced by foreign investors and the change in FDI

       policy as mentioned above, it is proposed to remove the Additional Disclosure
       Framework for FPIs.

5.2. Since the requirements under ADF are also applicable to subscribers of Offshore
       Derivative Instruments (ODIs) and segregated portfolio(s) maintained by FPI, the
       provisions under ADF, applicable to ODI subscribers and segregated portfolio(s)
       of FPI, are also proposed to be removed.
