Phoenix ARC Pvt Ltd v. Spade Financial Services Ltd

Collusive loans and related parties in the creditors' committee

The facts

In the insolvency of a real estate company, two entities connected with its promoter group claimed to be financial creditors on the basis of inter-corporate deposits and a property development arrangement, and sought seats on the committee of creditors. An asset reconstruction company holding genuine debt objected that the transactions were collusive and that the claimants were related parties who had shed that status before the insolvency began.

The question

Are sham or collusive transactions 'financial debt', and can an entity that stopped being a related party before insolvency sit on the committee of creditors?

What the court held

  • A transaction that is sham or collusive is not a financial debt, even if it is dressed up as a loan. The money must have been disbursed against consideration for the time value of money in a real transaction.
  • The definition of 'related party' is wide. The bar in the first proviso to section 21(2) is normally tested at the time the committee is formed, but it also covers a creditor that ceased to be a related party only to get around the bar.

What it means in practice

  • Genuine lenders can challenge the admission of promoter-linked claims and their voting share. Resolution professionals should look at the substance of inter-corporate deposits and at recent changes in shareholding or directorships of claimants.
  • A lender that takes equity, board seats or other control rights in a borrower should check whether it risks being treated as a related party. The Code has a specific carve-out for regulated financial creditors that become related only through conversion of debt into equity.
