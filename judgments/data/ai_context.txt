KEY JUDGMENTS REGISTER (107 rulings on financial services from the Supreme Court, High Courts, NCLAT and consumer and ombudsman forums, as on 2026-11-26). Each ruling starts with its id in square brackets.


[JUD-SHIKHA-AXIS] Shikha Ghosh v. Axis Bank Ltd | Delhi High Court | 2026-09-30 | FAO(COMM) No. 91 of 2025
Bench: Prathiba M. Singh and Dinesh Bhatt JJ.
Topic: Bank's mass use of one arbitrator. Category: Arbitration. Forum: High Courts. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Current. The appeal was part-heard when last listed in November 2026; later orders may follow.
Facts: A borrower challenged an award passed by a sole arbitrator whom the bank had appointed alone under the loan agreement. It emerged that the same arbitrator had been given thousands of the bank's recovery cases.
Issue: Is an award by an arbitrator appointed unilaterally by the lender enforceable, and what should regulators do about the practice?
Held: The award was unenforceable because the arbitrator had been appointed by the bank alone, following the Supreme Court's rulings on unilateral appointments.
Held: The bank was directed to explain on affidavit why thousands of cases had been sent to one arbitrator, and RBI was asked to caution banks against the practice.
What it means: Retail lenders that use the same arbitrator for bulk recovery cases risk unenforceable awards and regulatory scrutiny. Appointment through an institution or with the borrower's consent is safer.
Law involved: Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, ss. 12(5), 34, 37

[JUD-BHADRA] Bhadra International (India) Pvt Ltd v. Airports Authority of India | Supreme Court | 2026-01-05 | 2026 INSC 6, [2026] 1 SCR 30 | Civil Appeal Nos. 37-38 of 2026
Bench: J.B. Pardiwala and K.V. Viswanathan JJ.
Topic: Unilaterally appointed sole arbitrators. Category: Arbitration. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance, Stock broking.
Status: Current. Applies the Constitution Bench ruling in Central Organisation for Railway Electrification (2024).
Issue: Is an award by a sole arbitrator appointed by one party alone valid, and can the other party lose its objection by taking part in the arbitration?
Held: The unilateral appointment of a sole arbitrator is void from the start, and the arbitrator is ineligible under section 12(5) read with the Seventh Schedule. Where the arbitration agreement itself breaches section 12(5), no appointment made under it is valid.
Held: Ineligibility can be waived only by an express agreement in writing after disputes arise. It cannot be waived by implication or conduct.
Held: An arbitrator who is ineligible lacks inherent jurisdiction, so the objection can be raised at any stage, including for the first time in a challenge to the award under section 34.
What it means: Recovery awards obtained through a sole arbitrator named by the lender alone can be set aside even if the borrower took part without objecting. Lenders should obtain a clear written waiver after the dispute arises, or have the arbitrator appointed jointly, by an institution or by the court.
What it means: Lenders and brokers should review loan agreements, client agreements and pending arbitrations for unilateral appointment clauses.
Law involved: Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, ss. 12(5), 14, 18, 34 and Seventh Schedule

[JUD-PNBHF] PNB Housing Finance Ltd v. Manoj Saha | Supreme Court | 2025-07-15 | 2025 INSC 847, [2025] 7 SCR 1041 | Civil Appeal No. 9492 of 2025
Bench: P.S. Narasimha and Joymalya Bagchi JJ.
Topic: SARFAESI: tenants claiming occupation. Category: Security, charges and SARFAESI. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Housing finance, Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Issue: Can a person claiming an old, unregistered tenancy get back possession of a mortgaged property that a housing finance company has taken under SARFAESI?
Held: A person claiming tenancy through an oral or unregistered agreement must prove it with independent evidence such as rent receipts, tax receipts or electricity bills for the period before the demand notice under section 13(2).
Held: Even if proved, such a tenancy does not continue beyond one year from the section 13(2) notice; after that the occupant is a tenant at sufferance.
Held: A recital in the sale deed or a letter of attornment from the borrower is not enough. The High Court's order restoring possession was set aside.
What it means: Lenders can resist tenancy claims set up to block possession of mortgaged homes, by insisting on proof of occupation before the demand notice.
What it means: At loan origination, HFCs should record who occupies the property and on what terms.
Law involved: SARFAESI Act, 2002, ss. 13(2), 13(4), 17(4A); Transfer of Property Act, 1882; West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997

[JUD-DHFL] Piramal Capital and Housing Finance Ltd v. 63 Moons Technologies Ltd | Supreme Court | 2025-04-01 | 2025 INSC 421, [2025] 4 SCR 344 | Civil Appeal Nos. 1632-1634 of 2022
Bench: Bela M. Trivedi and Satish Chandra Sharma JJ.
Topic: Resolution of a housing finance company under the IBC. Category: Insolvency: starting and running the process. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Housing finance, Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current. From 26 May 2026 the substituted section 26 expressly provides that avoidance and fraudulent or wrongful trading applications do not affect the insolvency process and continue after it ends.
Facts: The Reserve Bank superseded the board of a large housing finance company and, as the first use of the special insolvency route for financial service providers, started its corporate insolvency. The approved resolution plan gave the benefit of any recoveries from fraudulent trading applications (section 66) to the resolution applicant, valuing them at a notional Re 1. A debenture holder objected, and the NCLAT sent that term back to the creditors. Fixed deposit holders demanded full repayment, and former promoters and directors sought to take part in the process.
Issue: Could the appellate tribunal reopen the plan's treatment of section 66 recoveries, are deposit holders entitled to full repayment, and can directors of a superseded board take part in the committee of creditors?
Held: The plan was restored. How recoveries are dealt with is a commercial decision of the committee of creditors, and the NCLAT could not substitute its own view.
Held: Avoidance applications (preferential, undervalued and extortionate transactions) are different from fraudulent and wrongful trading applications under section 66. The plan could assign section 66 recoveries to the resolution applicant, while the NCLT still decides those applications.
Held: A vote cast by the authorised representative for a class of creditors binds every member of that class, including those who voted against.
Held: Fixed deposit holders cannot claim full repayment by relying on section 45QA of the RBI Act or section 36A of the National Housing Bank Act. Once the plan is approved, they are bound by it.
Held: Directors of a board superseded by the Reserve Bank under section 45-IE are not 'suspended directors'. They cannot attend committee meetings or demand copies of the plan.
What it means: For lenders and bidders, the ruling confirms that the special route for NBFCs and HFCs works like an ordinary insolvency once it starts, with the Reserve Bank's administrator in charge and the old board out.
What it means: Depositors and debenture holders of a failed NBFC or HFC are creditors under the plan and take what the plan gives their class.
Law involved: Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, ss. 25A, 30, 31, 43-51, 66, 227; Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, ss. 45-IE, 45QA; National Housing Bank Act, 1987, s. 36A

[JUD-MAHAVEER] Mahaveer Sharma v. Exide Life Insurance Co. Ltd | Supreme Court | 2025-02-25 | 2025 INSC 268, [2025] 2 SCR 1311 | Civil Appeal No. 3562 of 2025
Bench: B.V. Nagarathna and S.C. Sharma JJ.
Topic: Substantial disclosure is enough. Category: Insurance claims. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Life insurance.
Status: Current.
Issue: Can a life insurer reject a claim because the insured disclosed one large existing policy but not some smaller ones?
Held: No. The insured had disclosed a Rs 40 lakh policy, much larger than the undisclosed policies and the policy in question. That was a substantial disclosure.
Held: The undisclosed policies were of inconsequential amounts, and their omission was not a material suppression. The repudiation was set aside.
What it means: Not every omission is material. Insurers should ask whether the missing fact would have changed a prudent insurer's decision before repudiating.
Law involved: Insurance Act, 1938, s. 45; Consumer Protection Act, 1986

[JUD-NCLAT-HOMEKRAFT] Home Kraft Avenues v. Jayesh Sanghrajka (Resolution Professional, Ornate Spaces Pvt Ltd) | National Company Law Appellate Tribunal | 2025-02-14 | Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 756 of 2023
Bench: Yogesh Khanna J. and Arun Baroka.
Topic: Unregistered charge in the CIRP. Category: Security, charges and SARFAESI. Forum: NCLAT. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Current.
Facts: In a company's insolvency a creditor's security interest had not been registered with the Registrar of Companies under section 77. The question was whether it could be classified as a secured creditor in the resolution process.
Issue: Does failure to register a charge stop a creditor being treated as secured in the CIRP?
Held: No. In the CIRP, a security interest can be proved by other evidence; non-registration under section 77 does not by itself stop the creditor being a secured creditor.
What it means: The position differs in liquidation, where registration or other proof under the Liquidation Regulations is needed (Volkswagen Finance, 2020). Registration remains the safe course.
Law involved: Companies Act, 2013, s. 77; Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, ss. 3(30), 3(31)

[JUD-CHOKSHI] AC Chokshi Share Broker Pvt Ltd v. Jatin Pratap Desai | Supreme Court | 2025-02-10 | 2025 INSC 174, [2025] 2 SCR 1545 | Civil Appeal No. 2227 of 2025
Bench: P.S. Narasimha and Sandeep Mehta JJ.
Topic: Broker-client arbitration and family accounts. Category: Securities markets and mutual funds. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Stock broking.
Status: Current.
Issue: Can a broker invoke exchange arbitration against a husband for his wife's trading debt, based on an oral arrangement?
Held: Yes. Courts must read contracts practically, looking at the parties' conduct and intention. The husband's oral instructions and the joint way the two accounts were operated showed joint and several liability.
Held: Separate client registration agreements, client codes and accounts did not change this. Arbitration under the exchange bye-laws was maintainable against the husband.
What it means: Brokers who act on a client's oral instructions to move credit balances to a family member's account may be able to recover from both. This does not relax SEBI's requirements on evidence of client orders, which still apply.
Law involved: Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, ss. 7, 34, 37; BSE Bye-laws, 1957

[JUD-NCLAT-ESSDEE] State Bank of India v. IDBI Bank Ltd | National Company Law Appellate Tribunal | 2025-01-28 | Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) Nos. 321 and 335 of 2024
Bench: Ashok Bhushan J. (Chairperson) and Barun Mitra.
Topic: Liquidation proceeds shared by admitted claims. Category: Insolvency: creditors, claims and distribution. Forum: NCLAT. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current. Subject to the Supreme Court's decision on the Technology Development Board appeal.
Facts: Secured lenders in a liquidation disagreed on how the sale proceeds should be shared: by the value of each lender's security or in proportion to admitted claims.
Issue: How are liquidation proceeds shared among secured creditors who relinquished their security?
Held: In proportion to admitted claims, following the Supreme Court in India Resurgence ARC v. Amit Metaliks (2021), subject to the outcome of the pending Supreme Court appeal against the Technology Development Board ruling.
What it means: Until the Supreme Court decides, liquidators distribute pro rata to admitted claims among secured creditors in the same class.
Law involved: Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, s. 53

[JUD-NCLAT-GST] Assistant Commissioner, CGST and Central Excise v. Pradeep Kabra (Cengres Tiles Ltd) | National Company Law Appellate Tribunal | 2025-01-23 | Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 844 of 2024
Bench: Ashok Bhushan J. (Chairperson), Barun Mitra and Arun Baroka.
Topic: Excise and GST dues in a resolution plan. Category: Insolvency: creditors, claims and distribution. Forum: NCLAT. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Facts: The tax department's admitted claim of about Rs 11.77 crore for excise and GST dues was given Rs 1 lakh under the approved resolution plan. It appealed, relying on Rainbow Papers to argue it was a secured creditor.
Issue: Are central excise and GST dues secured debt, entitling the department to more than the plan offered?
Held: No. These dues are operational debts. Rainbow Papers turned on the statutory first charge under the Gujarat VAT Act and did not apply. The appeal was dismissed.
What it means: Central tax departments rank as operational creditors. After the IBC (Amendment) Act, 2026, government dues have a fixed place in section 53 regardless of any statutory charge.
Law involved: Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, ss. 5(21), 30(2), 53; Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017

[JUD-HSBC-AWAZ] Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corp. Ltd v. Awaz | Supreme Court | 2024-12-20 | 2024 INSC 1044, [2024] 12 SCR 1482 | Civil Appeal No. 5273 of 2008
Bench: Bela M. Trivedi and Satish Chandra Sharma JJ.
Topic: Credit card interest rates. Category: RBI powers and lending conduct. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Issue: Could the National Consumer Commission cap credit card interest at 30% a year as an unfair trade practice?
Held: The National Commission's ruling that interest above 30% a year is usurious was set aside. It was contrary to section 21A of the Banking Regulation Act, which bars courts and tribunals from reopening a bank transaction on the ground that the interest is excessive, and it encroached on RBI's domain.
Held: Directions and guidelines issued by RBI are an extension of the statute. Interest charged as RBI's circulars allow is not an unfair trade practice in itself.
Held: The complaint was not maintainable: the trusts that filed it were not 'persons' or consumers, and no permission to sue in a representative capacity had been obtained.
What it means: Interest on credit cards and loans is set by the lender's Board-approved policy within RBI's framework, not by consumer forums. Transparency rules (KFS, APR and disclosure of charges) still apply.
What it means: The bar in section 21A is written for banking companies, so NBFCs should rely on the RBI framework and fair-dealing rules rather than on section 21A itself.
Law involved: Banking Regulation Act, 1949, s. 21A; Consumer Protection Act, 1986

[JUD-NCLAT-EIH] EIH Ltd v. Subodh Kumar Agrawal (Golden Jubilee Hotels) | National Company Law Appellate Tribunal | 2024-12-11 | Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 336 of 2020
Bench: Rakesh Kumar Jain J. and Naresh Salecha.
Topic: Plan can end existing contracts. Category: Insolvency: starting and running the process. Forum: NCLAT. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Facts: A hotel operator had a management contract with a hotel company that went into insolvency. The approved resolution plan terminated the contract, and the operator challenged the plan.
Issue: Can a resolution plan terminate the corporate debtor's existing contracts, and how far can the tribunals review the committee's decision?
Held: Yes. The committee's commercial decision to approve a plan that ends existing contracts cannot be questioned, provided the plan complies with section 30(2).
Held: Judicial review is limited to the grounds in sections 30(2), 31 and 61(3).
What it means: Counterparties of a borrower in insolvency, including service providers and lessees, may see their contracts ended by the plan.
Law involved: Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, ss. 30(2), 31, 61(3)

[JUD-HARERAM] Hare Ram Singh v. Reserve Bank of India | Delhi High Court | 2024-11-18 | 2024:DHC:8816 | W.P.(C) 13497/2022
Bench: Dharmesh Sharma J.
Topic: Unauthorised electronic transactions: customer's liability. Category: Customer protection and consumer disputes. Forum: High Courts. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Facts: A bank customer clicked a link in a message that appeared to come from his bank, and Rs 2.60 lakh was debited from his account in April 2021. He reported it at once to the bank and the police. The bank said he had shared his credentials and refused a refund. The Banking Ombudsman directed payment of only a part of the amount.
Issue: Who bears the loss from a phishing or vishing fraud that the customer reports promptly?
Held: Under the Reserve Bank's 2017 circular on customer protection in unauthorised electronic banking transactions, the burden of proving the customer's liability is on the bank.
Held: Clicking a fraudulent link is not by itself gross negligence. The bank did not show that the customer gave away his credentials, did not act promptly to block or recall the money, and its controls for authenticating the transactions fell short of the Reserve Bank's digital payment security requirements.
Held: The customer had zero liability. The bank was directed to refund Rs 2.60 lakh with interest at 9 per cent a year and pay Rs 25,000 in costs.
What it means: Banks, card issuers and prepaid instrument issuers should record the evidence of customer negligence at the time, act on a fraud report immediately (blocking, chargeback or recall) and be able to show that authentication worked. Without that, the loss stays with the institution.
Law involved: RBI circular on Customer Protection - Limiting Liability of Customers in Unauthorised Electronic Banking Transactions, 6 July 2017; RBI Master Direction on Digital Payment Security Controls, 2021

[JUD-CORE] Central Organisation for Railway Electrification v. ECI SPIC SMO MCML (JV) | Supreme Court (Constitution Bench) | 2024-11-08 | 2024 INSC 857, [2024] 11 SCR 2173 | Civil Appeal Nos. 9486-9487 of 2019
Bench: Five Judges, D.Y. Chandrachud CJI presiding.
Topic: Unilateral appointment of arbitrators. Category: Arbitration. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Current. Prospective for appointments after 8 November 2024, as stated for three-member tribunals.
Issue: Is a clause valid if it lets one party appoint a sole arbitrator, or makes the other side choose from a panel curated by that party?
Held: Equal treatment of parties applies at every stage of arbitration, including the appointment of arbitrators.
Held: A clause that lets one party unilaterally appoint a sole arbitrator gives rise to justifiable doubts about the arbitrator's independence and impartiality, and hinders the other party's equal participation. For a three-member tribunal, forcing the other party to pick its arbitrator from a panel curated by one side breaches equal treatment.
Held: A party can still expressly waive the objection in writing after disputes arise (proviso to section 12(5)). The Court said the law it laid down applies prospectively to appointments made after 8 November 2024, and that this direction applies to three-member tribunals.
What it means: Loan agreements that let the lender alone name the arbitrator are unsafe. Lenders should agree on the arbitrator with the borrower, use an arbitral institution, or apply to the court under section 11.
What it means: Awards by an arbitrator the lender appointed alone, without an express written waiver by the borrower after the dispute arose, are at risk of being set aside. Bhadra International (2026) confirms that such a sole arbitrator is ineligible.
Law involved: Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, ss. 12(5), 18

[JUD-BHOWMICK] State Bank of India v. Pallabh Bhowmick | Gauhati High Court | 2024-09-13 | Writ Appeal No. 364 of 2022
Bench: Lanusungkum Jamir and Kardak Ete JJ.
Topic: Unauthorised electronic transactions: proof of negligence. Category: Customer protection and consumer disputes. Forum: High Courts. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current. The Supreme Court dismissed the bank's special leave petition on 3 January 2025.
Facts: After a data breach at an online retailer, a fraudster posing as its customer care persuaded a customer to install a mobile application. Rs 94,204.80 was then taken from his bank account in October 2021. He reported it within a day. The bank refused a refund, alleging that he must have shared his one-time passwords, and the Banking Ombudsman closed his complaint. A single judge directed the bank to refund the amount.
Issue: Is a customer who is tricked into installing an application negligent, so that the bank need not refund the loss?
Held: The Division Bench upheld the direction to refund. The bank has to prove the customer's negligence with cogent material; a general allegation that he must have shared credentials is not enough.
Held: The loss arose from a third-party breach and was reported within the time allowed by the Reserve Bank's 2017 circular, so the customer's liability was zero. The bank had also not raised a chargeback.
What it means: Read with the Delhi High Court's ruling in Hare Ram Singh, the position is that prompt reporting shifts the loss to the bank unless it can prove the customer's negligence. An Ombudsman closure does not prevent the High Court from granting relief.
Law involved: RBI circular on Customer Protection - Limiting Liability of Customers in Unauthorised Electronic Banking Transactions, 6 July 2017

[JUD-BRS] BRS Ventures Investments Ltd v. SREI Infrastructure Finance Ltd | Supreme Court | 2024-07-23 | 2024 INSC 548, [2024] 7 SCR 2143 | Civil Appeal No. 4565 of 2021
Bench: Abhay S. Oka and Pankaj Mithal JJ.
Topic: Recovering from both borrower and corporate guarantor. Category: Guarantees, pledges and third-party security. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Facts: An NBFC lent Rs 100 crore to a company, with the borrower's holding company as corporate guarantor. In the guarantor's insolvency the NBFC received Rs 38.87 crore under the resolution plan. It then filed for insolvency of the borrower for the balance. The successful resolution applicant of the guarantor argued that the debt stood satisfied and that the borrower's assets were part of the guarantor's plan.
Issue: After receiving part payment in the corporate guarantor's insolvency, can the lender proceed against the principal borrower for the balance?
Held: Yes. Part payment under the guarantor's resolution plan does not extinguish the borrower's liability for the rest. The liability of borrower and surety is co-extensive, and the creditor can proceed against both, together or one after the other.
Held: A holding company does not own the assets of its subsidiary. The subsidiary's assets cannot be treated as part of the holding company's resolution plan.
Held: The guarantor (or whoever steps into its shoes) is subrogated to the creditor's rights only to the extent of the amount actually paid.
What it means: Lenders can file separate or simultaneous section 7 applications against the borrower and the corporate guarantor, and accepting a payout in one does not close the claim in the other, so long as total recovery does not exceed the debt.
What it means: Buyers of a guarantor company under a plan get subrogation rights only for what was paid, not control over the borrower's assets.
Law involved: Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, ss. 7, 18, 31, 36; Indian Contract Act, 1872, ss. 128, 134, 140

[JUD-SUJATHA] Mahakali Sujatha v. Branch Manager, Future Generali India Life Insurance Co. Ltd | Supreme Court | 2024-04-10 | 2024 INSC 296, [2024] 4 SCR 724 | Civil Appeal No. 3821 of 2024
Bench: B.V. Nagarathna and Augustine George Masih JJ.
Topic: Insurer's burden to prove suppression. Category: Insurance claims. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Life insurance.
Status: Current.
Issue: Who must prove that the insured suppressed other policies, and with what evidence?
Held: Where the insurer repudiates on the ground of suppression, the burden is on the insurer to prove that the fact suppressed was material and that the insured knew of it.
Held: The insurer had produced no documents to show the other policies. The repudiation could not stand.
What it means: Claims teams must keep documentary proof, such as other insurers' records, before repudiating for non-disclosure. Allegations alone will fail.
Law involved: Insurance Act, 1938, s. 45 (before the 2015 amendment); Evidence Act, 1872

[JUD-MILIND] Milind Patel v. Union Bank of India | Bombay High Court | 2024-03-04 | Writ Petition No. 3671 of 2023
Bench: B.P. Colabawalla and Somasekhar Sundaresan JJ.
Topic: Wilful defaulter process: disclosure and reasons. Category: Wilful default and fraud. Forum: High Courts. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Facts: A former joint managing director of an infrastructure finance group received a show cause notice from a bank proposing to declare him a wilful defaulter for the group's borrowings. The notice did not enclose the material relied on, and the orders of the bank's identification and review committees gave no real reasons.
Issue: What must a lender disclose and record when it declares a person a wilful defaulter?
Held: The lender must give the person all the material relevant to the proposed declaration, not only what it relies on. That includes material that helps the person's case.
Held: Both the identification committee and the review committee must pass reasoned orders, and those orders must be served on the person. The members of the committees who took the decision should be identifiable.
Held: The bank withdrew its orders, and the Court directed the credit information companies to remove the petitioner's name.
What it means: Lenders (banks and NBFCs to whom the wilful defaulter directions apply) should send the full record with the show cause notice, record reasons at both stages and keep the committee composition on file. Orders passed on a bare notice are likely to be set aside.
What it means: A wilful defaulter tag closes access to credit and has reputational effects, so courts hold lenders to a strict standard of fairness.
Law involved: RBI Master Circular on Wilful Defaulters, 1 July 2015 (since replaced by the 2024 Directions)

[JUD-YEZDANI] Shakti Yezdani v. Jayanand Jayant Salgaonkar | Supreme Court | 2023-12-14 | 2023 INSC 1076, [2023] 16 SCR 695 | Civil Appeal No. 7107 of 2017
Bench: Hrishikesh Roy and Pankaj Mithal JJ.
Topic: Nominee versus legal heirs. Category: Securities markets and mutual funds. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Stock broking, Mutual funds.
Status: Current.
Issue: Does a nominee of shares or securities become the owner on the holder's death, to the exclusion of the legal heirs?
Held: No. The nominee does not get absolute title. Vesting in the nominee under section 109A of the Companies Act, 1956 (section 72 of the 2013 Act) and Bye-law 9.11.1 under the Depositories Act is for a limited purpose.
Held: Nomination does not change the law of succession. The legal heirs, or the beneficiaries under a will, remain entitled.
What it means: Depository participants, brokers and registrars can transmit securities to the nominee to complete formalities, but the nominee holds them subject to the claims of the legal heirs.
What it means: Client communication should say clearly that a nomination is not a will.
Law involved: Companies Act, 2013, s. 72; Companies Act, 1956, ss. 109A, 109B; Depositories Act, 1996 and depository bye-laws

[JUD-JIWRAJKA] Dilip B. Jiwrajka v. Union of India | Supreme Court | 2023-11-09 | 2023 INSC 1018, [2023] 16 SCR 562 | Writ Petition (Civil) No. 1281 of 2021
Bench: D.Y. Chandrachud CJI, J.B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra JJ.
Topic: Insolvency of personal guarantors. Category: Guarantees, pledges and third-party security. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current. From 26-05-2026, s. 96(4), inserted by the IBC (Amendment) Act, 2026, provides that the interim moratorium does not apply to personal guarantors to corporate debtors. The rest of the ruling is unaffected.
Issue: Are sections 95 to 100 of the IBC, on insolvency of individuals such as personal guarantors, constitutional?
Held: Sections 95 to 100 are valid and do not violate Articles 14 and 21.
Held: The resolution professional only facilitates and recommends. No adjudication happens until the Adjudicating Authority decides under section 100, and natural justice must be observed at that stage.
Held: No hearing is needed before the resolution professional is appointed under section 97(5). The debtor can take part when the resolution professional examines the application under section 99. The interim moratorium under section 96 stays legal proceedings in respect of any debt.
What it means: Lenders can file insolvency against personal guarantors of corporate borrowers without a prior hearing on 'jurisdictional facts'. The guarantor is heard in the resolution professional's examination and before the Adjudicating Authority decides.
Law involved: Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, ss. 95-100

[JUD-IFCI-SUTANU] IFCI Ltd v. Sutanu Sinha | Supreme Court | 2023-11-09 | 2023 INSC 1023, [2023] 15 SCR 280 | Civil Appeal No. 4929 of 2023
Bench: Sanjay Kishan Kaul, Sudhanshu Dhulia and Ahsanuddin Amanullah JJ.
Topic: Compulsorily convertible debentures are equity. Category: Insolvency: creditors, claims and distribution. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Facts: IFCI had subscribed to compulsorily convertible debentures (CCDs) of a toll-road project company. In the company's insolvency it claimed as a financial creditor; the resolution professional rejected the claim because the project documents, the concessioning authority and the lenders had treated the CCDs as equity.
Issue: Are compulsorily convertible debentures debt or equity for a claim in insolvency?
Held: The CCDs were equity. The project documents treated them as equity, they were never recategorised with the concessioning authority's approval, and repayment obligations lay with the sponsor, not the company.
Held: Instruments compulsorily convertible into shares are generally equity, not debt. The holder was not a financial creditor.
What it means: Investors in CCDs rank as shareholders in insolvency. Those who want creditor rights must structure instruments as debt (for example, non-convertible or optionally convertible with a redemption obligation).
Law involved: Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, ss. 5(7), 5(8), 62

[JUD-CELIR] Celir LLP v. Bafna Motors (Mumbai) Pvt Ltd | Supreme Court | 2023-09-21 | 2023 INSC 838, [2023] 13 SCR 53 | Civil Appeal Nos. 5542-5543 of 2023
Bench: D.Y. Chandrachud CJI and J.B. Pardiwala J.
Topic: SARFAESI: right of redemption. Category: Security, charges and SARFAESI. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Current.
Issue: Until when can a borrower redeem a mortgaged property that a secured creditor is selling under SARFAESI?
Held: Under section 13(8) of SARFAESI as amended in 2016, the borrower's right to redeem ends on the date the auction notice is published under Rule 9(1) of the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules, not when the sale is completed.
Held: SARFAESI, as a special law with an overriding clause, prevails over the general right of redemption in section 60 of the Transfer of Property Act.
