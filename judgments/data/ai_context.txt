[JUD-SBI-LOGANATHAN] Assistant General Manager, State Bank of India v. S. Loganathan | Supreme Court | 2026-11-27 | 2026 INSC 1287 | Civil Appeal arising from SLP (C) No. 38284 of 2025
Bench: Pamidighantam Sri Narasimha and Alok Aradhe JJ.
Topic: DRT recovery certificate: property outside the tribunal's area. Category: Recovery, limitation and winding up. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Current.
Facts: A bank's case was transferred to and decided by the tribunal at Chennai, which issued a recovery certificate. The mortgaged properties were in Tiruchirappalli. The High Court held that only the tribunal at Madurai could execute the certificate.
Issue: Can the Debts Recovery Tribunal that issued a recovery certificate execute it against property outside its territorial limits?
Held: Yes. The tribunal that issued the certificate can execute it, through its Recovery Officer, against property outside its local limits.
Held: Territorial jurisdiction to decide the case (section 19(1)) is different from the reach of the Recovery Officer in execution.
Held: "May" in section 19(23) gives the tribunal a judicial discretion to send the certificate to another tribunal; it is not an option for the bank or the Recovery Officer.
Held: The Code of Civil Procedure does not restrict the tribunal's execution powers. Contrary High Court views (Delhi, Punjab and Haryana, Calcutta) are overruled.
What it means: Lenders need not start again before a second tribunal merely because the mortgaged property lies elsewhere.
What it means: Where transmission is sought, tribunals apply the test and safeguards in paragraphs 50 and 51 of the judgment.
Law involved: Recovery of Debts and Bankruptcy Act, 1993, s. 19(1), s. 19(23), Chapter V

[JUD-DALAL-UNCLAIMED] Sucheta Dalal v. Union of India | Supreme Court | 2026-11-27 | 2026 INSC 1282 | Writ Petition (Civil) No. 185 of 2022
Bench: Vikram Nath and R. Mahadevan JJ.

Status: Current.
Facts: A public interest petition raised the large sums lying unclaimed with banks and other financial institutions and transferred to statutory funds, and the difficulty heirs face in tracing them.
Issue: Should the Court direct a central mechanism for citizens to trace and recover unclaimed deposits, investments and insurance money?
Held: The Court closed the petition after noting the steps taken by the regulators and the Government during the case to help claimants find and recover unclaimed assets.
Held: It recorded the authorities' assurances and expects them to keep improving access, speed and transparency.
Held: A person with a specific grievance may approach the competent authority.
What it means: No new court-made obligation, but regulators' measures on unclaimed assets remain under watch.
What it means: Keep unclaimed-asset search tools, nominee processes and claim settlement in good order.
Law involved: Banking Regulation Act, 1949, s. 26A (Depositor Education and Awareness Fund); Companies Act, 2013, s. 125 (Investor Education and Protection Fund)

[JUD-SBI-MPID-REF] State Bank of India v. State of Maharashtra | Supreme Court | 2026-11-26 | 2026 INSC 1279 | Criminal Appeal arising from SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026
Bench: J.B. Pardiwala and Ujjal Bhuyan JJ.
Topic: Secured creditor versus attachment under a State depositor-protection law. Category: Security, charges and SARFAESI. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Under reconsideration. Referred to a larger Bench on 26-11-2026; not yet decided.
Facts: A bank held security over property that the State attached under its depositor-protection law for the benefit of defrauded depositors. The bank claimed priority as a secured creditor.
Issue: Does attachment of secured property under the Maharashtra Protection of Interest of Depositors Act prevail over a secured creditor's priority under section 26E of the SARFAESI Act?
Held: The Bench doubted the Court's earlier ruling in the NSEL case, which held that attachment under the State Act prevails over secured creditors, and referred the question to a larger Bench. The earlier ruling is not overruled; it stays in place until the larger Bench decides.
Held: As interim relief in this case only, the State must not transfer, use or disburse the sale proceeds in dispute (about ₹5.52 crore).
Held: The Court suggested that Parliament amend section 26E to explain the words "all other debts".
What it means: Priority over property attached under State depositor-protection laws is unsettled until the larger Bench rules.
What it means: Lenders facing such attachments should seek interim protection of sale proceeds and cite this reference.
Law involved: SARFAESI Act, 2002, s. 26E; Maharashtra Protection of Interest of Depositors (in Financial Establishments) Act, 1999

[JUD-UOI-SEKAR-PMLA] Union of India v. J. Sekar | Supreme Court | 2026-11-26 | 2026 INSC 1277 | Civil Appeal No. 1548 of 2024 and connected matters
Bench: Surya Kant CJI, Joymalya Bagchi and V. Mohana JJ.
Topic: PMLA attachment: who can confirm it. Category: Other laws (PMLA, cheques, evidence). Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Facts: Several High Courts had differed on whether a single non-judicial member of the Adjudicating Authority could confirm attachments made by the Enforcement Directorate.
Issue: Can a Bench of the PMLA Adjudicating Authority without a Judicial Member confirm a provisional attachment?
Held: Sections 6(5)(b) and 6(7) are valid: a Bench may have one or two members, and the Chairperson need not be the Judicial Member.
Held: But adjudication under sections 8(2) and 8(3), which confirms an attachment, must be done by a Bench that consists of or includes the Judicial Member.
Held: Connected decisions on retention of seized property and records (sections 9, 20(4), 21(4)) go to the same kind of Bench.
Held: The Court recommended that the Union consider returning to multiple Adjudicating Authorities.
What it means: A lender contesting attachment of its security can insist on a Bench that includes the Judicial Member.
What it means: Expect delay in confirmation hearings while Benches are reconstituted.
Law involved: Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002, ss. 6, 8, 9, 20, 21

[JUD-UVARC-SINHA] UV Asset Reconstruction Company Ltd. v. Gautam Sinha | Supreme Court | 2026-11-25 | 2026 INSC 1263 | Civil Appeal No. 3006 of 2020
Bench: K.V. Viswanathan and Arun Palli JJ.
Topic: Balance sheet entries and limitation. Category: Recovery, limitation and winding up. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Facts: A bank lent to a cold-storage company in 2013 and assigned the debt to an asset reconstruction company. The NCLAT dismissed the insolvency application as time-barred.
Issue: Do entries in the borrower's balance sheets extend limitation for a financial creditor's insolvency application?
Held: Yes. The Court held that the balance sheets for 2015-16 to 2017-18 acknowledged the liability while the debtor-creditor relationship continued, so the section 7 application filed in October 2018 was in time, though the account became an NPA in March 2014.
Held: The NCLAT misread the balance sheets and their notes. Its order was set aside and the case sent back only on whether there was a default.
What it means: Keep the borrower's signed financial statements for each year: they can save a claim that otherwise looks time-barred.
What it means: Read the notes to accounts; a caveat does not by itself cancel the acknowledgement.
Law involved: Limitation Act, 1963, s. 18; Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, s. 7

[JUD-JANGRA-SBI] Ravi Kumar Jangra v. Union of India | Supreme Court | 2026-11-19 | Writ Petition (Civil) No. 1415 of 2026 (order)
Bench: Surya Kant CJI, Joymalya Bagchi and V. Mohana JJ.
Topic: Homebuyer EMIs under a tripartite agreement: check the builder-bank nexus first. Category: Customer protection and consumer disputes. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Housing finance, Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Interim order. Order of 19-11-2026 disposing of the writ petition with ad-interim protection; no view on the merits.
Facts: Homebuyers in a stalled housing project in Gurugram took loans under a tripartite agreement with the bank and the builder, with disbursement linked to construction. The bank allegedly paid the builder without matching progress. The builder entered insolvency in August 2025, and the bank sued the homebuyers for recovery and obtained interim orders to pay instalments.
Issue: Can a court direct homebuyers to pay instalments on housing loans disbursed under a tripartite agreement while the project is stalled and the builder is in insolvency?
Held: Before ordering a homebuyer to pay instalments, the court must first examine the nexus between the builder and the bank, and whether the bank released the loan strictly as the tripartite agreement required.
Held: No interim direction to pay instalments can issue unless the court finds clearly that the homebuyer is in default.
Held: The Civil Court was told not to take coercive action against the petitioners or similarly placed homebuyers until it decides that issue. This is ad-interim protection only; the Court expressed no view on the merits.
What it means: Lenders with construction-linked or subvention home loans should be ready to show that each disbursement matched construction progress as the tripartite agreement required.
What it means: Recovery suits against homebuyers in stalled projects may not get interim payment orders until this is decided.
Law involved: Constitution of India, Article 32

[JUD-EDELWEISS-NCTD] Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction Company Ltd. v. State of NCT of Delhi | Supreme Court | 2026-11-18 | 2026 INSC 1236 | Criminal Appeal No. 4622 of 2026 (with Criminal Appeal No. 4623 of 2026)
Bench: Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta JJ.
Topic: A lender is not a trustee of the mortgaged property. Category: Security, charges and SARFAESI. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Current. The judgment PDF bears the date 29-09-2026; it was signed and released on 18-11-2026.
Facts: Homebuyers complained that the developer's project land was mortgaged to a lender, whose loan later passed to an asset reconstruction company, as cross-collateral. On the Magistrate's direction the police registered an FIR against the lenders for breach of trust, cheating and conspiracy.
Issue: Can homebuyers prosecute the project lender and the asset reconstruction company that took over the loan for criminal breach of trust and cheating because the mortgaged project land was used as security?
Held: No. Taking the complaint at face value, there was no entrustment of the complainants' property to the lenders, no deception and nothing showing a conspiracy, so the offences of criminal breach of trust, cheating and conspiracy were not made out. The FIR was quashed against the lender, the ARC and their officers.
Held: Breaches of RERA are the promoter's obligations, prosecutable only on a complaint by the Authority; they cannot be the foundation for prosecuting a lender, which is not the regulated entity.
Held: The Magistrate who ordered the FIR had to give reasons for disagreeing with the police report that no offence was made out. The complainants remain free to pursue civil, arbitral and statutory remedies.
What it means: Real estate lenders and ARCs have a strong answer to criminal complaints built only on their holding project land as security.
What it means: Disputes over the security interest stay in civil, RERA and insolvency forums.
Law involved: Indian Penal Code, 1860, ss. 405, 406, 409, 420, 120B; Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, s. 156(3); Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016, s. 80

[JUD-TWINSTAR-BOM] Twinstar Technologies Ltd. v. Bank of Maharashtra | Supreme Court | 2026-11-16 | 2026 INSC 1214 | Civil Appeal No. 894 of 2022 and connected appeals
Bench: Pamidighantam Sri Narasimha and Alok Aradhe JJ.
Topic: Sending a resolution plan back to the creditors' committee. Category: Insolvency: creditors, claims and distribution. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Facts: A single resolution plan was approved for thirteen companies of one group. Three dissenting lenders appealed, and the NCLAT set the approval aside.
Issue: Could the NCLAT set aside the approval of a group resolution plan at the instance of dissenting financial creditors and send it back to the committee of creditors?
Held: Yes. The Supreme Court dismissed the successful bidder's appeals and upheld the NCLAT's order remitting the plan to the committee of creditors.
Held: The Court set out when the NCLAT may remand a plan, and said a remand must come with strict timelines so that it does not cause the delay the Code seeks to avoid.
Held: The NCLAT's decision to keep the group's foreign oil and gas assets out of the parent's insolvency process was also upheld.
What it means: Dissenting financial creditors can challenge a plan that does not treat them as the Code requires.
What it means: An approved plan is not safe until appeals end; factor that into recovery timelines.
Law involved: Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, ss. 30, 31, 61

Status: Current.
Facts: Personal guarantors argued that the interim moratorium from their earlier filing still protected them.
Issue: Does the 2026 amendment to section 96, which removed the automatic interim moratorium, apply to personal insolvency cases filed before it?

[JUD-AVARSEKAR-ACRE] Kishore Avarsekar v. Assets Care and Reconstruction Enterprises Ltd. | Supreme Court | 2026-11-04 | 2026 INSC 1217 | SLP (Crl.) No. 20619 of 2026
Bench: Dipankar Datta and Sheel Nagu JJ.
Topic: Cheque-bounce cases survive assignment to an ARC. Category: Recovery, limitation and winding up. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current. Reportable order dismissing the special leave petition.
Facts: A bank had filed cheque-bounce complaints against the borrowers and then assigned the debt to an asset reconstruction company, which asked to be substituted as complainant. The borrowers objected.
Issue: When a bank assigns a loan to an asset reconstruction company, can the ARC continue the bank's pending cheque-bounce complaint?
Held: Yes. A pending section 138 complaint relating to the assigned financial asset is an "other proceeding of whatever nature" under section 5(4) of the SARFAESI Act, so it does not abate and the ARC may be substituted.
Held: But assignment alone does not make the ARC the payee or holder in due course. The ARC must prove that the dishonoured cheque itself was transferred and delivered with the asset, that there was an endorsement or assignment making it a holder in due course, that the ingredients of section 138 are met, and that it is not improving on the original complainant's case.
What it means: When assigning stressed loans, transfer and endorse the dishonoured cheques along with the debt, and record this in the assignment documents.
What it means: ARCs taking over cheque cases must be ready to prove holder-in-due-course status strictly.
Law involved: SARFAESI Act, 2002, s. 5(1), 5(4); Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, ss. 8, 9, 138

[JUD-YES-AT1] Yes Bank Ltd v. Axis Trustee Services Ltd | Supreme Court | 2026-10-15 | 2026 INSC 1132 | Civil Appeal No. 3182 of 2025 and connected appeals
Bench: Dipankar Datta and Augustine George Masih JJ.
Topic: Write-down of Additional Tier 1 bonds. Category: RBI powers and lending conduct. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Review pending. The bank and the Reserve Bank filed review petitions in November 2026, which are pending. The ruling affirms the Bombay High Court's judgment of 20 January 2023 in Writ Petition No. 785 of 2021.
Facts: A private sector bank issued Additional Tier 1 bonds of Rs 3,000 crore in 2016 and Rs 5,415 crore in 2017. On 5 March 2020 the bank was placed under moratorium and an Administrator was appointed. The draft reconstruction scheme published next day provided for the bonds to be written down, but the final scheme notified on 13 March 2020 did not. On 14 March 2020 the Administrator wrote the bonds down in full. The Bombay High Court quashed the write-down in January 2023, and the bank, the Reserve Bank and the Union appealed.
Issue: Could the Administrator write down the bank's AT-1 bonds after the reconstruction scheme had come into force without a write-down clause?
Held: No. The appeals were dismissed and the write-down stays quashed. The validity of the decision is judged as on the date it was taken; later events cannot rescue it.
Held: Under the Basel III Master Circular, a write-down at the point of non-viability has to happen before the bank is reconstituted, that is, before the scheme takes effect. Once the final scheme was in force without a write-down clause, the bonds could not be written down contrary to it.
Held: At the point of non-viability it is the Reserve Bank that decides whether instruments are written down, not the bank or its Administrator. The Administrator only stands in place of the board.
Held: The annual coupon remains payable in terms of the information memoranda.
Held: The Court was critical of the sale of these high-risk instruments to individual investors, which the Master Circular did not permit.
What it means: Loss-absorption terms in regulatory capital instruments will be enforced, but only if the regulator and the issuer follow the sequence and the decision-maker laid down in the Reserve Bank's capital framework and in the scheme itself.
What it means: Issuers and distributors of AT-1 and similar instruments (banks, and NBFCs issuing perpetual debt) should keep to the permitted investor classes and give clear risk disclosure. Mis-selling to retail investors was a central concern.
Law involved: Banking Regulation Act, 1949, ss. 36ACA, 45; Yes Bank Ltd Reconstruction Scheme, 2020; RBI Master Circular - Basel III Capital Regulations, Annex 16

[JUD-SHIKHA-AXIS] Shikha Ghosh v. Axis Bank Ltd | Delhi High Court | 2026-09-30 | FAO(COMM) No. 91 of 2025
Bench: Prathiba M. Singh and Dinesh Bhatt JJ.
Topic: Bank's mass use of one arbitrator. Category: Arbitration. Forum: High Courts. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Current. The court kept the matter part-heard for affidavits from the bank and RBI, so further orders may follow.
Facts: A borrower challenged an award passed by a sole arbitrator whom the bank had appointed alone under the loan agreement. It emerged that the same arbitrator had been given thousands of the bank's recovery cases.
Issue: Is an award by an arbitrator appointed unilaterally by the lender enforceable, and what should regulators do about the practice?
Held: The bank conceded that awards by arbitrators it had appointed alone are void, following Perkins Eastman and CORE (2025). The award was declared unenforceable against the borrower, and the bank was left free to take fresh recovery steps in accordance with law.
Held: The same arbitrator had handled 4,155 of the bank's cases. The bank was directed to explain this on affidavit and to say how it would deal with such awards, and RBI was asked to caution banks and consider guidance.
What it means: Awards from arbitrators appointed by the lender alone, or used in bulk by one lender, are open to challenge as void, and the court has drawn RBI into the issue. Under section 12(5), an ineligible arbitrator can act only if the parties waive the bar by express written agreement after the dispute has arisen.
Law involved: Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, ss. 12(5), 34, 37

[JUD-BHADRA] Bhadra International (India) Pvt Ltd v. Airports Authority of India | Supreme Court | 2026-01-05 | 2026 INSC 6, [2026] 1 SCR 30 | Civil Appeal Nos. 37-38 of 2026
Bench: J.B. Pardiwala and K.V. Viswanathan JJ.
Topic: Unilaterally appointed sole arbitrators. Category: Arbitration. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance, Stock broking.
Status: Current. Applies the Constitution Bench ruling in Central Organisation for Railway Electrification (2024).
Issue: Is an award by a sole arbitrator appointed by one party alone valid, and can the other party lose its objection by taking part in the arbitration?
Held: The unilateral appointment of a sole arbitrator is void from the start, and the arbitrator is ineligible under section 12(5) read with the Seventh Schedule. Where the arbitration agreement itself breaches section 12(5), no appointment made under it is valid.
Held: Ineligibility can be waived only by an express agreement in writing after disputes arise. It cannot be waived by implication or conduct.
Held: An arbitrator who is ineligible lacks inherent jurisdiction, so the objection can be raised at any stage, including for the first time in a challenge to the award under section 34.
What it means: Recovery awards obtained through a sole arbitrator named by the lender alone can be set aside even if the borrower took part without objecting. Lenders should obtain a clear written waiver after the dispute arises, or have the arbitrator appointed jointly, by an institution or by the court.
What it means: Lenders and brokers should review loan agreements, client agreements and pending arbitrations for unilateral appointment clauses.
Law involved: Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, ss. 12(5), 14, 18, 34 and Seventh Schedule

[JUD-TARI] Sanjabij Tari v. Kishore S. Borcar | Supreme Court | 2025-09-25 | 2025 INSC 1158, [2025] 9 SCR 1515 | Criminal Appeal No. 1755 of 2010
Bench: Manmohan and N.V. Anjaria JJ.
Topic: Cheque bounce: presumptions and trial directions. Category: Other laws (PMLA, cheques, evidence). Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Issue: How should trial courts apply the statutory presumptions in cheque-dishonour cases, and how should the backlog be handled?
Held: Courts must give effect to the presumptions in sections 118 and 139 of the NI Act. Treating a section 138 case as a civil recovery suit and asking the complainant to prove the underlying debt is contrary to what Parliament intended.
Held: The Court issued directions for all section 138 cases: summons may also be served by the complainant ('dasti') and electronically, with an affidavit of service; district courts are to set up online payment by QR code or UPI so that the accused can pay and the case be compounded or closed; every complaint must carry a synopsis in a set format; and no pre-cognizance summons under section 223 of the BNSS is needed.
Held: Trial courts must record reasons before converting a summary trial into a summons trial.
What it means: Lenders filing section 138 complaints should use the synopsis format and the faster service routes, and can expect earlier settlement through online payment.
What it means: The burden is on the drawer to rebut the presumption that the cheque was for a debt or liability.
Law involved: Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, ss. 118, 138, 139, 143, 147; Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, s. 223

[JUD-PNBHF] PNB Housing Finance Ltd v. Manoj Saha | Supreme Court | 2025-07-15 | 2025 INSC 847, [2025] 7 SCR 1041 | Civil Appeal No. 9492 of 2025
Bench: P.S. Narasimha and Joymalya Bagchi JJ.
Topic: SARFAESI: tenants claiming occupation. Category: Security, charges and SARFAESI. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Housing finance, Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Issue: Can a person claiming an old, unregistered tenancy get back possession of a mortgaged property that a housing finance company has taken under SARFAESI?
Held: A person claiming tenancy through an oral or unregistered agreement must prove it with independent evidence such as rent receipts, tax receipts or electricity bills for the period before the demand notice under section 13(2).

Held: A recital in the sale deed or a letter of attornment from the borrower is not enough. The High Court's order restoring possession was set aside.
What it means: Lenders can resist tenancy claims set up to block possession of mortgaged homes, by insisting on proof of occupation before the demand notice.
What it means: At loan origination, HFCs should record who occupies the property and on what terms.

[JUD-DHFL] Piramal Capital and Housing Finance Ltd v. 63 Moons Technologies Ltd | Supreme Court | 2025-04-01 | 2025 INSC 421, [2025] 4 SCR 344 | Civil Appeal Nos. 1632-1634 of 2022
Bench: Bela M. Trivedi and Satish Chandra Sharma JJ.
Topic: Resolution of a housing finance company under the IBC. Category: Insolvency: starting and running the process. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Housing finance, Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current. From 26 May 2026 the substituted section 26 expressly provides that avoidance and fraudulent or wrongful trading applications do not affect the insolvency process and continue after it ends.
Facts: The Reserve Bank superseded the board of a large housing finance company and, as the first use of the special insolvency route for financial service providers, started its corporate insolvency. The approved resolution plan gave the benefit of any recoveries from fraudulent trading applications (section 66) to the resolution applicant, valuing them at a notional Re 1. A debenture holder objected, and the NCLAT sent that term back to the creditors. Fixed deposit holders demanded full repayment, and former promoters and directors sought to take part in the process.
Issue: Could the appellate tribunal reopen the plan's treatment of section 66 recoveries, are deposit holders entitled to full repayment, and can directors of a superseded board take part in the committee of creditors?
Held: The plan was restored. How recoveries are dealt with is a commercial decision of the committee of creditors, and the NCLAT could not substitute its own view.
Held: Avoidance applications (preferential, undervalued and extortionate transactions) are different from fraudulent and wrongful trading applications under section 66. The plan could assign section 66 recoveries to the resolution applicant, while the NCLT still decides those applications.
Held: A vote cast by the authorised representative for a class of creditors binds every member of that class, including those who voted against.
Held: Fixed deposit holders cannot claim full repayment by relying on section 45QA of the RBI Act or section 36A of the National Housing Bank Act. Once the plan is approved, they are bound by it.
Held: Directors of a board superseded by the Reserve Bank under section 45-IE are not 'suspended directors'. They cannot attend committee meetings or demand copies of the plan.
What it means: For lenders and bidders, the ruling confirms that the special route for NBFCs and HFCs works like an ordinary insolvency once it starts, with the Reserve Bank's administrator in charge and the old board out.
What it means: Depositors and debenture holders of a failed NBFC or HFC are creditors under the plan and take what the plan gives their class.
Law involved: Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, ss. 25A, 30, 31, 43-51, 66, 227; Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, ss. 45-IE, 45QA; National Housing Bank Act, 1987, s. 36A

[JUD-MAHAVEER] Mahaveer Sharma v. Exide Life Insurance Co. Ltd | Supreme Court | 2025-02-25 | 2025 INSC 268, [2025] 2 SCR 1311 | Civil Appeal No. 3562 of 2025
Bench: B.V. Nagarathna and S.C. Sharma JJ.
Topic: Substantial disclosure is enough. Category: Insurance claims. Forum: Supreme Court. Sectors: Life insurance.
Status: Current.
Issue: Can a life insurer reject a claim because the insured disclosed one large existing policy but not some smaller ones?
Held: No. The insured had disclosed a Rs 40 lakh policy, much larger than the undisclosed policies and the policy in question. That was a substantial disclosure.
Held: The undisclosed policies were of inconsequential amounts, and their omission was not a material suppression. The repudiation was set aside.
