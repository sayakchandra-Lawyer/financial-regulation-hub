[JUD-BHADRA] Bhadra International (India) Pvt Ltd v. Airports Authority of India | Supreme Court | 2026-01-05 | 2026 INSC 6, [2026] 1 SCR 30 | Civil Appeal Nos. 37-38 of 2026
Bench: J.B. Pardiwala and K.V. Viswanathan JJ.
Topic: Unilaterally appointed sole arbitrators. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance, Stock broking.
Status: Current. Applies the Constitution Bench ruling in Central Organisation for Railway Electrification (2024).
Issue: Is an award by a sole arbitrator appointed by one party alone valid, and can the other party lose its objection by taking part in the arbitration?
Held: The unilateral appointment of a sole arbitrator is void from the start, and the arbitrator is ineligible under section 12(5) read with the Seventh Schedule. Where the arbitration agreement itself breaches section 12(5), no appointment made under it is valid.
Held: Ineligibility can be waived only by an express agreement in writing after disputes arise. It cannot be waived by implication or conduct.
Held: An arbitrator who is ineligible lacks inherent jurisdiction, so the objection can be raised at any stage, including for the first time in a challenge to the award under section 34.
What it means: Recovery awards obtained through a sole arbitrator named by the lender alone can be set aside even if the borrower took part without objecting. Lenders should obtain a clear written waiver after the dispute arises, or have the arbitrator appointed jointly, by an institution or by the court.
What it means: Lenders and brokers should review loan agreements, client agreements and pending arbitrations for unilateral appointment clauses.
Law involved: Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, ss. 12(5), 14, 18, 34 and Seventh Schedule

[JUD-TARI] Sanjabij Tari v. Kishore S. Borcar | Supreme Court | 2025-09-25 | 2025 INSC 1158, [2025] 9 SCR 1515 | Criminal Appeal No. 1755 of 2010
Bench: Manmohan and N.V. Anjaria JJ.
Topic: Cheque bounce: presumptions and trial directions. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Issue: How should trial courts apply the statutory presumptions in cheque-dishonour cases, and how should the backlog be handled?
Held: Courts must give effect to the presumptions in sections 118 and 139 of the NI Act. Treating a section 138 case as a civil recovery suit and asking the complainant to prove the underlying debt is contrary to what Parliament intended.
Held: The Court issued directions for all section 138 cases: summons may also be served by the complainant ('dasti') and electronically, with an affidavit of service; district courts are to set up online payment by QR code or UPI so that the accused can pay and the case be compounded or closed; every complaint must carry a synopsis in a set format; and no pre-cognizance summons under section 223 of the BNSS is needed.
Held: Trial courts must record reasons before converting a summary trial into a summons trial.
What it means: Lenders filing section 138 complaints should use the synopsis format and the faster service routes, and can expect earlier settlement through online payment.
What it means: The burden is on the drawer to rebut the presumption that the cheque was for a debt or liability.
Law involved: Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, ss. 118, 138, 139, 143, 147; Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, s. 223

[JUD-PNBHF] PNB Housing Finance Ltd v. Manoj Saha | Supreme Court | 2025-07-15 | 2025 INSC 847, [2025] 7 SCR 1041 | Civil Appeal No. 9492 of 2025
Bench: P.S. Narasimha and Joymalya Bagchi JJ.
Topic: SARFAESI: tenants claiming occupation. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Housing finance, Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Issue: Can a person claiming an old, unregistered tenancy get back possession of a mortgaged property that a housing finance company has taken under SARFAESI?
Held: A person claiming tenancy through an oral or unregistered agreement must prove it with independent evidence such as rent receipts, tax receipts or electricity bills for the period before the demand notice under section 13(2).
Held: Even if proved, such a tenancy does not continue beyond one year from the section 13(2) notice; after that the occupant is a tenant at sufferance.
Held: A recital in the sale deed or a letter of attornment from the borrower is not enough. The High Court's order restoring possession was set aside.
What it means: Lenders can resist tenancy claims set up to block possession of mortgaged homes, by insisting on proof of occupation before the demand notice.
What it means: At loan origination, HFCs should record who occupies the property and on what terms.
Law involved: SARFAESI Act, 2002, ss. 13(2), 13(4), 17(4A); Transfer of Property Act, 1882; West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1997

[JUD-MAHAVEER] Mahaveer Sharma v. Exide Life Insurance Co. Ltd | Supreme Court | 2025-02-25 | 2025 INSC 268, [2025] 2 SCR 1311 | Civil Appeal No. 3562 of 2025
Bench: B.V. Nagarathna and S.C. Sharma JJ.
Topic: Substantial disclosure is enough. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Life insurance.
Status: Current.
Issue: Can a life insurer reject a claim because the insured disclosed one large existing policy but not some smaller ones?
Held: No. The insured had disclosed a Rs 40 lakh policy, much larger than the undisclosed policies and the policy in question. That was a substantial disclosure.
Held: The undisclosed policies were of inconsequential amounts, and their omission was not a material suppression. The repudiation was set aside.
What it means: Not every omission is material. Insurers should ask whether the missing fact would have changed a prudent insurer's decision before repudiating.
Law involved: Insurance Act, 1938, s. 45; Consumer Protection Act, 1986

[JUD-CHOKSHI] AC Chokshi Share Broker Pvt Ltd v. Jatin Pratap Desai | Supreme Court | 2025-02-10 | 2025 INSC 174, [2025] 2 SCR 1545 | Civil Appeal No. 2227 of 2025
Bench: P.S. Narasimha and Sandeep Mehta JJ.
Topic: Broker-client arbitration and family accounts. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Stock broking.
Status: Current.
Issue: Can a broker invoke exchange arbitration against a husband for his wife's trading debt, based on an oral arrangement?
Held: Yes. Courts must read contracts practically, looking at the parties' conduct and intention. The husband's oral instructions and the joint way the two accounts were operated showed joint and several liability.
Held: Separate client registration agreements, client codes and accounts did not change this. Arbitration under the exchange bye-laws was maintainable against the husband.
What it means: Brokers who act on a client's oral instructions to move credit balances to a family member's account may be able to recover from both. This does not relax SEBI's requirements on evidence of client orders, which still apply.
Law involved: Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, ss. 7, 34, 37; BSE Bye-laws, 1957

[JUD-HSBC-AWAZ] Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corp. Ltd v. Awaz | Supreme Court | 2024-12-20 | 2024 INSC 1044, [2024] 12 SCR 1482 | Civil Appeal No. 5273 of 2008
Bench: Bela M. Trivedi and Satish Chandra Sharma JJ.
Topic: Credit card interest rates. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Issue: Could the National Consumer Commission cap credit card interest at 30% a year as an unfair trade practice?
Held: The National Commission's ruling that interest above 30% a year is usurious was set aside. It was contrary to section 21A of the Banking Regulation Act, which bars courts and tribunals from reopening a bank transaction on the ground that the interest is excessive, and it encroached on RBI's domain.
Held: Directions and guidelines issued by RBI are an extension of the statute. Interest charged as RBI's circulars allow is not an unfair trade practice in itself.
Held: The complaint was not maintainable: the trusts that filed it were not 'persons' or consumers, and no permission to sue in a representative capacity had been obtained.
What it means: Interest on credit cards and loans is set by the lender's Board-approved policy within RBI's framework, not by consumer forums. Transparency rules (KFS, APR and disclosure of charges) still apply.
What it means: The bar in section 21A is written for banking companies, so NBFCs should rely on the RBI framework and fair-dealing rules rather than on section 21A itself.
Law involved: Banking Regulation Act, 1949, s. 21A; Consumer Protection Act, 1986

[JUD-CORE] Central Organisation for Railway Electrification v. ECI SPIC SMO MCML (JV) | Supreme Court (Constitution Bench) | 2024-11-08 | 2024 INSC 857, [2024] 11 SCR 2173 | Civil Appeal Nos. 9486-9487 of 2019
Bench: Five Judges, D.Y. Chandrachud CJI presiding.
Topic: Unilateral appointment of arbitrators. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Current. Prospective for appointments after 8 November 2024, as stated for three-member tribunals.
Issue: Is a clause valid if it lets one party appoint a sole arbitrator, or makes the other side choose from a panel curated by that party?
Held: Equal treatment of parties applies at every stage of arbitration, including the appointment of arbitrators.
Held: A clause that lets one party unilaterally appoint a sole arbitrator gives rise to justifiable doubts about the arbitrator's independence and impartiality, and hinders the other party's equal participation. For a three-member tribunal, forcing the other party to pick its arbitrator from a panel curated by one side breaches equal treatment.
Held: A party can still expressly waive the objection in writing after disputes arise (proviso to section 12(5)). The Court said the law it laid down applies prospectively to appointments made after 8 November 2024, and that this direction applies to three-member tribunals.
What it means: Loan agreements that let the lender alone name the arbitrator are unsafe. Lenders should agree on the arbitrator with the borrower, use an arbitral institution, or apply to the court under section 11.
What it means: Awards by an arbitrator the lender appointed alone, without an express written waiver by the borrower after the dispute arose, are at risk of being set aside. Bhadra International (2026) confirms that such a sole arbitrator is ineligible.
Law involved: Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, ss. 12(5), 18

[JUD-SUJATHA] Mahakali Sujatha v. Branch Manager, Future Generali India Life Insurance Co. Ltd | Supreme Court | 2024-04-10 | 2024 INSC 296, [2024] 4 SCR 724 | Civil Appeal No. 3821 of 2024
Bench: B.V. Nagarathna and Augustine George Masih JJ.
Topic: Insurer's burden to prove suppression. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Life insurance.
Status: Current.
Issue: Who must prove that the insured suppressed other policies, and with what evidence?
Held: Where the insurer repudiates on the ground of suppression, the burden is on the insurer to prove that the fact suppressed was material and that the insured knew of it.
Held: The insurer had produced no documents to show the other policies. The repudiation could not stand.
What it means: Claims teams must keep documentary proof, such as other insurers' records, before repudiating for non-disclosure. Allegations alone will fail.
Law involved: Insurance Act, 1938, s. 45 (before the 2015 amendment); Evidence Act, 1872

[JUD-YEZDANI] Shakti Yezdani v. Jayanand Jayant Salgaonkar | Supreme Court | 2023-12-14 | 2023 INSC 1076, [2023] 16 SCR 695 | Civil Appeal No. 7107 of 2017
Bench: Hrishikesh Roy and Pankaj Mithal JJ.
Topic: Nominee versus legal heirs. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Stock broking, Mutual funds.
Status: Current.
Issue: Does a nominee of shares or securities become the owner on the holder's death, to the exclusion of the legal heirs?
Held: No. The nominee does not get absolute title. Vesting in the nominee under section 109A of the Companies Act, 1956 (section 72 of the 2013 Act) and Bye-law 9.11.1 under the Depositories Act is for a limited purpose.
Held: Nomination does not change the law of succession. The legal heirs, or the beneficiaries under a will, remain entitled.
What it means: Depository participants, brokers and registrars can transmit securities to the nominee to complete formalities, but the nominee holds them subject to the claims of the legal heirs.
What it means: Client communication should say clearly that a nomination is not a will.
Law involved: Companies Act, 2013, s. 72; Companies Act, 1956, ss. 109A, 109B; Depositories Act, 1996 and depository bye-laws

[JUD-INTERPLAY] In re: Interplay between Arbitration Agreements under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 and the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 | Supreme Court | 2023-12-13 | 2023 INSC 1066, [2023] 15 SCR 1081 | Curative Petition (C) No. 44 of 2023 in Review Petition (C) No. 704 of 2021 in Civil Appeal No. 1599 of 2020
Bench: D.Y. Chandrachud CJI, Sanjay Kishan Kaul, Sanjiv Khanna, B.R. Gavai, Surya Kant, J.B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra JJ.
Topic: Unstamped agreements and arbitration. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Current.
Issue: Is an arbitration agreement in an unstamped or insufficiently stamped contract void, and must the court deal with stamping before appointing an arbitrator?
Held: An unstamped or insufficiently stamped agreement is inadmissible in evidence under section 35 of the Stamp Act, but it is not void, void from the start or unenforceable. Non-stamping is a curable defect, and the Stamp Act itself sets out how it is cured.
Held: Admissibility and validity are different questions: a valid agreement can be inadmissible until the duty and penalty are paid.
Held: When deciding applications under sections 8 or 11 of the Arbitration Act, the court only examines whether an arbitration agreement prima facie exists. Objections on stamping are for the arbitral tribunal. The five-judge ruling in N.N. Global (2023) was overruled on this point.
What it means: A lender's loan agreement or guarantee that was not properly stamped can still be enforced once the deficit duty and penalty are paid, but it cannot be relied on in evidence until then. Stamping at execution remains the safe course, because the penalty for late stamping can be many times the duty.
What it means: Borrowers cannot block the appointment of an arbitrator by objecting to stamping; the tribunal will deal with it.
Law involved: Indian Stamp Act, 1899, ss. 33, 35, 42; Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, ss. 5, 8, 11, 16

[JUD-JIWRAJKA] Dilip B. Jiwrajka v. Union of India | Supreme Court | 2023-11-09 | 2023 INSC 1018, [2023] 16 SCR 562 | Writ Petition (Civil) No. 1281 of 2021
Bench: D.Y. Chandrachud CJI, J.B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra JJ.
Topic: Insolvency of personal guarantors. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current. From 26-05-2026, s. 96(4), inserted by the IBC (Amendment) Act, 2026, provides that the interim moratorium does not apply to personal guarantors to corporate debtors. The rest of the ruling is unaffected.
Issue: Are sections 95 to 100 of the IBC, on insolvency of individuals such as personal guarantors, constitutional?
Held: Sections 95 to 100 are valid and do not violate Articles 14 and 21.
Held: The resolution professional only facilitates and recommends. No adjudication happens until the Adjudicating Authority decides under section 100, and natural justice must be observed at that stage.
Held: No hearing is needed before the resolution professional is appointed under section 97(5). The debtor can take part when the resolution professional examines the application under section 99. The interim moratorium under section 96 stays legal proceedings in respect of any debt.
What it means: Lenders can file insolvency against personal guarantors of corporate borrowers without a prior hearing on 'jurisdictional facts'. The guarantor is heard in the resolution professional's examination and before the Adjudicating Authority decides.
Law involved: Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, ss. 95-100

[JUD-CELIR] Celir LLP v. Bafna Motors (Mumbai) Pvt Ltd | Supreme Court | 2023-09-21 | 2023 INSC 838, [2023] 13 SCR 53 | Civil Appeal Nos. 5542-5543 of 2023
Bench: D.Y. Chandrachud CJI and J.B. Pardiwala J.
Topic: SARFAESI: right of redemption. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Current.
Issue: Until when can a borrower redeem a mortgaged property that a secured creditor is selling under SARFAESI?
Held: Under section 13(8) of SARFAESI as amended in 2016, the borrower's right to redeem ends on the date the auction notice is published under Rule 9(1) of the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules, not when the sale is completed.
Held: SARFAESI, as a special law with an overriding clause, prevails over the general right of redemption in section 60 of the Transfer of Property Act.
Held: Once the sale is confirmed, the lender cannot withhold the sale certificate and make a private settlement with the borrower. High Courts should not use writ jurisdiction to undo an auction when the remedy under section 17 is available.
What it means: Borrowers must pay in full before the auction notice is published. After that, lenders should complete the sale and should not accept late settlements that defeat the auction purchaser.
What it means: Auction purchasers get firmer title, which supports bids at SARFAESI auctions.
Law involved: SARFAESI Act, 2002, ss. 13(8), 17; Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules, 2002, r. 9; Transfer of Property Act, 1882, s. 60

[JUD-SIB-NAVEEN] South Indian Bank Ltd v. Naveen Mathew Philip | Supreme Court | 2023-04-17 | 2023 INSC 379, [2023] 4 SCR 18 | Civil Appeal Nos. 2861-2862 of 2023
Bench: Sanjiv Khanna and M.M. Sundresh JJ.
Topic: SARFAESI: writs against lenders. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Current.
Issue: Should High Courts entertain writ petitions against SARFAESI notices when the Debts Recovery Tribunal is available?
Held: The power under Article 226 is wide but should be used only in extraordinary cases. Where the legislature has provided a specific remedy, as SARFAESI does through the Debts Recovery Tribunal, the writ remedy should not be used.
Held: A writ of mandamus in a commercial matter against a private party needs a clear legal right, and more caution is needed when the respondent is a private lender.
What it means: Borrowers challenging SARFAESI measures will normally be sent to the DRT under section 17. This reduces stays obtained through writ petitions.
Law involved: SARFAESI Act, 2002, ss. 13(4), 17; Constitution of India, Art. 226

[JUD-SBI-RAJESH] State Bank of India v. Rajesh Agarwal | Supreme Court | 2023-03-27 | 2023 INSC 303, [2023] 7 SCR 476 | Civil Appeal No. 7300 of 2022
Bench: D.Y. Chandrachud CJI and Hima Kohli J.
Topic: Fraud classification. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Current. Clarified in State Bank of India v. Amit Iron Pvt Ltd (2026 INSC 323): no personal hearing is needed, and the steps it requires are part of RBI's Master Directions of 2024 on fraud.
Issue: Must a lender hear the borrower before classifying its account as fraud under RBI's Master Directions on Frauds?
Held: Classifying an account as fraud has civil consequences for the borrower: the penal measures that apply to wilful defaulters, no restructuring and, in effect, a credit freeze that the Court described as close to 'civil death'.
Held: The Master Directions do not expressly exclude a hearing, so the rule of audi alteram partem is read into them. The borrower must be given notice, a chance to explain the forensic audit findings and to make a representation before the account is classified as fraud.
Held: The decision classifying the account as fraud must be made by a reasoned order.
What it means: Every fraud classification needs a show-cause notice with the material relied on, a real chance to reply in writing, and a reasoned order. Classifications made without these are open to challenge in writ proceedings.
What it means: In State Bank of India v. Amit Iron Pvt Ltd (7 April 2026, 2026 INSC 323) the Supreme Court held that the borrower has no right to a personal hearing, and that forensic audit reports the lender relies on should be furnished to the borrower. A lender that wants to withhold a report to protect third parties must first show that disclosure would affect their rights.
What it means: RBI's Fraud Risk Management Directions for NBFCs now build these steps into the process.
Law involved: RBI Act, 1934; Banking Regulation Act, 1949; RBI Master Directions on Frauds, 2016

[JUD-GIRNAR] Kotak Mahindra Bank Ltd v. Girnar Corrugators Pvt Ltd | Supreme Court | 2023-01-05 | 2023 INSC 12, [2023] 1 SCR 873 | Civil Appeal No. 6662 of 2022
Bench: M.R. Shah and Krishna Murari JJ.
Topic: SARFAESI priority over MSME dues. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
Status: Current.
Issue: Does recovery under the MSMED Act prevail over a secured creditor's enforcement under SARFAESI?
Held: The MSMED Act provides a forum for buyer-seller disputes but gives no priority over secured creditors. Section 26E of SARFAESI gives a secured creditor with a registered security interest priority over other debts and Government dues, subject to the IBC.
Held: Recoveries under SARFAESI from secured assets prevail over recoveries under the MSMED Act; there is no repugnancy between the two Acts.
Held: The Magistrate under section 14 only assists the secured creditor to take possession and cannot adjudicate; challenges go to the DRT under section 17.
What it means: Registering the security interest with CERSAI matters: section 26E priority depends on registration.
Law involved: SARFAESI Act, 2002, ss. 14, 17, 26E; Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises Development Act, 2006, ss. 15-24

[JUD-FT-2022] Franklin Templeton Trustee Services Pvt Ltd v. Amruta Garg (August 2022) | Supreme Court | 2022-08-12 | 2022 INSC 829, [2022] 6 SCR 880 | I.A. No. 53453 of 2022 in Civil Appeal Nos. 498-501 of 2021
Bench: S. Abdul Nazeer and Sanjiv Khanna JJ.
Topic: Distributor commission on winding up. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Mutual funds.
Status: Current. Decided under the 1996 Regulations, which the SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 2026 replaced from 1 April 2026. The 2026 Regulations follow this ruling.
Issue: Are mutual fund distributors entitled to trail commission from a scheme that is being wound up?
Held: Regulation 52, which allows recurring expenses including distributor commission, applies only while a scheme is operating, not after the notice of winding up is published.
Held: Distributor commission is not an expense connected with winding up. Future trail commission is not an amount 'due and payable' at the time of winding up.
What it means: Once a winding-up notice is published, distributors stop earning trail commission from that scheme. The SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 2026 now say expressly that distribution commission is not a winding-up cost (regulation 38(1)(d), Explanation).
Law involved: SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 1996, regs. 39-41, 52; SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 2026, reg. 38

[JUD-PTCIFS] PTC India Financial Services Ltd v. Venkateswarlu Kari | Supreme Court | 2022-05-12 | 2022 INSC 562, [2022] 9 SCR 1063 | Civil Appeal No. 5443 of 2019
Bench: Sanjiv Khanna and M.R. Shah JJ.
Topic: Pledge of demat shares. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Issue: When a lender invokes a pledge of dematerialised shares and is recorded as beneficial owner, has it 'sold' them and must it credit their value against the debt?
Held: The Depositories Act and its regulations add to, and do not displace, the pledge provisions of the Contract Act.
Held: Recording the pledgee as beneficial owner on invocation is not a sale; it only enables the pledgee to sell. The pledge is not discharged, and the pledgee need not account for the shares until an actual sale.
Held: The notice under section 176 before sale is mandatory and parties cannot contract out of it. The pledgor may redeem under section 177 until the actual sale. A pledgee cannot sell to itself.
What it means: A lender invoking a share pledge must still give reasonable notice and sell to a third party, and should credit the borrower only with the actual sale proceeds. Until then the borrower can redeem.
Law involved: Indian Contract Act, 1872, ss. 172, 176, 177; Depositories Act, 1996; SEBI (Depositories and Participants) Regulations, 1996, reg. 58

[JUD-PUNNEN] Jacob Punnen v. United India Insurance Co. Ltd | Supreme Court | 2021-12-09 | 2021 INSC 853, [2021] 9 SCR 787 | Civil Appeal No. 6778 of 2013
Bench: K.M. Joseph and S. Ravindra Bhat JJ.
Topic: Changed terms on renewal. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Health insurance.
Status: Current. IRDAI's current product and policyholder rules also govern disclosure of changes at renewal.
Issue: Can a health insurer rely on a new cap on cover that it introduced at renewal without telling the policyholder?
Held: The policyholders were led to believe they were renewing the same policy, while the insurer had introduced caps on certain surgical procedures. The insurer had a duty to tell them about the new limits.

[JUD-DENA] Dena Bank (now Bank of Baroda) v. C. Shivakumar Reddy | Supreme Court | 2021-08-04 | 2021 INSC 380, [2021] 8 SCR 1061 | Civil Appeal No. 1650 of 2020
Bench: Indira Banerjee and V. Ramasubramanian JJ.
Topic: Balance sheet entries and limitation. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks).
Status: Current.
Issue: When does limitation run for a lender's insolvency application, and do acknowledgements and recovery decrees restart it?
Held: An application under section 7 or 9 of the IBC is governed by Article 137 of the Limitation Act: three years from the date of default.

Issue: Can a health insurer treat mental illness differently from physical illness?
Held: Section 21(4) of the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 requires every insurer to provide medical insurance for treatment of mental illness on the same basis as physical illness. Insurers are bound by the Act regardless of any circular.

[JUD-SSIMA] Small Scale Industrial Manufactures Association v. Union of India | Supreme Court | 2021-03-23 | 2021 INSC 203, [2021] 15 SCR 1 | Writ Petition (Civil) No. 476 of 2020
Bench: Ashok Bhushan, R. Subhash Reddy and M.R. Shah JJ.
Topic: COVID-19 moratorium and interest. Category: . Forum: . Sectors: Lending (NBFCs and banks), Housing finance.
