SHARED STATUTORY PENALTY TEXTS:
P1: DPDP Act, 2023: if the Data Protection Board finds, on concluding an inquiry, that a breach of the Act or the Rules is significant, it may impose a monetary penalty under s. 33 and the Schedule: up to Rs 250 crore for failing to take reasonable security safeguards (s. 8(5)); up to Rs 200 crore for failing to notify a personal data breach (s. 8(6)) or for breaching the obligations on children's data (s. 9); up to Rs 150 crore for breaching the additional obligations of a Significant Data Fiduciary (s. 10); up to Rs 10,000 for a Data Principal who breaches her duties (s. 15); and up to Rs 50 crore for any other breach. The Board weighs the nature, gravity and duration of the breach, the data affected, repetition, any gain made or loss avoided, mitigation, proportionality and the likely impact of the penalty (s. 33(2)). It may accept a voluntary undertaking, which bars proceedings under the Act on its contents unless the undertaking is breached (s. 32(4) and (5)). An appeal lies to the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal within 60 days (s. 29). Where penalties have been imposed on a Data Fiduciary in two or more instances, the Central Government may, on the Board's reference, order public access to its service to be blocked (s. 37). These provisions apply from 13-05-2027.
P2: RBI Act, 1934: RBI may impose penalties on an NBFC that fails to comply with its directions (s. 58G read with s. 58B), and may cancel its certificate of registration (s. 45-IA(6)). The DPDP Act applies alongside these directions (s. 38).
P3: Credit Information Companies (Regulation) Act, 2005: fines imposed by a court under ss. 22 and 23 (up to Rs 1 crore for wilful breach of the privacy principles or wilfully false credit information; otherwise up to Rs 1 lakh, plus Rs 5,000 a day while it continues), and penalties imposed by RBI under s. 25. The DPDP Act applies alongside it (s. 38).
P4: Information Technology Act, 2000: compensation under s. 43A until it is omitted, and the offence under s. 70B(7).
P5: Information Technology Act, 2000, s. 70B(7): failure to provide information or comply with a CERT-In direction is punishable with imprisonment up to one year, or fine up to Rs 1 crore, or both.
P6: Securities and Exchange Board of India Act, 1992: SEBI may issue directions and impose penalties for failure to comply with its circulars. The DPDP Act applies alongside (s. 38).
P7: Insurance Act, 1938: IRDAI may impose penalties and give directions for failure to comply with its regulations. The DPDP Act applies alongside (s. 38).
P8: Insurance Act, 1938: IRDAI may impose penalties and give directions for failure to comply with its guidelines. The DPDP Act applies alongside (s. 38).

GLOSSARY (plain-language meanings, not statutory text):
DPDP Act (Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023): India's general law on the processing of digital personal data. It applies to banks, NBFCs, insurers and market intermediaries like any other business, alongside their sector rules.
DPDP Rules (Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025): The rules made under the Act on notice, Consent Managers, security, breach reporting, retention, children's data, Significant Data Fiduciaries and the Board, notified on 13 November 2025.
Personal data: Any data about an individual who can be identified by or in relation to it. The Act covers it when it is in digital form or digitised later.
Data Fiduciary: The person who decides, alone or with others, why and how personal data is processed. A lender, insurer or broker is the Data Fiduciary for its customers' data.
Data Processor: A person that processes personal data on behalf of a Data Fiduciary, such as a cloud provider, call centre or lending service provider. It needs a valid contract with the Data Fiduciary, which stays responsible.
Data Principal: The individual the data is about. For a child it includes the parent, and for a person with disability it includes the lawful guardian.
Consent: Permission that is free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, given by a clear affirmative action, for a specified purpose, after a notice. It can be withdrawn as easily as it was given.
Notice: The statement a Data Fiduciary must give with or before a request for consent, listing the data, the purpose and how to withdraw consent, exercise rights and complain to the Board.
Legitimate uses (Certain legitimate uses): Cases in s. 7 where data may be processed without consent, such as data the person gave voluntarily for a purpose, legal duties to disclose to the State, court orders, emergencies and employment.
Consent Manager: A company registered with the Data Protection Board that runs a platform through which people give, manage, review and withdraw consent across Data Fiduciaries. Registration opened on 13 November 2026.
Personal data breach: Any unauthorised processing, or accidental disclosure, acquisition, sharing, use, alteration, destruction or loss of access to personal data, that compromises its confidentiality, integrity or availability.
72-hour report: The detailed report on a personal data breach that a Data Fiduciary must send to the Board within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, after a first intimation without delay.
Significant Data Fiduciary (SDF): A Data Fiduciary, or class of them, that the Central Government notifies because of the volume and sensitivity of its data and the risks involved. It must appoint a Data Protection Officer in India and an independent auditor, and run yearly impact assessments and audits.
Data Protection Officer (DPO): The individual a Significant Data Fiduciary appoints, based in India and answerable to its board, who represents it under the Act and handles grievances.
DPIA (Data Protection Impact Assessment): An assessment of the rights of Data Principals, the purpose of processing and the risks to their rights, which a Significant Data Fiduciary must carry out every twelve months.
Data Protection Board (Data Protection Board of India): The body set up on 13 November 2025 to take breach reports and complaints, inquire, direct remedial steps and impose penalties under the Act. It works as a digital office.
Voluntary undertaking: A commitment that a person under inquiry offers the Board and the Board accepts. It bars further proceedings on what it covers, unless it is breached.
Verifiable consent: Consent of a parent or lawful guardian that the Data Fiduciary checks against reliable identity and age details, or a token from an authorised entity such as DigiLocker, before processing a child's data.
Child: Anyone under eighteen. Their data needs a parent's verifiable consent, and they may not be tracked, monitored or targeted with advertising.
Cross-border transfer: Sending personal data outside India. The Act allows it unless the Government restricts a country by notification, and keeps stricter sector rules, such as RBI's data storage rules, in force.
Lending service provider (LSP): An agent of a lender that does part of its digital lending, such as sourcing, servicing or recovery. RBI makes the lender responsible for the LSP's handling of borrower data.
CERT-In (Indian Computer Emergency Response Team): The national agency for cyber incidents. Its 2022 directions require incidents to be reported within six hours and logs kept for 180 days in India.
Sensitive personal data: Passwords, financial, health, biometric and similar information protected under the 2011 rules made under s. 43A of the IT Act. On 13 May 2027 the DPDP Act omits s. 43A and the rule-making power behind those rules, and the DPDP regime takes over.

DPDP CHANGES (2025-11-13 to 2026-11-26):
- 2026-11-13 Commencement: Consent Manager registration opens under the DPDP Act (s. 6(9) and s. 27(1)(d) of the Act; rule 4 of the DPDP Rules, 2025). Applies: Companies that want to register as Consent Managers, and Data Fiduciaries that plan to accept consent through them. Changes: Rule 4 and the First Schedule apply from 13-11-2026: an Indian company with net worth of at least Rs 2 crore and an independently certified platform may apply to the Board. The Board may inquire into a breach of a Consent Manager's registration conditions (s. 27(1)(d)). The duties of Data Fiduciaries on notice, consent, security and breaches still start on 13-05-2027. Actions: Track which Consent Managers the Board registers and publishes on its website.
- 2026-10-06 Order: DPDP (Removal of Difficulties) Order, 2026 adjusts the wording of ss. 9(1) and 10(2)(c)(ii) (S.O. 5458(E) dated 05-10-2026). Applies: Data Fiduciaries processing data of children or persons with disability, and Significant Data Fiduciaries. Changes: s. 9(1) now refers to the personal data of a child or of a person with disability who has a lawful guardian. s. 10(2)(c)(ii) now requires a periodic data audit. The Board was established on 13-11-2025 and its strength fixed at four Members besides the Chairperson; appointments are made on the recommendation of search-cum-selection committees under rule 17. - 2025-11-13 Rules and commencement: DPDP Rules, 2025 notified; Act brought into force in three phases; Data Protection Board established (G.S.R. 843(E), 844(E), 845(E) and 846(E) dated 13-11-2025). Applies: Every Data Fiduciary, including banks, NBFCs, insurers, intermediaries and market entities, and their processors. Changes: Phase 1 (13-11-2025): definitions, the Board, the rule-making and overriding provisions, and the RTI Act amendment; rules 1, 2 and 17 to 21. Phase 2 (13-11-2026): Consent Manager registration (s. 6(9), rule 4). Phase 3 (13-05-2027): notice, consent, legitimate uses, security, breach intimation, retention, children, Significant Data Fiduciaries, rights, transfers abroad, exemptions, Board inquiries and penalties; omission of IT Act s. 43A. The Board is set up with its head office in the National Capital Region, and the number of its Members is fixed at four. Actions: Map personal data held and the lawful ground for each use. Redesign notices and consent records so consent can be proved (s. 6(10)). Build a breach response that reports to the Board without delay and within 72 hours, alongside RBI, SEBI or IRDAI and CERT-In six-hour reports. Set retention schedules that reconcile erasure with KYC, PMLA and sector record-keeping laws. Update processor contracts for rule 6 safeguards.
