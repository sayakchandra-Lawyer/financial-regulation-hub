Introduction and Conference Overview

The International Financial Services Centres Authority (IFSCA) organised a full-day
conference titled “Consumer Awareness and Protection in the Digital Era” on 6
October 2026 at GIFT City Club, GIFT City, as part of the 10th edition of IOSCO World
Investor Week (WIW) 2026.
The conference brought together senior officials from IFSCA, the Reserve Bank of India
(RBI), the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) and the Insurance Regulatory
and Development Authority of India (IRDAI), along with representatives from market
infrastructure institutions, banks, insurance companies, fintech enterprises, educational
institutions and law-enforcement agencies.
The conference focused on consumer protection across financial services, with particular
emphasis on digital frauds, risks associated with artificial intelligence (AI), institutional
safeguards and grievance redressal, in line with the WIW 2026 themes of “Investor
Resilience, Digital Deception and Scams.”
The increasing use of mobile applications, online platforms and AI-enabled services has
made financial services more accessible and convenient. However, these developments
have also given rise to new risks, including impersonation of regulated entities,
misleading investment information, deepfakes, account compromise, manipulative
digital interfaces and social-engineering frauds. Against this backdrop, the conference
provided a platform for regulators, financial institutions, technology providers and law-
enforcement agencies to share their perspectives on emerging risks, preventive
measures and initiatives to strengthen consumer awareness and protection.

Dr. Praveen Trivedi, Executive Director, IFSCA delivering his welcome address and introductory remarks

Dr. Praveen Trivedi welcomed the dignitaries, speakers and participants to the
Conference. He noted that the conference was organised as part of the 10th edition of
IOSCO World Investor Week 2026, focusing on the themes of investor resilience, digital
deception and scams.
In his address, Dr. Trivedi explained that IFSCA, being a unified regulator for financial
services in GIFT IFSC, adopts a broad approach to consumer protection. While
participants in different financial sectors are referred to as investors, banking customers,
insurance policyholders and pension subscribers, the term consumer encompasses all
these categories for the purpose of consumer education and protection.
He emphasised that consumer protection requires the collective efforts of all participants
in the financial ecosystem. Referring to a 360-degree approach, he highlighted the
respective roles of regulators, market infrastructure institutions, intermediaries and
consumers in ensuring a safe and trustworthy financial environment. He noted that stock
exchanges act as first-line regulators, while intermediaries have a direct responsibility
towards their customers.
Dr. Trivedi also drew attention to emerging forms of digital fraud, including OTP and SIM-
related scams, fraudulent messages regarding bank account deactivation, voice cloning
and impersonation of senior executives through AI-generated communications. He

question investment claims and make informed choices.

financial literacy outreach in collaboration with the Ministry of Panchayati Raj, the State

choices. She emphasised the need to ensure that digital platforms provide consumers

insurance products and providing consent.

Special Address by Shri R. Lakshmi Kanth Rao, Executive
Director, Reserve Bank of India (RBI)

Shri R. Lakshmi Kanth Rao, Executive Director, RBI, delivering his special address.

Shri R. Lakshmi Kanth Rao, Executive Director, RBI, delivered a special address focusing
on consumer protection in the banking sector, particularly in relation to fair market
conduct, digital financial frauds and emerging technological measures to safeguard
customers. He highlighted the importance of ensuring that the expansion of digital
banking services is accompanied by appropriate consumer protection measures and
effective grievance redressal arrangements.
Shri Rao discussed the regulatory measures undertaken by RBI to strengthen fair conduct
in banking services. He referred to recent guidelines on the sale and marketing of
financial products, including provisions relating to mis-selling and suitability of products
for customers. He also highlighted safeguards against practices such as the arbitrary
remote disabling of mobile devices financed through loans, emphasising the need to
protect customers from unfair treatment.
Addressing the increasing incidence of digital financial frauds, Shri Rao observed that a
significant proportion of frauds are perpetrated through social engineering rather than
direct breaches of banking systems. He explained how fraudsters manipulate individuals
into disclosing sensitive information or authorising transactions, while using mule
accounts to receive and rapidly transfer fraudulently obtained funds. He noted that
banks are increasingly using real-time transaction monitoring, behavioural analysis and
location-based checks to detect suspicious activities, enabling them to intercept
approximately 25–30% of potential fraudulent transactions.
Shri Rao also outlined a proposed compensation framework for victims of small-value
digital financial frauds involving amounts up to ₹50,000, expected to be introduced from
1 January 2027. Under the framework described, the affected customer would bear 15%
of the loss, while the remaining 85% would be covered by banks and RBI.
He further discussed various preventive measures under consideration to reduce the risk
of fraudulent digital transactions. These included introducing a one-hour delayed credit
window for certain high-risk transactions, providing customers with an opportunity to

                                               Page 11 of 35

identify and revoke suspicious transfers before funds are credited to the beneficiary.
Other measures discussed included an additional authentication requirement through a
designated trusted person for vulnerable customers, allowing customers to restrict
digital access to a specified portion of their account balance, and enabling them to set
time-based restrictions on digital transactions.
On the issue of freezing or placing holds on bank accounts suspected of involvement in
fraudulent transactions, Shri Rao referred to a draft Standard Operating Procedure (SOP)
intended to establish clearer processes, notice requirements and timelines. He
highlighted the need to balance timely intervention against financial frauds with the
protection of legitimate customers from unnecessary restrictions on their accounts.
Shri Rao also highlighted the use of artificial intelligence and data analytics in preventing
financial frauds. He referred to MuleHunter AI, an AI-based tool for identifying
suspected mule accounts, which he stated had demonstrated an accuracy rate of
approximately 95%. He also discussed the Digital Payment Intelligence (DPI) Platform,
envisaged as a shared intelligence system for financial institutions, incorporating a
common registry of suspicious identifiers and AI-based transaction risk assessment to
facilitate early detection and prevention of fraud.
Through his address, Shri Rao highlighted RBI's efforts to strengthen consumer
protection through regulatory measures, improved fraud detection, inter-institutional
information sharing and technology-based safeguards. He emphasised the importance of
preventive interventions alongside effective redressal and compensation arrangements
for customers affected by digital financial frauds.

Keynote Address by Shri K. Rajaraman, Chairperson,
International Financial Services Centres Authority (IFSCA)

Shri K. Rajaraman, Chairperson, IFSCA, delivering his Keynote Address

Shri K. Rajaraman, Chairperson, IFSCA, delivered the keynote address, highlighting the
growth of India's financial sector, the development of GIFT IFSC as an international
financial centre and the importance of consumer protection in an increasingly digital
financial environment. He emphasised that the expansion of financial services must be
accompanied by greater consumer awareness, transparent practices and effective
safeguards against emerging technological risks.
Shri Rajaraman highlighted India's progress in financial inclusion and participation in
formal financial markets. He noted that the country has approximately 590 million Jan
Dhan accounts, 290 million demat accounts representing around 135 million unique
investors, and 62 million mutual fund subscribers. He observed that these developments
demonstrate the increasing participation of individuals in financial services and
underline the importance of strengthening financial literacy and consumer protection as
the financial ecosystem expands.
Referring to the growth of GIFT IFSC, Shri Rajaraman stated that banking assets in the
IFSC had crossed USD 93 billion, while fund commitments had exceeded USD 45
million. He also highlighted that average monthly stock exchange turnover had reached
approximately USD 105 million, with daily open interest of around USD 21 billion.
Further, retail banking deposits had grown to approximately USD 2 billion, comprising
around 33,000 depositors. He noted that the increasing scale and diversity of financial
activities in GIFT IFSC make consumer confidence and protection important
considerations for its continued development.
Shri Rajaraman also discussed the potential for greater participation of the Indian
diaspora in GIFT IFSC's financial ecosystem. He referred to the approximately 36 million
overseas Indians, who collectively remit more than USD 145 billion annually, and
highlighted the opportunity for GIFT IFSC to serve their cross-border banking and
investment requirements.

A central theme of his address was the need to incorporate consumer protection by
design into financial products, services and operational processes. He emphasised that
consumer protection should not be treated as an additional requirement introduced after
a product or service has been developed, but should form an integral part of its design
and delivery. He observed that institutions must place consumer interests at the centre
of their operations to maintain trust and protect their reputation.
In this context, Shri Rajaraman outlined five key areas requiring attention from financial
institutions.

    1. First, design products and processes from the consumer’s perspective.
    2. Second, communicate clearly and disclose risks at the right time.
    3. Third, build security, privacy and fraud prevention into digital services from the

         beginning.
    4. Fourth, treat complaints as a source of information for improving systems and

         services.
    5. Fifth, remain accountable for activities performed through technology,

         intermediaries and service providers.
Shri Rajaraman also drew attention to emerging technological risks, particularly those
associated with artificial intelligence and advances in quantum computing. He
highlighted the potential implications of quantum computing for existing encryption
systems and referred to the need for critical financial infrastructure to transition towards
quantum-resistant cryptographic systems by 2029, in accordance with the guidelines
under the National Quantum Mission.
Concluding his keynote address, Shri Rajaraman emphasised that the sustainable growth
of GIFT IFSC depends not only on expanding financial activities and attracting
international participation, but also on ensuring that consumers can access financial
services with confidence. He underscored the responsibility of financial institutions to
adopt transparent practices, strengthen digital security, address grievances effectively
and promote informed financial decision-making.

Special Session: How to Combat Digital Frauds in the Era of AI
by Mr. Hardik Makadia, Assistant Commissioner of Police
(ACP), Cyber Crime, Ahmedabad

Mr. Hardik Makadia, ACP, Cyber Crime, Ahmedabad, delivering his special session

Mr. Hardik Makadia delivered a special session on emerging digital financial frauds,
highlighting the methods adopted by cybercriminals and preventive measures for
consumers. He stated that reported financial frauds in India had reached approximately
₹23,000 crore in 2024, representing a fivefold increase since 2019. He also highlighted
the involvement of organised cybercrime networks operating from Southeast Asia and
referred to domestic cybercrime hotspots such as Jamtara and Mewat.
Common Digital Frauds and Their Modus Operandi
Mr. Makadia explained various types of digital frauds through examples drawn from
cybercrime investigations:

    • Digital Arrest Scams: Fraudsters impersonate police or other law-enforcement
         officials, use fabricated legal documents and video calls to intimidate victims, and
         persuade them to transfer funds to purported verification accounts.

    • Fraudulent Investment and Romance Scams: Scammers use social media
         advertisements, WhatsApp and Telegram groups, and fraudulent investment
         applications to gain victims' trust. They display fictitious investment returns and
         subsequently prevent withdrawals or demand additional payments.

    • Task-Based Scams: Individuals are offered payments for completing simple
         online tasks, such as reviewing hotels or liking videos. He explained that payments
                                               Page 16 of 35
              Conference on Consumer Awareness on Protection in the Digital Era | 2026 | Post Event Report

         may originate from other victims' accounts, potentially resulting in the freezing of
         recipients' bank accounts.
    • Executive Impersonation and WhatsApp Hijacking: Fraudsters circulate
         malicious files to compromise WhatsApp Web sessions and impersonate senior
         executives, subsequently directing employees to make unauthorised fund
         transfers.
    • Fraudulent Loan Applications: Unverified loan applications may access users'
         contacts and personal information, which can subsequently be misused for
         harassment, blackmail and financial exploitation.
Preventive Measures
Mr. Makadia emphasised the importance of digital awareness and basic cyber security
practices. He advised consumers to regularly check devices linked to their WhatsApp
accounts, enable two-factor authentication, avoid downloading files or applications from
unverified sources and independently verify suspicious communications or payment
requests.
He concluded by reiterating the message “Stop, Verify and Take Advice”, encouraging
consumers to exercise caution before responding to suspicious financial requests.

The first panel discussion focused on emerging challenges for consumer protection

Opening Remarks by Mr. G. Ram Mohan Rao, Executive Director, SEBI

Shri G. Ram Mohan Rao, Executive Director, SEBI delivering his initial remarks

Mr. G. Ram Mohan Rao set the context for the discussion by identifying several challenges
arising from the rapid adoption of AI and digital technologies in securities markets. These
included impersonation of registered intermediaries through fraudulent websites and
social media profiles, the growing volume of unverified financial information, the
distinction between AI-generated analysis and unauthorised investment advice, and the
increasing accessibility of algorithmic trading tools to retail investors.
He also highlighted the shift in cyber threats towards investors' personal devices and the
risks associated with regulated entities relying on third-party AI applications. He
emphasised that outsourcing technology does not absolve regulated entities of their
regulatory responsibilities.
Mr. Rao stressed the need for regulators to move from reactive intervention towards
preventive measures aimed at protecting investors before financial losses occur. He

    • Institutional Safeguards: Representatives of the market infrastructure

         integrity, including safeguards for client securities, upstreaming of client funds to

and Initiatives Taken by IFSCA by Mr. Arjun Prasad, Chief

Shri Arjun Prasad, Chief General Manager, IFSCA, delivering his special session

Mr. Arjun Prasad, Chief General Manager, IFSCA, delivered a special session on
international standards relating to financial consumer protection and the initiatives
undertaken by IFSCA to strengthen consumer awareness and grievance redressal in GIFT
IFSC.
International Standards on Consumer Protection
Mr. Prasad outlined the international regulatory principles and standards relevant to
consumer protection, including those developed by the International Organization of
Securities Commissions (IOSCO), International Association of Insurance Supervisors
(IAIS), Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) and G20/OECD.
He highlighted IOSCO's 38 Principles of Securities Regulation and its Multilateral
Memorandum of Understanding (MMoU) and Enhanced MMoU (EMMoU), which facilitate
regulatory cooperation, information-sharing and cross-border enforcement. He also
referred to the work of IOSCO Committee 8 on Retail Investors, including its report
identifying nine good practices relating to complaint handling by financial institutions.

He highlighted the IFSCA (Complaint Handling and Grievance Redressal) Circular,
effective from 1 April 2025, which specifies a common grievance redressal process for
regulated entities across banking, capital markets, insurance and other financial services.
The Circular requires regulated entities to adopt a Board-approved complaint handling
policy, designate officers for complaint handling and escalation, and provide consumers
with an internal grievance redressal process before approaching IFSCA.
Mr. Prasad further highlighted IFSCA's initiative on the Consumer Charter, under which
regulated entities have been encouraged to adopt charters setting out the rights and
responsibilities of consumers and the commitments of financial institutions towards
them.
Key Suggestions for Regulated Entities
Mr. Prasad outlined five measures that regulated entities could undertake to strengthen
consumer protection:

    1. Dedicated Website Information: Maintain dedicated webpages providing clear
         information on financial products, services and complaint handling procedures.

    2. Effective Compliance: Implement regulatory requirements in their true spirit
         rather than adopting a checklist-based approach.

    3. Monitoring Digital Impersonation: Use AI-based monitoring tools to identify
         fraudulent websites, social media profiles and communications impersonating
         regulated entities or their officials.

    4. Consumer Awareness: Conduct regular awareness programmes to educate
         consumers about financial products, associated risks and digital frauds.

    5. Investor Relations: Consider appointing dedicated Investor Relations Officers to
         facilitate communication with retail investors and address their concerns.

The session provided an overview of IFSCA's consumer protection initiatives and
highlighted the role of regulated entities in ensuring transparent practices, accessible
grievance redressal and greater consumer awareness in GIFT IFSC.

1. Digital Payment Fraud Detection and Information Sharing

Mr. K. Satyanarayana Raju highlighted the scale of digital payments in India, noting that
monthly UPI transactions had reached approximately 24–29 billion, alongside a
significant increase in reported cyber fraud complaints.

He discussed the Digital Payment Intelligence (DPI) Platform, operationalised on 1
September 2026, which facilitates information sharing among participating banks to
identify suspicious accounts and prevent fraudulent transactions. He explained that the
initial phase involves sharing fraud-related identifiers to detect mule accounts, while the
subsequent phase envisages AI-based risk scoring of originating and beneficiary accounts
to identify suspicious transactions before funds are transferred.

2. Banking Challenges and Customer Identification

Mr. Shiv Bajrang Singh highlighted challenges faced by banks in identifying fraudulent
accounts, including inconsistencies in customer identity documents, misuse of accounts
and the changing geographical concentration of mule-account networks.

He emphasised the need for common customer identifiers, improved Know Your
Customer (KYC) processes and greater information sharing among financial institutions
to strengthen fraud prevention and facilitate timely identification of suspicious accounts.

3. AI-Based Fraud Detection in Insurance

Mr. Abhay Tewari discussed the application of artificial intelligence and machine learning
in insurance operations, particularly for identifying fraudulent claims and assessing
emerging risks.

He referred to the use of technologies such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
and autoencoders for developing simulated risk scenarios and improving risk
assessment. He also highlighted the importance of independent verification of high-value
claims through reliable sources, including hospitals and other service providers, to
reduce fraudulent claims while avoiding unnecessary inconvenience to genuine
policyholders.

4. Consumer Protection in Fintech Platforms

Mr. Ashish Kashyap discussed the importance of incorporating security and compliance
requirements into the design of digital financial products. He highlighted safeguards
adopted by INDmoney, including multi-factor authentication and continuous monitoring
of fraudulent websites and digital profiles impersonating legitimate financial service
providers.

He further emphasised that consumer protection should not remain confined to
compliance departments. Product managers, software developers and user-experience
designers must also understand regulatory requirements and incorporate appropriate
safeguards while developing digital financial services.

5. Emerging Technology Risks and Industry Collaboration
Mr. Siddarth Pai discussed the growing risks associated with AI-generated deepfakes,
digital impersonation and advances in computing technologies. He highlighted the
importance of strengthening cryptographic safeguards, including preparedness for post-
quantum cryptography.
He also advocated greater industry collaboration through mechanisms for sharing
anonymised fraud-related information, enabling financial institutions to identify
emerging fraud patterns and respond more effectively. He further highlighted the need
to adapt consumer education initiatives to changing digital consumption patterns,
including the use of short-form digital content to reach younger audiences.
Key Takeaways
The panel emphasised the need to shift from reactive fraud management towards
predictive and preventive measures, supported by AI-based detection systems,
stronger identity verification, timely information sharing and appropriate safeguards
before financial transactions are completed.
The discussion also highlighted that consumer protection must be incorporated into the
design and delivery of financial products, with responsibility extending beyond
compliance teams to technology developers, financial institutions and other
stakeholders. Consumer education, clear disclosures and timely digital alerts were
identified as important complementary measures for strengthening protection against
emerging digital frauds.

Special Session: Corporate Frauds – Case Studies by Shri
Pradeep Ramakrishnan, Executive Director, IFSCA

Shri Pradeep Ramakrishnan, Executive Director, IFSCA, delivering his special session

Shri Pradeep Ramakrishnan, Executive Director, IFSCA, delivered a special session on
corporate frauds, drawing upon historical financial scandals, real-world case studies and
examples from popular films to explain how fraudulent practices develop within
organisations. He highlighted the role of weak internal controls, inadequate oversight and
organisational structures in facilitating financial misconduct.
Understanding Corporate Frauds and Organisational Weaknesses
Shri Ramakrishnan discussed the Satyam corporate fraud case, highlighting how a major
financial fraud involving an organisation with approximately 50,000 employees was
orchestrated by a small group of individuals. He explained that frauds can remain
undetected when organisational departments, including finance, sales, procurement and
internal audit, operate in isolation with limited information sharing and cross-functional
oversight.
He emphasised the importance of strong internal controls, effective communication
across departments and institutional accountability in preventing and detecting
fraudulent activities.

                                               Page 32 of 35
              Conference on Consumer Awareness on Protection in the Digital Era | 2026 | Post Event Report

Historical Fraud Schemes and Lessons
Referring to the history of financial frauds, Shri Ramakrishnan discussed Ponzi schemes
and how similar fraudulent structures have repeatedly emerged over time. He cited
examples of plantation investment schemes and Emu farming scams in India, where
promises of unusually high returns attracted investors despite substantial underlying
risks.
He observed that such schemes often exploit expectations of high returns and limited
public awareness of earlier financial frauds, underscoring the importance of financial
literacy and investor vigilance.
Regulatory Oversight and Institutional Resilience
Shri Ramakrishnan also discussed the limitations of relying solely on legislation to
prevent financial misconduct. He observed that attempting to address every possible
form of fraud through increasingly restrictive regulations could adversely affect
legitimate business activities.
He emphasised that effective fraud prevention requires a combination of
appropriate regulation, strong enforcement, sound internal controls and
continuous institutional vigilance. He also highlighted the importance of
organisational resilience, including the ability to contain fraud-related incidents, respond
effectively and maintain business continuity.
The session highlighted that understanding regulatory requirements, strengthening
internal governance and encouraging accountability across organisational functions are
essential for preventing corporate frauds and protecting stakeholders.
