                        Findings

27. These two appeals are filed under Section 61 of the Code, challenging the
common order dated 16.07.2026 of the Adjudicating Authority passed in I.A. No.
06/2023 in C.P. (IB) No. 2060 (PB)/2019 and I.A. No. 05/2023 in C.P. (IB) No.
2061 (PB)/2019, which were dismissed. Both applications were filed under Rule

                                                     Page 14 of 31
11 of the NCLT Rules, 2016. They asked the Adjudicating Authority to recall its
order dated 19.04.2022, by which it had dismissed the Section 7 petitions of the
two Appellants against Cynosure Real Estates Pvt. Ltd. (the Corporate Debtor).
28. We note that the facts are almost the same and the questions are identical,
hence both appeals are decided together. We have heard learned counsel for the
Appellants on the question of admission. The Corporate Debtor has not appeared
and no notice has been issued to it. For that reason, we confine ourselves to the
question whether these appeals deserve admission. We make no finding on
whether any debt is owed to the Appellants. Dismissal at this stage does not
prejudice the Corporate Debtor.
29. It has been brought to our notice that Amarendra Financial Pvt. Ltd. paid
the Corporate Debtor Rs. 1.50 crore on 05.04.2017 and Rs. 20 lakhs on
06.04.2017, a total of Rs. 1.70 crores. Similarly, Dinkar Commercial Pvt. Ltd.
paid Rs. 2.00 crore on 03.04.2017 and Rs. 30 lakhs on 06.04.2017, a total of Rs.
2.30 crore. Both describe these as short-term unsecured loans. Both are NBFCs.
The Corporate Debtor had been incorporated on 24.03.2017.
30. We have been advised that both the Appellants filed petitions under
Section 7 of the Code on 22.08.2019. The Corporate Debtor filed its reply on
09.10.2019. The Appellants stated that the reply denied any loan and denied that
the Appellants were financial creditors. It has been brought out to us that in
January 2022, the Appellants placed on record an unaudited balance sheet of the
Corporate Debtor for the year 2017-18. A note to that balance sheet lists

                                                     Page 15 of 31
unsecured loans and shows Rs. 1,70,00,000 against Amarendra Financial Pvt.
Ltd. and Rs. 2,30,00,000 against Dinkar Commercials Pvt. Ltd. The petitions
were dismissed on 19.04.2022.
31. We note that the reasons for that dismissal are contained in para three of
the impugned order. The Adjudicating Authority recorded that no loan agreement
had been filed. The bank statement was not certified by the bank. The terms of
repayment were not shown. No proof of service was given for the Appellants’
letters dated 14.07.2017, 12.01.2018, 11.04.2018 and 30.04.2019. Not a single
document from the Corporate Debtor acknowledging the debt had been produced.
On this basis the Adjudicating Authority held that the amounts were not shown
to be a financial debt under Section 5(8) of the Code.
32. We also take into consideration the fact that no appeal was filed against
the order dated 19.04.2022. The Appellants have also declared that they have filed
no other proceeding. They filed the recall applications on 29.12.2022. On
22.02.2023 they filed an affidavit placing on record the Corporate Debtor’s
income tax return for the assessment year 2018-19. Further affidavits and written
submissions followed on 03.06.2024, 13.08.2024, 09.10.2025 and 27.02.2026.
The recall applications were dismissed on 16.07.2026.
33. We note that the Adjudicating Authority accepted that it has inherent power
to recall its orders and held that the power is used sparingly and not as a review,
and that fraud is a ground on which it can be used. It then held that there was
nothing to suggest fraud as the order of 19.04.2022 was passed after hearing the

                                                     Page 16 of 31
Appellants, on their inability to produce cogent evidence. The onus was on them.
Allegations of fraud were easy to make but difficult to prove, and bald assertions
were not enough. It also observed that an NBFC must lend only on a formal
written agreement, and referred to an RBI Master Circular on the Fair Practices
Code dated 01.07.2015.
34. The Appellants’ main complaint is that the impugned order does not
mention the balance sheet or the income tax return, and that a finding of no fraud
cannot stand without dealing with them. The appellants argued that the view on
written agreements is contrary to the law and requested that the impugned order
be set aside, the order of 19.04.2022 be recalled and the petitions be restored. In
the alternative they ask for a remand.
35. Based on the broad facts noted above, we find that three questions arise.
First, what is the scope of the recall power, and what can be examined in this
appeal. Second, whether the order dated 19.04.2022 was obtained by fraud or
misrepresentation on the Adjudicating Authority. Third, whether the way the
Adjudicating Authority dealt with the plea calls for interference or remand.

       Since all these issues are interconnected, interdependent, and interrelated,
we shall treat them as a single issue and determine them in a conjoint manner in
the subsequent discussions.
36. At first, we will deal with the scope of the recall powers of the Adjudicating
authority or, for that matter, of this appellate tribunal against an order refusing

                                                     Page 17 of 31
recall is an order of the Adjudicating Authority where an appeal lies against it
under Section 61.
37. We are of the considered opinion that the law on the power of recall is
now clear. A five-member bench of this Appellate Tribunal held in Union Bank
of India v. Dinkar T. Venkatasubramanian & Ors., Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No.
729 of 2020 (reported as 2023 SCC OnLine NCLAT 283) that the Tribunal has
no power of review. It has an inherent power to recall its judgment, preserved by
Rule 11 of its Rules. That power can be used where a procedural error has
occurred, for example where a necessary party was not served. It can also be used
on other grounds, one of which is fraud played on the Court in obtaining the
judgment. The Hon’ble Supreme Court dismissed the appeal against that decision
on 31.07.2023 in Civil Appeal No. 4620 of 2023 (Union Bank of India v.
Financial Creditors of Amtek Auto Ltd.), saying that it agreed with the view of
the five-member bench. Thus, it becomes clear that the outcome of this Appellate
tribunal held by a five-member bench is now final.
38. We also know that in the case of Prabhjit Singh Soni (supra), the Hon’ble
Supreme Court dealt with the same power in the hands of the Adjudicating
Authority. It held that Rule 11 of the NCLT Rules, 2016 preserves the inherent
power of the Tribunal, and that neither the Code nor the Regulations prohibit its
use. It referred to Section 60(5)(c) of the Code. It then sets the limits. The power
is to be exercised sparingly and not as a tool to re-hear the matter. Ordinarily an
application for recall is maintainable on limited grounds. These include an order

                                                     Page 18 of 31
passed without jurisdiction, an order passed without notice to a party who was
affected, and an order obtained by misrepresentation of facts or by fraud on the
Court or Tribunal resulting in gross failure of justice.
39. We need to appreciate that recall and review are different. A party who
says the Tribunal made a mistake of fact or law, overlooked a document on the
record, took a wrong view of the evidence, or has found new evidence later, is
asking for a review or an appeal. The Adjudicating Authority has no power of
review. It cannot use recall to do what it cannot do by review, even where the
error is plain on the face of the record. The Adjudicating Authority said so in para
5 of its order, and it was right.
40. We are of the considered opinion that refusal of recall is a matter of judicial
discretion, and this appellate tribunal cannot disturb the way it was exercised.
Where the reasons given are incomplete, we can look at the record ourselves and
decide whether the result is right. We do that here.
41. While dealing with such an application for recall, the adjudicating
authority needs to be cautious, keeping in consideration the fact that a petition
under Section 7 can lead to a moratorium and a change in the control of the
company. Such petitions are meant to be decided quickly. A dismissal that is not
appealed lets the company and its other creditors plan on that footing. If it could
be reopened years later on a charge of fraud that does not hold, the speed and
certainty that the Code promises would be lost. At the same time the power is
real, and a proved fraud on the Tribunal must be undone whenever it is found.

                                                     Page 19 of 31
The balance is struck by insisting on proof of the fraud. We apply that standard
in the present case which sets the boundary of the present appeals. We are asked
to decide whether recall was rightly refused. We are not asked to decide whether
the order dated 19.04.2022 was right. That order was open to appeal under Section
61 of the code. The Appellants did not file one. A recall application cannot be
used to get around the limitation for an appeal that was not filed. The prayer to
restore the petitions follows only if recall was justified. The question is therefore
narrow. Did the Appellants show that the order of 19.04.2022 was obtained by
fraud or misrepresentation on the Adjudicating Authority?
42. We are of the considered opinion that the principle is settled on this issue.
A judgment obtained by fraud on the court is a nullity, and a court or tribunal is
not powerless to recall it as noted read civil cases by the Honourable Supreme
Court where it has been stipulated that that no court or tribunal can be regarded
as powerless to recall its own order if it is convinced that the order was wangled
through fraud or misrepresentation of such a dimension as would affect the very
basis of the claim. That case concerned awards obtained on a false account of
how the injuries were caused, a fraud of a high degree that came to light
afterwards. The Hon’ble Supreme Court recalled the words of Lord Denning that
no judgment of a court can be allowed to stand if it has been obtained by fraud,
for fraud unravels everything. Lazarus Estates Ltd. v. Beasley, [1956] 1 QB 702.
The decisions, and the nature of the wrong, show what a party must establish.
There must be a false statement of fact, or a deliberate concealment of a material

                                                     Page 20 of 31
fact that the party was bound to disclose. It must have been made knowingly or
recklessly or making a representation known to be false, from which injury
follows. The order must have been obtained because of it, so that went to the very
basis of the decision. The other side, or the Court, must have had no fair chance
to expose it at the time. And, in the words of Prabhjit Singh Soni (supra), there
must be a gross failure of justice.
43. It is one settled principle of law that in such cases, the plea must also be
specific. Where fraud is alleged, the party must set out full particulars, and the
case is decided on the particulars. A general charge that the other side lied is not
enough. Thus, the Tribunal which is being requested and asked to recall its own
order on this ground must look at the specific material relied on and say why it
does or does not show fraud.
44. In the above background, in order to judge whether the order of 19.04.2022
was obtained by any fraudulent act of the Corporate Debtor, we must first see
what the Appellants had to prove in their Section 7 petitions. Prima facie, we are
of the opinion that a financial creditor must show a financial debt owed to it and
a default by the corporate debtor. A financial debt, under Section 5(8), is a debt
disbursed against the consideration for the time value of money. Default, under
Section 3(12), is non-payment of a debt that has become due and payable. The
application in Form 1 must carry the particulars of the financial debt and the
documents, records and evidence of default. The Adjudicating Authority has to

                                                     Page 21 of 31
be satisfied, on the material that the financial creditor produces, that a default has
occurred.
45. After examining the facts of the present case, in light of the discussion in
preceding para elaborating certain basic facts which should be met to make a
financial debt, we also find certain gaps (as noted in the order by the Adjudicating
Authority in its order on 19 April 2022), which are material in the present case.
Seen this way, the gaps noted on 19.04.2022 were gaps in proof of these very
things. The want of a loan agreement and of the terms of payment went to the
nature of the debt and the date on which it fell due. The uncertified bank statement
went to the disbursal. The absence of proof of service of the letters went to
demand and default. The absence of any acknowledgement by the Corporate
Debtor went to the admission of liability. The balance sheet and the return, even
taken at their highest, speak only to the last of these. We do not decide what the
effect would have been had they been accepted. The point is a limited one. The
order was passed on a failure of proof under several heads. The Corporate
Debtor’s reply was not the reason for it.
46. After hearing the pleadings set up before us by the Appellant, we find that
the Appellants’ case of fraud has three parts. One, the Corporate Debtor’s reply
dated 09.10.2019 was false, because its own books showed the loans. Two, the
Corporate Debtor suppressed its income tax return for the assessment year 2018-
19 throughout the Section 7 proceedings. Three, the Corporate Debtor never filed

                                                     Page 22 of 31
audited balance sheets with the Registrar of Companies, which kept the
Appellants from placing them on record. We take these in turn.
47. First, we take up the issue of reply. The Appellants describe it in two ways.
In the facts and the grounds, the Appellants submitted that the Corporate Debtor
denied that any loan was taken. In one of the grounds, the Appellant stated that
the Corporate Debtor never denied receipt or utilization of the loan amounts and
treated that as an implied admission. Both cannot be right. What the reply actually
says matters, and it has to be read as it stands and not as the Appellants summarise
it.
48. If the reply only denied that the Appellants were financial creditors, it took
a stand on how the transaction should be characterised under Section 5(8) of the
Code. That is a debt disbursed against the consideration for the time value of
money. Whether a transfer of money meets that description was the very question
the Adjudicating Authority had to decide. Money paid between companies may
be a loan, an advance, a deposit or something else. A stand on that question, even
a wrong one, is a legal position that the Tribunal is there to decide. It is not a false
statement of fact.
49. If, on the other hand, the reply denied receipt of the money, it would be
false on the Corporate Debtor’s own books and on the bank statements the
Appellants had filed. But that is not clearly the Appellants’ case, and the reply
must be read to see. Even then, the answer would not change, for the reasons.

                                                     Page 23 of 31
50. The question whether the money was a loan was the matter in contest. Both
sides placed their stands before the Tribunal. The Appellants had the chance to
expose the weakness of the Corporate Debtor’s stand, and they tried to do so with
the balance sheet. When the Tribunal decided that question, it decided a contested
point on the merits. That a party’s stand on a contested point was weak does not
make the decision on it a fraud on the Court. A knowingly false statement that
the Tribunal accepted and acted on stands on a different footing, and the cases
cited above would then apply.
51. As regard the issue of the balance sheet, we note that the Appellants
themselves placed the unaudited balance sheet for 2017-18 on the record in
January 2022. That was about three months before the order of 19.04.2022. So,
the Adjudicating Authority had before it the Corporate Debtor’s denial and the
document said to contradict it. The Corporate Debtor was heard. The order
records that the Authority heard both parties and perused the documents. We are
of clear opinion that fraud on a court means that the court was deceived. A
document put before the court by the very party who now alleges fraud was not
kept from the court. If the Authority did not accept the balance sheet as proof, or
did not mention it, the complaint is that it made an error. An error of that kind,
even a manifest one, is a ground of appeal and not of recall.
52. The Appellants’ own pleading point the same way. Ground II calls the
Authority’s disregard of the balance sheet “inadvertent”. That word describes a
mistake by the Tribunal. It does not describe a fraud by the Corporate Debtor.

                                                     Page 24 of 31
The Appellants add that the mistake was “induced” by the Corporate Debtor’s
false averment. We do not see how. The Corporate Debtor did not hide the
document. The Appellants filed it and argued on it.
53. The Appellants also argued that the Authority was swayed by the denial.
The reasons for the order of 19.04.2022, as reproduced in the impugned order, do
not show this. The Authority did not say that it accepted the Corporate Debtor’s
version. It found that the Appellants had not produced a loan agreement, a
certified bank statement, the terms of the loan, proof of service of their letters, or
any acknowledgement by the Corporate Debtor. Each of these is a gap in the
Appellants’ own proof. Under Section 7 it is for the financial creditor to place
evidence of the debt and of the default before the Adjudicating Authority, which
must be satisfied that a default has occurred. The order of 19.04.2022 was passed
because that proof was found wanting. It was not obtained by the Corporate
Debtor’s reply.
54. The Appellants also argued that the Corporate Debtor never disputed the
authenticity of the balance sheet. We find that Silence of that kind does not prove
a document. In a Section 7 petition the Adjudicating Authority has to be satisfied
with the material before it. It cannot treat the absence of a denial as proof of debt
and default. Nor was the Corporate Debtor bound to say anything about a
document that the Appellants produced late, at the rejoinder stage.
55. We also find that the Appellants relied rather too heavily on what the
balance sheet proves. In this connection, we would like to refer to the ratio held

                                                     Page 25 of 31
by the Honourable Supreme Court in case of the Asset Reconstruction Company
(India) Ltd. v. Bishal Jaiswal, (2021) 6 SCC 366, where the Honourable Supreme
Court held that an entry in a balance sheet can amount to an acknowledgement of
liability under Section 18 of the Limitation Act, 1963. It also held that whether it
does depend on the facts of each case, including any caveats in the balance sheet
or the auditor’s report. That decision is about limitation. It does not hold that a
balance sheet entry proves that money was disbursed against the time value of
money, or fixes the date on which a debt became due and payable. The balance
sheet here is unaudited. The Appellants have not explained and elaborated the
details regarding who signed it, when it was approved, or how they came to have
it. These are matters that had to be argued in an appeal against the order of
19.04.2022. we do not find merit in the contention of the appellant on this issue;
the point was one for appeal and not for recall.
56. Now we look into points raised by the Appellant about the income tax
return. This is the only new document. It was placed on record on 22.02.2023,
after the order. The Appellants say the Corporate Debtor suppressed it. We do not
find suppression as a respondent in a Section 7 petition has no duty to volunteer
its tax returns to the applicant. The burden of proving debt and default lies on the
financial creditor, who must furnish evidence of default with the application. If a
financial creditor believes that the debtor holds a document that proves its case,
it can ask the Adjudicating Authority to direct its production. The Adjudicating
Authority has the power to do so. Nothing in the Appeal or pleadings show that

                                                     Page 26 of 31
the Appellants asked for the return, or for any of the Corporate Debtor’s books,
at any time before 19.04.2022. A party cannot be said to have hidden a document
that was never called for from it. Further, the return adds little. The excerpt in the
memoranda shows one line, “Other loans and advances”, of Rs. 24,62,80,069,
forming part of “Total long-term borrowings”. It names no lender. It matches only
the grand total of the balance sheet note. In the balance sheet the same total is
shown under “short term borrowings”. So, the return repeats a total figure, and
the two documents do not even classify it alike. The only document that names
the Appellants is the balance sheet note, and that was already before the Tribunal.
The Appellants say that two independent filings record their loans under the
specific head “Unsecured Loans”. The excerpt does not bear this out. Both
documents were also prepared by the Corporate Debtor from its own books, so
they are not independent of each other. The Appellants pleaded that the return
was within the exclusive knowledge and custody of the Corporate Debtor, and
also that they discovered it in February 2023. Both cannot be wholly true. The
appeal does not indicate how or from whom the Appellants obtained it, or what
stopped them from getting it earlier. The Appellants themselves submitted that
the return had been filed before the Corporate Debtor’s reply of 09.10.2019. They
had a copy of the unaudited balance sheet by January 2022, and do not explain
why the route by which they got it could not also have produced the return. If
they could obtain the return in 2023, they could have sought it in the two years
and eight months the petitions were pending. A document that could have been

                                                     Page 27 of 31
brought out in the original proceedings with ordinary care cannot be called
fraudulently kept back. At its highest the return is new evidence found after the
order. Discovery of new evidence is a ground for review. The Adjudicating
Authority has no power of review. New evidence can support recall only if its
non-production was itself a fraud on the Tribunal, and for the reasons above it
was not. If new evidence found later could reopen a dismissed petition, no order
under the Code would be final. Recall would become review under another name.
We are of the considered opinion that a recall must not become a tool to re-hear
the matter.
57. The Appellants argue on the question of onus that the Authority placed the
whole burden on them and ignored the Corporate Debtor’s conduct. In the Section
7 petition the burden was on the Appellants, and the Tribunal was right to say so.
In the recall applications the burden was on the Appellants again, this time to
prove fraud. They had to show that the order was obtained by it. For the reasons
given, they did not.
58. Several grounds in the appeals are really arguments on the merits of the
original petitions. The submissions that a balance sheet entry is an
acknowledgement under Section 18 of the Limitation Act, that a written
agreement is not needed to prove a loan, and that the bank statements were
enough, would have formed the content of an appeal against the order of
19.04.2022. They are not grounds for recall, and we do not examine them as such.

                                                     Page 28 of 31
We have looked at them only to the extent that they bear on whether any fraud
was practised.
59. The Appellants also argue that fraud can be raised at any time and that
limitation is no bar. That is correct where fraud is established. It does not help
where fraud is not made out. An unproven claim of fraud cannot be used to get
around the limitation for an appeal against the original order. We do not hold the
recall applications to be late. The point is that the Appellants chose recall over
appeal, and recall has a narrower field. A party whose petition was heard, who
filed its documents and who lost for want of proof has not suffered such a failure
through any act of the other side. The remedy for a mistake by the Tribunal was
an appeal. The position would be different if the Appellants had been kept out of
the proceedings, or if a document that they could not otherwise reach had been
withheld from them. That is not the case.
60. We are of the view that the question in a recall application is not whether
a party’s stand was creditable, but whether the order under challenge was
obtained by a fraud on the Tribunal. On the material before us, it was not. The
Appellants have not shown a false statement to the Tribunal on which the order
rested, or a concealment of a document that the Corporate Debtor was bound to
produce and that the Appellants could not otherwise get.
61. We are of the considered opinion that even the case of Prabhjit Singh Soni
(supra) does not help the Appellants. That was a case of an approved resolution
plan that did not deal, as the Code requires, with the claim of a statutory authority

                                                     Page 29 of 31
that held a charge over the corporate debtor’s land. The Hon’ble Supreme Court
allowed recall in that setting. The present case is of a different kind. There is no
resolution plan and no third party. The Appellants were the petitioners. They were
heard, they filed their documents, and they failed for want of proof. Their remedy
was an appeal.
62. The Appellants have not shown that the order dated 19.04.2022 was
obtained by fraud or misrepresentation on the Adjudicating Authority. The
documents they rely on either were already before the Authority or are, at most,
new evidence found after the order. The order was passed on the Appellants’ own
failure of proof and not on the Corporate Debtor’s reply. The Adjudicating
Authority was right to refuse recall, though its reasons were thin. The appeals do
not disclose a case for admission.
63. We state that the applicant, who wants to recall the order of the
Adjudicating authority must plead the false statement or concealment with
particulars. The applicant must show that it was made knowingly or recklessly to
the Tribunal, that the order was obtained because of it, and that the applicant had
no fair chance to meet it at the time. A document that was already on the record,
or that the applicant could have called for in the original proceedings, cannot
support a charge of fraud. A mistake in dealing with such a document is for
appeal. New evidence found later is not a ground for recall. In the present case,
we do not find any reason or cause that support the pleadings of the appellants.

                                                     Page 30 of 31
These findings apply to both Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1839 of 2026 and Comp.
App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1840 of 2026.
64. In view of above detailed examinations of facts and law, we find no merit
in the appeals. The appeals fail and stand rejected. There would be no orders to
cost and, IA if any, stand closed.
