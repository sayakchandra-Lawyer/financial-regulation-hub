We, the members of Working Group (WG), constituted by the IFSCA in October 2025, are
pleased to submit this Report in accordance with the mandate entrusted to us.

The increasing frequency and severity of catastrophic events, driven by climate change, rapid
urbanisation and growing concentrations of economic assets, have significantly heightened
the demand for innovative catastrophe risk financing solutions. In response, the global market
for ILS, particularly Catastrophe Bonds (CAT Bonds), has witnessed substantial growth over
the past two decades. The broader ILS market reached roughly $120 billion at year-end 2025,
up from $107 billion in 2024, per a joint AM Best and Guy Carpenter estimate. That growth
has continued into 2026: the outstanding cat bond market hit a record $65.6 billion by the end
of Q2 2026, up 7% from year-end 2025, even after $9.6 billion of maturities that quarter.1

India presents a compelling case for the development of an ILS market. As one of the world's
most disaster-prone countries, India faces recurrent exposure to cyclones, floods,
earthquakes, droughts and other natural catastrophes. At the same time, the country's
insurance sector is expanding rapidly, accompanied by increasing policy emphasis on climate
resilience, disaster risk financing and financial innovation. These developments present a
timely opportunity to explore the role of capital markets in complementing traditional insurance
and reinsurance capacity.

Need for ILS

The increasing frequency and severity of natural catastrophes, driven by climate change,
urbanisation and the growing concentration of economic assets, have intensified the need for
innovative mechanisms to finance catastrophe risks. While conventional insurance and
reinsurance continue to play a central role in absorbing catastrophe losses, the scale of
potential losses has led to the emergence of ILS as an important source of alternative risk-
bearing capacity. Among these instruments, CAT Bonds have become the most widely
adopted mechanism for transferring catastrophe risks from insurers and reinsurers to
institutional investors.

Growth of ILS

Over the past three decades, the global ILS market has evolved into a mature segment of
international capital markets, providing insurers with diversified sources of catastrophe
protection while offering investors access to returns that are largely uncorrelated with
traditional financial market risks. Although the market has grown steadily, CAT Bond issuance
remains concentrated in a few jurisdictions, particularly Bermuda, with increasing participation
from Singapore, Hong Kong and the United Kingdom. The underlying catastrophe risks
similarly remain concentrated in North America and Europe, notwithstanding growing disaster
exposures across Asia and other emerging markets.

India as the HUB

India presents a compelling case for the development of an ILS market. As one of the world's
most disaster-prone countries, India faces recurring exposure to cyclones, floods,
earthquakes, droughts, landslides and other climate-related events. At the same time,
insurance penetration remains relatively modest, resulting in a significant protection gap
between economic losses and insured losses. Total insurance penetration in India is about
3.7% (with life insurance at 2.7% and non-life at 1%) and Insurance density (premium per
capita) sits at around $97 USD while the commensurate global averages stand at 7.3% for
insurance penetration and $943 USD for insurance density 3. Thus, compared to global
averages, these figures remain significantly low, especially in the light of towering goal of
‘Insurance for All by 2047’. Traditional reinsurance alone may not be sufficient to meet the
country's growing catastrophe risk financing requirements. Access to global institutional
capital through ILS, therefore, presents an opportunity to supplement conventional insurance
capacity, diversify sources of risk capital and strengthen financial resilience against major
natural disasters.

Terms of Reference of Working Group

Recognising these developments, the IFSCA constituted WG to examine the legal, regulatory
and operational framework necessary for enabling ILS within GIFT IFSC. The WG was tasked
with studying international regulatory practices, identifying legal and prudential considerations
relevant to the Indian context and recommending an appropriate framework for
operationalising an ILS market in IFSC.

This Report undertakes a comprehensive examination of the global ILS ecosystem. It traces
the evolution of ILS from their origins following major catastrophe events, analyses the
development of ART mechanisms and explains the role of Special Purpose Insurers (SPIs4)
in facilitating the transfer of insurance risks to capital markets. The Report also examines
India's catastrophe risk landscape and highlights the potential role of ILS in addressing the
country's catastrophe protection gap.

Evaluation of Regulatory Frameworks in other Jurisdictions

A detailed review has been undertaken of the regulatory frameworks adopted by leading
international jurisdictions, including Bermuda, Singapore, Hong Kong, the United Kingdom
and the United States. While each jurisdiction has adopted a distinct institutional model,
several common regulatory principles emerge. These include the establishment of dedicated
SPIs or equivalent vehicles, full collateralisation of contractual obligations, bankruptcy-remote
legal structures, transaction-specific authorisations, proportionate prudential regulation and
strong governance, disclosure and supervisory oversight. Collectively, these jurisdictions
demonstrate that ILS require a regulatory framework that is distinct from the prudential regime
applicable to conventional insurers.

Legal Framework at IFSC

Against this international backdrop, the Report examines the existing legal framework under
the Insurance Act, 1938 read with its latest amendment via the Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha
(Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act, 2025. The current statutory requirement for minimum
paid-up equity capital was conceived for insurers operating as going concerns with diversified
and continuously evolving underwriting portfolios. By contrast, an ILS SPI is established solely
for a specified transaction, assumes only defined risks, operates for a limited duration and
funds its maximum contractual obligations through investor-funded collateral held in ring-
fenced accounts. Consequently, the prudential objectives achieved through conventional
paid-up capital are instead achieved through full collateralisation and legally protected assets.

Recommendations

Formation of SPIs/SPVs

Unlike conventional insurers, the SPI would not undertake underwriting activities involving
multiple policyholders, maintain diversified insurance portfolios or retain underwriting risk on
its own balance sheet. Instead, its liabilities to the sponsoring insurer would be fully secured
through collateral provided by investors, thereby substantially reducing counterparty credit
risk. In recognition of this unique business model, the WG recommends that SPIs should be
subject to a separate prudential regime and be exempt from the conventional paid-up capital
and solvency requirements applicable to insurers and reinsurers, while remaining subject to
appropriate governance, operational, disclosure and reporting obligations.

The Report, therefore, concludes that fully collateralised SPIs should not be subject to the
conventional minimum paid-up capital requirements applicable to traditional insurers. Instead,
it recommends the creation of a dedicated statutory carve-out under the Insurance Act, 1938,
or an equivalent legislative mechanism, permitting the establishment of SPIs subject to a
mandatory requirement of one hundred per cent collateralisation, restricted business
activities, bankruptcy remoteness, robust governance, comprehensive disclosure and
appropriate regulatory oversight. Such an approach would be consistent with internationally
accepted supervisory principles and with the regulatory frameworks adopted by leading ILS
jurisdictions.

Creation of a Conducive Ecosystem

The Report also recognises that the development of a competitive ecosystem will require the
parallel establishment of catastrophe modelling capabilities, actuarial expertise, SPV
administration services, trustee and collateral management functions, legal and accounting
advisory services and active participation by insurers, reinsurers, institutional investors and
other market participants.

Implementation Strategy

In light of these findings, the WG recommends a phased implementation strategy comprising
legislative enablement, notification of a dedicated regulatory framework, ecosystem
development. The first step would involve obtaining targeted statutory carve-out under the
Insurance Act, 1938 or such other legislative mechanism as may be considered appropriate.
This would be followed by the formulation of comprehensive regulations governing SPIs and
ILS within GIFT IFSC.

The WG believes that GIFT IFSC is uniquely positioned to emerge as a regional hub for
catastrophe risk financing. As a unified financial regulator overseeing insurance, banking,
capital markets and fund management within an international financial centre, IFSCA
possesses institutional advantages that enable the seamless regulation of ILS across their

                                                                                                                                   Page 9 of 41
entire lifecycle. By combining internationally aligned regulation with a supportive market
ecosystem, GIFT IFSC can attract global catastrophe risk capital, enhance India's financial
resilience against natural disasters.
The recommendations contained in this Report are intended to provide a robust, proportionate
and internationally credible foundation for operationalising ILS in GIFT IFSC. Their
implementation would not only integrate India into the global catastrophe risk financing market
but also establish an enabling framework capable of supporting financial innovation while
maintaining high standards of prudential supervision, investor confidence and policyholder
protection.

1. Introduction

1.1 Emergence of ILS

The global insurance industry has historically relied upon a well-established risk transfer
hierarchy; wherein primary insurers underwrite risks and transfer a portion of those risks to
reinsurers to maintain financial stability and underwriting capacity. For several decades, this
model adequately supported the insurance needs arising from natural catastrophes and other
large-scale loss events.

However, during the late twentieth century, the frequency and severity of catastrophic events
increased significantly, exposing the limitations of the traditional reinsurance market. A series
of major natural disasters including Hurricane Andrew (1992), the Northridge Earthquake
(1994), Hurricane Katrina (2005), the Tōhoku Earthquake and Tsunami (2011), and more
recent hurricanes, floods and wildfires, resulted in unprecedented insured losses amounting
to several hundreds of billions of dollars. These events not only caused extensive economic
disruption but also substantially depleted the capital base of global reinsurers.

The experience of these events demonstrated that catastrophe risk possesses characteristics
fundamentally different from conventional insurance risks. Catastrophic events are capable
of generating highly concentrated losses affecting numerous policyholders simultaneously
across wide geographical regions. Unlike ordinary insurance losses, catastrophe losses are
infrequent but potentially enormous, making them difficult to diversify within the traditional
insurance and reinsurance markets.

At the same time, economic development, urbanisation, increasing concentration of insured
assets and the growing effects of climate change have continued to expand global
catastrophe exposure. Insured losses arising from tropical cyclones, earthquakes, floods,
wildfires, severe convective storms and other natural perils have shown an increasing trend
over the past three decades. In parallel, the industry has also witnessed the emergence of
new forms of systemic risks, including cyber events, pandemics and infrastructure failures,
which further strain conventional reinsurance capacity.

These developments created sustained pressure on the global reinsurance industry. While
reinsurers continue to play a central role in catastrophe risk management, their balance
sheets are finite and subject to capital adequacy requirements. Following major catastrophe
events, reinsurers often need to replenish capital, resulting in higher reinsurance prices,
tighter underwriting conditions and reduced market capacity. The cyclical nature of the
reinsurance market can, therefore, leave insurers exposed precisely when additional capacity
is most needed.

The search for new and sustainable sources of catastrophe risk-bearing capacity led to the
development of ART mechanisms. Rather than relying solely on the balance sheets of
insurers and reinsurers, ART sought to access the significantly larger pool of capital available
in global financial markets. This marked the beginning of a fundamental shift in catastrophe

                                                                                                                                 Page 11 of 41
risk financing—from transferring risk exclusively within the insurance sector to sharing
catastrophe risk with capital market investors.

ILS emerged from this evolution as an innovative mechanism for transferring specified
insurance risks directly to investors. Among the various forms of ILS, CAT Bonds have
become the most widely used and internationally recognised instrument, enabling insurers
and reinsurers to obtain multi-year risk protection while providing investors with access to
returns that are generally uncorrelated with traditional financial market movements.

Over the past three decades, the ILS market has evolved from a niche response to a
reinsurance capacity crisis into a mature segment of the global financial system. Today, ILS
are issued by insurers, reinsurers, governments, multilateral development banks and other
risk-bearing entities across numerous jurisdictions, playing an increasingly important role in
strengthening financial resilience against natural catastrophes.

Despite this expansion, risk exposure embedded in outstanding ILS remains heavily
concentrated in the United States and Europe, with only limited exceptions such as
transactions covering Japanese earthquake and typhoon risk, New Zealand earthquake risk,
and a small number of World Bank-backed sovereign CAT Bonds (including facilities for the
Philippines and Pacific Alliance countries). Emerging-market and Asian perils, including
Indian catastrophe risk, remain largely absent from the capital-markets risk-transfer universe.

ILS help catastrophe-prone geographies by shifting extreme natural disaster risks away from
local budgets and traditional insurers onto global capital markets. They provide fast, rules-
based payouts that fund emergency response and recovery without draining public funds.

Mexico and the Philippines serve as global models for how vulnerable countries can utilize
catastrophe bonds and ILS to build fiscal resilience against natural disasters. Both countries
rely on the World Bank to issue these bonds.

In 2014, the World Bank arranged a US$30 million cat bond linked to hurricane and
earthquake risks across 16 Caribbean countries through the Caribbean Catastrophe Risk
Insurance Facility (CCRIF). The transaction enabled catastrophe risk to be transferred into
the capital markets and broadened the capacity available to the region.

In 2019, Aon’s Bermuda-domiciled White Rock Insurance (SAC) Ltd. issued approximately
US$70 million of privately placed Asagao principal-at-risk notes covering Japanese
earthquake risk. The transaction was structured using a parametric trigger and placed with
qualified institutional investors, understood to include ILS funds.

1.2 India's Catastrophe Risk Landscape and Insurance Protection Gap

India is among the world's most disaster-prone countries owing to its diverse geography,
climatic conditions and high concentration of population and economic assets. The country is
exposed to a wide spectrum of natural hazards, including earthquakes, tropical cyclones,

                                                                                                                                 Page 12 of 41
floods, droughts, landslides, cloudbursts, avalanches, heatwaves and, increasingly, climate-
induced extreme weather events. Rapid urbanisation, expanding infrastructure, industrial
development and rising asset values have significantly increased the economic
consequences of these hazards.

India's geographical profile illustrates the magnitude of this exposure. Approximately 58% of
the landmass lies in moderate to severe earthquake zones, around 12% is susceptible to
flooding, nearly 5,700 kilometres of coastline is exposed to cyclones and storm surges, while
large parts of the Himalayan region remain vulnerable to landslides and glacial lake outburst
floods. Climate change is further amplifying the frequency and severity of extreme weather
events, increasing both the probability and the financial impact of catastrophic losses.

Over the past two decades, India has experienced several large-scale catastrophe events,
including the 2001 Gujarat Earthquake, 2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami, 2008 Kosi Floods, 2013
Uttarakhand Floods, 2018 Kerala Floods, Cyclones Fani, Amphan, Tauktae and Biparjoy, and
recurring episodes of urban flooding in cities such as Mumbai, Chennai, Bengaluru and
Hyderabad. These events have caused substantial human suffering, widespread destruction
of property and infrastructure, and significant disruption to economic activity.

Despite this high level of catastrophe exposure, insurance penetration against natural
catastrophe risks in India remains relatively low. A substantial proportion of residential
property, agricultural assets, small businesses and public infrastructure remains uninsured or
underinsured. Consequently, the overwhelming majority of catastrophe losses are borne
directly by individuals, businesses and governments rather than being transferred to the
insurance sector.

This disparity between total economic losses and insured losses is commonly referred to as
the insurance protection gap. In many catastrophe events affecting India, insured losses
represent only a small fraction of total economic losses, leaving governments to finance
post-disaster reconstruction through budgetary allocations, relief funds, external
borrowing or other fiscal measures. Such dependence on public finances can delay
recovery efforts and increase fiscal pressures following major disasters.

The protection gap also limits the ability of insurers to provide comprehensive catastrophe
coverage. Given the potential accumulation of losses arising from a single catastrophic event,
insurers rely heavily on reinsurance to manage their catastrophe exposures. However, the
availability and cost of catastrophe reinsurance are influenced by global loss experience and
market cycles. Following major international catastrophe events, reinsurance capacity often
contracts and prices increase, affecting insurers worldwide irrespective of whether the losses
originated in their domestic markets.

As India's economy continues to expand and insured values increase, demand for
catastrophe insurance is expected to grow significantly. This, in turn, will require greater
access to stable and diversified sources of catastrophe risk capacity beyond conventional

                                                                                                                                 Page 13 of 41
reinsurance markets. While traditional reinsurance will continue to remain indispensable,
complementary mechanisms capable of mobilising long-term capital from global investors can
enhance the resilience of India's catastrophe risk financing framework.

ILS offer one such mechanism. By transferring defined catastrophe risks directly to capital
market investors, ILS can supplement conventional reinsurance capacity, diversify sources
of risk capital and improve the financial resilience of insurers against extreme loss events.
For India, the establishment of an enabling framework for ILS through the IFSC presents an
opportunity not only to strengthen domestic catastrophe risk financing but also to position
IFSC as a regional hub for insurance-linked capital market solutions.

India's increasing catastrophe exposure, coupled with a significant insurance protection gap
and dependence on conventional reinsurance, underscores the need for innovative
catastrophe risk financing mechanisms. While global jurisdictions have successfully
leveraged ILS to access diversified capital, India currently lacks an enabling framework for
issuing ILS. The IFSC, with its internationally oriented regulatory architecture and integrated
financial ecosystem, provides an ideal platform for developing such a market.

Further, India's insurance market is expanding rapidly, with increasing regulatory focus on
climate resilience and financial innovation. In such a ripe environment, IFSCA can play a
crucial role in making India a hub for ILS, especially as climate risk intensifies and the global
ILS market seeks geographic diversification. Realising this potential, however, requires
strategic regulatory reforms, investment in catastrophe risk modelling capability, and strong
collaboration between government, the private sector, and international ILS market
participants.

1.3 Benefits of ILS to Capital Markets

ILS have created an important bridge between the insurance industry and the global capital
markets. By allowing investors to assume clearly defined insurance risks in exchange for
attractive returns, ILS have introduced a source of risk and return that is fundamentally
different from traditional financial assets. Their significance therefore extends beyond
providing additional capacity to insurers and reinsurers as they have contributed to the
development of a distinct and increasingly institutionalised asset class within the capital
markets.

1.3.1 A Rare, Near-Uncorrelated Asset Class

One of the most distinctive characteristics of catastrophe bonds is their low correlation with
traditional financial markets. The return on a cat bond is principally driven by whether a
specified catastrophe event such as a hurricane, earthquake or other natural peril, occurs and
meets the predefined trigger conditions. It is therefore influenced by physical catastrophe risk

                                                                                                                                 Page 14 of 41
rather than by factors such as interest rates, equity valuations, corporate earnings or credit
cycles.

This makes ILS structurally different from most conventional asset classes. An investor
holding equities, corporate bonds and government securities may find that these investments
are simultaneously affected by changes in economic growth, monetary policy, inflation or
financial-market sentiment. Cat bonds, by contrast, are linked primarily to the occurrence and
severity of specified insurance events.

The resulting low correlation makes ILS particularly valuable from a portfolio-construction
perspective. For large institutional investors, even a relatively small allocation to an asset
class with genuinely different risk drivers can improve the overall diversification of the
portfolio.

1.3.2 Potential for Better Risk-Adjusted Portfolio Outcomes

The diversification benefits of ILS can translate into improved risk-adjusted portfolio
outcomes. Cat bonds have historically been regarded as offering attractive returns relative to
their volatility, while maintaining low correlation with traditional financial assets.

This combination is particularly relevant for institutional investors such as pension funds,
sovereign wealth funds, endowments and asset managers, which manage large and
diversified portfolios and are constantly seeking sources of return that do not simply replicate
the risks already present in their portfolios.

The appeal of ILS is therefore not merely that they may generate attractive absolute returns.
Their greater significance lies in the possibility of adding return without proportionately adding
exposure to conventional market risks. In portfolio terms, ILS can occupy a role similar to that
of other alternative investments, while providing exposure to an underlying risk i.e. natural
catastrophe risk, that is largely independent of the economic and financial cycle.

1.3.3 A Widening and Deepening Investor Base

ILS have also expanded the range of investors able to participate in insurance risk.
Traditionally, the capacity to assume catastrophe risk was concentrated largely within the
balance sheets of insurers and reinsurers. Capital markets have opened this risk to a much
broader pool of institutional capital.

Specialist ILS funds, asset managers and other institutional investors can invest in
catastrophe bonds and related structures, thereby bringing capital into the insurance
ecosystem from sources that previously had limited or no direct access to insurance risk.

This widening investor base is important for two reasons. First, it increases the amount of
capital available to absorb catastrophe losses. Second, it creates greater competition among

                                                                                                                                 Page 15 of 41
different sources of risk-bearing capital. Insurance risk is consequently no longer dependent
exclusively on the capacity and risk appetite of traditional reinsurance markets.

Over time, this has helped ILS evolve from a niche financial innovation into an increasingly
established component of the institutional investment landscape.

1.3.4 Expanding and Completing the Market

Perhaps the most important structural contribution of ILS is that they connect two markets
that historically operated largely separately: the insurance market and the capital markets.

Under the traditional reinsurance model, catastrophe risk is principally transferred among
insurers and reinsurers and ultimately remains concentrated on the balance sheets of
specialised risk-bearing institutions. ILS provide an alternative channel through which specific
insurance risks can be transferred directly to capital-market investors.

A catastrophe bond, for example, may transfer the risk of a defined hurricane or earthquake
event from a sponsor such as an insurer or reinsurer to investors. In return for assuming that
risk, investors receive a premium or coupon, subject to the terms of the transaction. If the
specified catastrophe does not occur, investors receive the contracted return; if the predefined
trigger is breached, some or all of the invested principal may be used to meet the sponsor's
catastrophe-related losses.

This mechanism effectively transforms insurance risk into an investable financial instrument.
Because catastrophe bonds can be held, traded and managed by capital-market participants,
they also create an ongoing channel for the transfer of risk between the insurance sector and
the wider financial system.

ILS therefore represent more than an alternative method of financing insurance risk. They
have changed the architecture of risk transfer by allowing insurance and reinsurance risks to
interact directly with global capital markets.

For capital markets, their value lies in the combination of diversification, additional risk-
bearing capacity and the creation of a new investable asset class. For the insurance industry,
they provide access to a significantly larger pool of capital than could be mobilised through
traditional reinsurance alone.

In this sense, ILS can be viewed as a mechanism for recycling global capital into catastrophe-
risk-bearing capacity—bringing insurance risk into the portfolios of investors while bringing
capital-market capacity into the insurance ecosystem.

This report examines these dimensions to set out how IFSCA can enable an ILS ecosystem
from GIFT-IFSC.

1.4 IFSCA's Initiative on ILS – Formation of Working Group

Recognising the growing importance of alternative sources of catastrophe risk capital and the
strategic opportunity for the IFSC to emerge as a global insurance hub, the IFSCA initiated a
detailed examination of the feasibility of introducing an ILS framework in IFSC.

With this objective, IFSCA constituted a WG on ART in February 2024 under the
chairmanship of Shri G. Srinivasan, former Chairman-cum-Managing Director of New India
Assurance Company Limited. It was entrusted with undertaking a comprehensive study of the
global ART landscape, including the evolution of ILS. Its mandate included an examination of
regulatory frameworks adopted in leading international financial centres such as Bermuda,
Singapore, Hong Kong, the United Kingdom and the European Union, and a preliminary
assessment of the idea of operationalising similar arrangements from GIFT IFSC. The WG
submitted its report to the Chairperson of IFSCA in June 2025.

The WG observed that ILS represent a distinct category of financial products that cannot
be adequately accommodated within the traditional regulatory frameworks governing either
insurance or securities markets. Unlike conventional insurance or reinsurance arrangements,
where insurance risks remain within the insurance sector, ILS transactions transfer defined
insurance risks directly to capital market investors through specialised financial instruments.
Consequently, an ILS transaction combines characteristics of both insurance contracts and
capital market issuances, requiring a regulatory approach that appropriately addresses both
dimensions.

The report highlighted that from an insurance perspective, the originating transaction involves
the assumption of insurance or reinsurance risk from a sponsoring insurer or
reinsurer. However, from a capital markets perspective, the principal purpose of the structure
is to transform that insurance risk into an investment product that can be subscribed by
institutional investors. This dual character distinguishes ILS from conventional insurance
products, reinsurance arrangements and ordinary debt securities, necessitating a bespoke
regulatory framework rather than attempting to fit ILS within existing regulatory categories.

The WG further recognised that the establishment of an ILS framework would complement
IFSCA’s broader objective of developing a comprehensive international insurance
ecosystem within IFSC. By enabling insurers and reinsurers to access global institutional
capital, an ILS framework would diversify sources of catastrophe risk capacity, enhance
market resilience, deepen the interaction between insurance and capital markets, and
strengthen IFSC's competitiveness as an international financial centre.

Accordingly, the WG recommended the formulation of a dedicated regulatory framework
for ILS that would facilitate efficient issuance of CAT Bonds and other ART instruments while
maintaining appropriate standards of investor protection, prudential oversight and market
integrity. The report envisaged a regulatory framework characterised by streamlined
authorisation procedures, proportionate regulatory requirements, clear disclosure standards
and robust governance arrangements, while ensuring that participation on the investor side

                                                                                                                                 Page 17 of 41
remained restricted to sophisticated and Qualified Investors capable of understanding the
specialised risks associated with ILS instruments.

A significant recommendation of the WG was the introduction of a new class of
insurance entity, namely the SPI, within the regulatory framework administered by IFSCA.
The proposed SPI would function exclusively as a bankruptcy-remote risk transformation
vehicle, assuming specified insurance or reinsurance risks from a sponsoring insurer or
reinsurer under a legally enforceable risk transfer agreement and fully collateralising those
risks through the issuance of ILS to investors.

1.5 WG for Developing Framework on issuance of ILS

The recommendations of the first WG established the policy rationale for introducing an ILS
framework in GIFT IFSC and identified the broad contours of the regulatory architecture
required to support ILS. However, the implementation of such a framework necessitated a far
more detailed examination of the legal, regulatory and operational mechanisms governing the
issuance, supervision and lifecycle of ILS transactions.

Unlike conventional insurance or reinsurance business, ILS involve specialised structures
that combine insurance risk transfer with capital market financing. In addition, a successful
ILS ecosystem depends not only upon an enabling regulatory framework but also upon the
availability of specialised market infrastructure, including catastrophe risk modelling firms,
collateral managers, trustees, legal advisers, calculation agents and other professional
service providers.

Recognising that these matters required focused technical analysis and stakeholder
consultation, IFSCA constituted another WG in October 2025 to develop a comprehensive
framework for the issuance of ILS from GIFT IFSC.

Unlike the first WG, whose primary focus was on examining the feasibility of introducing ART
mechanisms, the mandate of latter WG was implementation-oriented. The WG was tasked
with developing a practical regulatory framework capable of supporting the issuance,
operation and supervision of ILS within the existing institutional architecture of IFSC.

Specifically, the terms of reference of WG included:

    a) identifying the legal and regulatory requirements necessary to facilitate ART
         arrangements from GIFT IFSC;

    b) developing an enabling framework for the establishment, authorisation and supervision
         of SPIs for the issuance of ILS;

                                                                                                                                 Page 18 of 41
    c) proposing measures for developing the supporting ILS ecosystem within IFSC,
         including catastrophe modelling agencies, SPV managers, trustees, collateral
         managers, legal specialists, calculation agents and other specialised market
         participants;

    d) formulating guiding principles governing the structuring, underwriting, issuance and
         ongoing supervision of ILS;

    e) examining the feasibility of undertaking a pilot reinsurance sidecar transaction through
         GIFT IFSC as an initial proof of concept for ART arrangements; and

    f) considering any other legal, regulatory or operational matter incidental or
         consequential to the development of an internationally competitive ILS framework.

In carrying out its mandate, the WG adopted a comparative and principles-based approach.
The WG reviewed regulatory frameworks in established ILS jurisdictions, examined
prevailing international market practices and assessed the compatibility of these practices
with the legal and regulatory architecture governing IFSC. Particular emphasis was placed on
identifying regulatory gaps that could impede the efficient issuance and operation of ILS
while ensuring that any proposed framework remained proportionate, internationally
competitive and consistent with IFSCA’s statutory objectives.

Over the course of multiple meetings, the WG deliberated on a broad range of legal,
prudential, operational and market-development issues relating to the establishment of an
ILS regime. These deliberations culminated in a set of recommendations addressing the
regulatory design of SPIs, governance requirements, collateral arrangements, investor
eligibility, disclosure standards, risk transfer mechanisms and the institutional infrastructure
necessary to support a sustainable ILS market in IFSC.

This report sets out the findings and recommendations of the WG. It begins with a
comparative analysis of leading international ILS jurisdictions, examining how different
regulatory models have evolved to facilitate ILS while safeguarding financial stability and
investor confidence. Drawing upon these international experiences, the report subsequently
develops a proposed regulatory framework for the operationalisation of ILS from GIFT IFSC.

The subsequent chapters examine the international evolution of ILS, analyse the regulatory
approaches adopted by leading ILS jurisdictions, identify the essential building blocks of a
successful ILS ecosystem and develop a comprehensive regulatory framework for the
issuance of ILS from GIFT IFSC. The recommendations are intended to facilitate the
development of a robust, internationally competitive and prudentially sound ILS market
capable of serving both domestic and international risk transfer needs.

2. Study of Legal and Regulatory Framework in International Markets

2.1 Evolution of ILS in International Markets

The first CAT Bonds issued during the mid-1990s were largely structured through bespoke
legal arrangements because no jurisdiction had enacted legislation specifically addressing
ILS. As market confidence grew and transaction volumes increased, several international
financial centres recognised the strategic opportunity to position themselves as preferred
domiciles for ILS issuances. Rather than requiring these transactions to operate within
regulatory frameworks designed for conventional insurers, these jurisdictions introduced
dedicated legal and regulatory regimes tailored to the unique characteristics of fully
collateralised risk-transfer vehicles.

The evolution of ILS regulation has generally followed a common pattern across jurisdictions.
Initially, catastrophe bond transactions were facilitated using existing corporate and trust law
structures with case-by-case regulatory approvals. As the market matured, regulators
introduced specialised legal entities—commonly referred to as SPIs SPI, Special Purpose
Reinsurers (SPRs) or Insurance Special Purpose Vehicles (ISPVs)—supported by dedicated
licensing, governance and supervisory requirements. These frameworks recognised that ILS
issuers do not engage in conventional underwriting or assume diversified insurance portfolios;
instead, they operate as fully funded, bankruptcy-remote vehicles established solely to
transform insurance risks into investment securities.

Over time, regulatory frameworks evolved beyond merely authorising special purpose
vehicles. Leading jurisdictions introduced measures to streamline approval processes,
reduce unnecessary regulatory burdens, clarify collateral requirements, establish governance
standards, define investor eligibility and facilitate efficient issuance of ILS. Several
jurisdictions also introduced fiscal incentives, expedited authorisation procedures and
specialised service-provider ecosystems to enhance their competitiveness as international
ILS centres.

As a result, the global ILS market has become concentrated in a relatively small number of
jurisdictions possessing mature legal systems, internationally recognised financial centres
and regulatory frameworks specifically designed for insurance risk securitisation.

Among these, Bermuda has emerged as the world's leading domicile for ILS, owing to its
long-standing expertise in reinsurance and risk transformation. Singapore and Hong Kong
have developed dedicated ILS frameworks to establish themselves as regional centres for
catastrophe risk financing in Asia, supported by government grant schemes and streamlined
regulatory approval processes.

The United Kingdom introduced a comprehensive framework for Insurance SPVs to reinforce
London's position as a global insurance and capital markets centre. In the United States,
although ILS are generally issued through offshore domiciles such as Bermuda or the

                                                                                                                                 Page 20 of 41
Cayman Islands, the regulatory guidance developed by the National Association of Insurance
Commissioners (NAIC) has played a significant role in establishing principles governing
insurer participation in SPRVs and other structured risk transfer arrangements.

Despite differences in legal systems and regulatory architecture, these jurisdictions exhibit
several common features. Each has recognised the need for a dedicated regulatory
framework separate from conventional insurance regulation; each permits the establishment
of specialised risk transformation vehicles operating on a fully collateralised basis; each limits
participation to sophisticated market participants; and each seeks to balance innovation with
robust prudential safeguards, transparency and investor protection.

The experiences of these jurisdictions provide valuable guidance for developing an enabling
framework for ILS in the IFSC. While no single model can be transplanted in its entirety, the
common regulatory principles that have emerged across these markets offer useful insights
into the design of a proportionate, internationally competitive and prudentially sound ILS
framework for IFSC.

Further, it is found that in all major jurisdictions, ILS are issued through SPIs or vehicles that
are not subject to the traditional minimum capital requirements applicable to conventional
commercial insurers — a distinction that reflects the nature of the risk assumed, the limited
recourse available to counterparties, and the bankruptcy-remote structuring of the vehicle.
However, the SPI/SPV in every case must follow a fully funded or fully collateralised model,
under which capital is pre-funded in the form of collateral rather than held as an ongoing
regulatory buffer.

3. International Supervisory Standards – IAIS Insurance Core Principles and
       Supervisory Expectations for ILS

While individual jurisdictions have adopted different legislative and institutional approaches
for regulating ILS, their supervisory frameworks are underpinned by internationally accepted
principles developed by the International Association of Insurance Supervisors (IAIS).

The IAIS serves as the global standard-setting body for insurance supervision and develops
internationally recognised standards intended to promote effective and globally consistent
regulation of insurance markets. Although the IAIS has not issued a standalone Insurance
Core Principle dedicated exclusively to ILS, the regulatory treatment of SPIs and ILS is
addressed through several Insurance Core Principles (ICPs), most notably ICP 13
(Reinsurance and Other Forms of Risk Transfer), read together with the broader prudential,
governance and solvency principles contained elsewhere in the IAIS framework.

The IAIS recognises that the transfer of insurance risks through capital market instruments
constitutes a legitimate form of risk transfer alongside conventional reinsurance.
Consequently, supervisory expectations applicable to reinsurance arrangements extend
equally to ILS where insurance risks are transferred to investors through SPVs, SPRVs, SPIs
(collectively referred to as special purpose entities (SPEs) in the ICP).

The IAIS framework does not prescribe a single legal model for regulating SPEs. Rather, it
adopts a principles-based and outcomes-focused approach, recognising that jurisdictions
may implement different legal structures provided they achieve the fundamental supervisory

                                                                                                                                 Page 23 of 41
objectives of effective risk transfer, policyholder protection, financial stability and market
integrity.

ICP 13 expressly recognises that insurers may transfer risks not only through traditional
reinsurance contracts but also through other forms of risk transfer, including arrangements
that transfer insurance risks to the capital markets. Accordingly, the supervisory principles
applicable to reinsurance are intended to extend to ILS.

Under ICP 13, supervisors are expected to ensure that insurers maintain appropriate policies
governing their use of reinsurance and other forms of risk transfer and that such
arrangements effectively support prudent risk management rather than merely improving
regulatory capital positions.

Importantly, ICP 13 recognises that SPEs conduct insurance business, since they assume
insurance or reinsurance risks under legally enforceable contracts. Consequently,
supervisors should consider licensing and supervising SPEs as insurance entities. At the
same time, the IAIS acknowledges that SPEs differ fundamentally from conventional insurers
because they operate as transaction-specific, fully collateralised entities that do not engage
in ongoing underwriting activities.

The IAIS therefore supports a proportionate supervisory approach, under which regulatory
requirements applicable to SPEs are calibrated according to the limited nature, scale and
complexity of their activities rather than automatically applying the full prudential regime
designed for traditional insurers.

CAT Bonds are typically issued only to institutional or qualified investors capable of
understanding the specialised risks associated with structured catastrophe risk transfer.

4. Notification of Special Purpose Insurer (SPI) under the Insurance Act, 1938

4.1 Introduction

The successful introduction of an ILS framework in India requires not only an appropriate
regulatory architecture but also a legal framework that accommodates the unique
characteristics of SPIs. While the regulatory models adopted by Bermuda, Singapore, Hong
Kong and the United Kingdom differ in institutional design, they all recognise a common
principle: fully collateralised SPIs are fundamentally different from conventional insurance
companies and therefore warrant a distinct prudential regime.

The Insurance Act, 1938, however, was enacted to regulate insurers operating as going
concerns that continuously underwrite insurance risks over an indefinite period. The
prudential framework embodied in the Act—including minimum paid-up capital, solvency
margins and ongoing capital maintenance—is designed for insurers maintaining diversified
portfolios of insurance business and assuming long-term underwriting and operational risks.

                                                                                                                                 Page 24 of 41
An ILS SPI operates on an entirely different business model. It is established solely for the
purpose of assuming one or more clearly identified insurance or reinsurance risks under a
specified transaction. Its obligations are fully funded in advance through investor
contributions, secured by ring-fenced collateral and extinguished upon completion of the
transaction. It neither solicits insurance business from the public nor maintains an ongoing
underwriting portfolio.

These structural differences raise an important legal question: Should a fully collateralised
SPI be subject to the same statutory capital requirements applicable to conventional insurers?

This Report examines this question and presents the rationale for introducing a targeted
statutory carve-out under the Insurance Act, 1938 in the IFSC Jurisdiction The amendment
to the Insurance Act - the SBSR Act also assists in moving towards the enablement of
establishing a new line of “Insurance Business”. This is discussed in greater detail in the
report.

4.2 Existing Capital Framework

The Insurance Act, 1938 requires every insurer carrying on insurance business in India to
maintain minimum paid-up equity capital. At present, the Act prescribes:

             a) ₹100 crore for life insurers;
             b) ₹100 crore for general insurers;
             c) ₹100 crore for health insurers; and
             d) ₹200 crore for reinsurers.

The legislative purpose of these requirements is to ensure that insurers possess a permanent
capital base capable of absorbing unexpected losses arising from underwriting activities,
operational failures, investment volatility and other business risks throughout the life of the
institution.

Paid-up equity capital serves several prudential objectives:

    a) providing an initial financial cushion before policyholder liabilities arise;
    b) absorbing unexpected losses not anticipated in pricing;
    c) supporting business growth;
    d) demonstrating long-term financial commitment by shareholders;
    e) protecting policyholders against insurer insolvency; and
    f) promoting confidence in the insurance sector.

                                                                                                                                 Page 25 of 41
The capital requirement therefore reflects the assumption that insurers will continue writing
new business, renewing policies and managing evolving risk portfolios over an indefinite
period.

4.3 Limitation of the Conventional Capital Model

The prudential assumptions underpinning the Insurance Act differ fundamentally from the
operating model of an ILS SPI. Unlike conventional insurers, an SPI:

    a) undertakes only a predefined risk transfer transaction;
    b) does not continuously write insurance business;
    c) generally, has a limited operational lifespan;
    d) assumes only specified risks under a single or limited number of contracts;
    e) does not accumulate underwriting exposures over time;
    f) does not depend upon future premium income to meet liabilities; and
    g) funds all contractual obligations before assuming risk.

Most importantly, the SPI’s liabilities are fully collateralised from inception. Investor proceeds
are deposited into protected collateral accounts before the reinsurance agreement becomes
effective. Those assets remain legally ring-fenced and may only be used to satisfy obligations
arising under the specific risk-transfer transaction. Consequently, the financial resources
available to meet claims are not dependent upon shareholder capital, future earnings or
continued business operations. The insurer receives security through pre-funded collateral,
not through reliance upon the continuing solvency of the SPI.

4.4 Full Collateralisation as an Alternative Prudential Model

The defining characteristic of an ILS transaction is full collateralisation. Rather than
maintaining shareholder equity capable of absorbing uncertain future losses, the SPI
maintains collateral equal to its maximum contractual exposure. The prudential philosophy
therefore differs fundamentally.

Traditional insurance regulation asks: Does the insurer possess sufficient capital to meet
uncertain future obligations arising from an evolving insurance portfolio?

The ILS framework instead asks: Have all contractual obligations already been fully funded
through protected collateral before risk is assumed?

Under the traditional insurance model:

a) liabilities emerge gradually;
b) future claims remain uncertain;
c) premiums are received over time;

                                        Page 26 of 41
             d) shareholder capital absorbs adverse deviations.
Under an SPI:

    a) liabilities are contractually limited;
    b) maximum exposure is known at inception;
    c) collateral is fully funded before risk attachment;
    d) investors bear the catastrophe risk.
Accordingly, collateral functions as the primary prudential safeguard rather than shareholder
equity. In many respects, a fully collateralised SPI offers greater certainty of claims payment
than an insurer relying upon future solvency.
4.5 International Regulatory Practice
International regulatory practice consistently recognises this distinction. The jurisdictions
examined in the preceding chapter have each concluded that conventional minimum capital
requirements are not appropriate for fully collateralised SPIs.
Although their legislative mechanisms differ, they share common principles:
   a) recognition of SPIs as distinct regulated entities;
   b) substitution of full collateralisation for traditional solvency capital;
   c) bankruptcy-remote legal structures;
   d) ring-fenced collateral;
   e) transaction-specific authorisation;
   f) limited business scope; and
   g) proportionate prudential supervision.

                                                                                                                                 Page 27 of 41
For example:

    a) Bermuda authorises SPIs under a dedicated regulatory class and relies upon full
         collateralisation rather than conventional insurer capital.

    b) Singapore regulates Insurance Special Purpose Vehicles through a separate
         framework that recognises fully funded obligations as the primary prudential
         safeguard.

    c) Hong Kong similarly authorises SPIs subject to complete collateralisation and
         restricted business activities.

    d) The United Kingdom, through the Risk Transformation Regulations, expressly
         distinguishes Insurance Special Purpose Vehicles from conventional insurers and
         subjects them to a dedicated prudential regime.

The consistency of these approaches demonstrates an emerging international supervisory
consensus that fully collateralised SPIs require a regulatory framework distinct from that
applicable to traditional insurers.

4.6 Need for a Statutory Carve-Out

Although IFSCA possesses broad regulatory powers within the IFSCs, those powers operate
within the statutory framework established by the Insurance Act, 1938. The requirement for
minimum paid-up equity capital is prescribed directly by statute. Consequently, regulatory
rules alone cannot substitute full collateralisation for statutory paid-up capital unless the
enabling legislation expressly permits such substitution.

From a legal perspective, therefore, introducing an ILS framework requires statutory
recognition that fully collateralised SPIs constitute a distinct category of regulated insurance
entity. Such recognition would provide:

             a) legal certainty for sponsors and investors;
             b) consistency with international practice;
             c) reduced regulatory ambiguity;
             d) greater investor confidence; and
             e) enhanced enforceability of supervisory requirements.

Absent such statutory recognition, the validity of an SPI authorised without the prescribed
paid-up capital could be open to legal challenge.

4.7 Policy Rationale for a Carve-Out

A statutory carve-out is supported by several important policy considerations.

                                                                                                                                 Page 28 of 41
    1. The insurer’s exposure is fully pre-funded through collateral before risk attachment.
         Unlike conventional insurers, there is no reliance upon future shareholder support or
         retained earnings.

    2. The business scope of an SPI is extremely limited. It does not undertake retail
         insurance, solicit new policyholders or accumulate diversified underwriting exposures.

    3. Investor-funded collateral is legally segregated from the sponsor and remains available
         exclusively for payment of claims.

    4. Investors—not policyholders—ultimately bear the catastrophe risk associated with the
         transaction. Investors knowingly assume that risk in return for a contractual investment
         return.

    5. SPIs generally exist only for the duration of the underlying transaction, after which they
         are wound up. The long-term prudential concerns applicable to going-concern insurers
         therefore arise to a much lesser extent.

Collectively, these characteristics justify replacing traditional minimum capital requirements
with a prudential framework centred upon:

     a) 100% collateralisation;
     b) bankruptcy remoteness;
     c) ring-fencing of assets;
     d) restricted business activities;
     e) robust governance;
     f) regulatory oversight; and
     g) comprehensive disclosure.

This substitution does not weaken prudential supervision. Rather, it reflects a different—and
in many respects stronger—mechanism for securing policyholder protection.

4.8 Proposed Regulatory Framework for SPIs in IFSC

The review of international regulatory frameworks demonstrates a broad convergence in the
manner in which leading jurisdictions regulate ILS. While the legislative mechanisms adopted
by Bermuda, Singapore, Hong Kong and the United Kingdom differ in their institutional design,
they are underpinned by a common regulatory philosophy: SPIs are fundamentally different
from conventional insurance companies and therefore require a dedicated, proportionate and
transaction-specific regulatory framework.

                                                                                                                                 Page 29 of 41
Accordingly, the regulatory framework to be developed by IFSCA should be guided by
internationally accepted supervisory principles while remaining sufficiently flexible to
accommodate future developments in the ILS market.

At the outset, the framework should recognise ILS as a distinct form of insurance risk transfer
and establish a separate regulatory regime for entities undertaking such activities. Rather
than treating SPIs as conventional insurers, the framework should acknowledge their unique
characteristics, including their limited business scope, transaction-specific nature, full
collateralisation and bankruptcy-remote structure. The regulatory framework should,
therefore, be based on the economic substance of these entities rather than their legal form.

The regulatory framework should adopt the principle of proportionate regulation, under which
supervisory requirements are calibrated according to the nature, scale and complexity of the
risks undertaken by the SPI.

The framework should clearly define the permissible scope of activities of an SPI and their
purpose should remain limited to assuming defined insurance or reinsurance risks, issuing
ILS or entering into other approved funding arrangements, holding collateral, and
administering the approved transaction throughout its lifecycle.

A central pillar of the regulatory framework should be the requirement that all obligations
assumed by an SPI remain fully funded throughout the duration of the transaction. The
framework should prescribe appropriate standards governing the eligibility, valuation,
investment, custody and ongoing maintenance of collateral, ensuring that collateral assets
remain sufficient to meet the maximum contractual obligations of the SPI. Such assets should
remain legally segregated from the sponsor, investors and other counterparties and should
be protected against insolvency through appropriate trust or equivalent legal arrangements.

The regulatory framework should further ensure that every ILS transaction achieves genuine
transfer of insurance risk. Supervisory assessment should therefore extend beyond legal
documentation to the underlying economic substance of the transaction. Appropriate
contractual safeguards including limited recourse provisions, bankruptcy-remoteness,
enforceable collateral arrangements and clearly defined trigger mechanisms should form
essential components of the regulatory architecture.

Governance requirements should reflect the limited nature of SPI operations while ensuring
adequate oversight and accountability. Every SPI should maintain fit and proper
management, appropriate internal controls, sound operational arrangements and effective
risk management systems proportionate to its activities. The framework may also recognise
the role of specialised service providers, including administrators, trustees, collateral
managers, calculation agents and other transaction participants.

Recognising that ILS are sophisticated financial instruments intended primarily for institutional
market participants, the framework should establish appropriate safeguards governing

4.8.2 Financial Support Services Ecosystem

A comprehensive Financial Support Services ecosystem has already been enabled to support
the development and growth of the financial services market in GIFT-IFSC under three
specific regulations, namely, (i) IFSCA (TechFin and Ancillary Services) Regulations, 2025
(TAS Regulations) (ii) IFSCA (Book-keeping, Accounting, Taxation and Financial Crime
Compliance Services) Regulations, 2024 (BATF Regulations) and (iii) IFSCA (Global In-
house Centre) Regulations, 2025.

(i) The IFSCA (TechFin and Ancillary Services) Regulations, 2025 provides an important
enabling layer for the development of an Insurance-Linked Securities (ILS) ecosystem in GIFT
IFSC. These regulations establish a regulated ecosystem of ancillary and technology service
providers capable of supporting the implementation and operation of ILS activities. The
Regulations also recognise a broad range of services, including actuarial, advisory, risk
management, legal, audit, valuation, fund administration, compliance management,
trusteeship and research and capability-building services.

The recently enabled ‘Trust and Company Services Provider (TCSP) services to support
leasing activities through an SPV in IFSC will also play a significant role in ILS activities.

These services are particularly relevant to the ILS value chain. Actuarial, advisory and risk-
management capabilities can support catastrophe-risk modelling, risk assessment, pricing
and structuring of ILS transactions, while legal, valuation, audit, fund administration and
compliance services can provide the supporting infrastructure necessary for issuance,
administration, reporting and investor protection. The framework also permits technology-
enabled services, including big-data and analytics, climate and sustainable technology,
cyber-risk management, regulatory technology, supervisory technology, AI/ML, IoT, DLT and
tokenisation support, which can facilitate the development of sophisticated catastrophe-risk
analytics and technology infrastructure for the ILS market.

The Regulations further provide for centres of excellence and financial capability centres to
undertake research and development, skill development, training and certification. This can
contribute to building specialised capabilities in catastrophe modelling, insurance risk

                                                                                                                                 Page 33 of 41
analytics, ILS structuring, legal and regulatory expertise and capital-markets operations within
GIFT IFSC.

Accordingly, the existing TAS regulatory framework can be leveraged to develop a
comprehensive support-services ecosystem around the proposed ILS regime.

In this manner, the professional and technology services ecosystem can complement the
proposed ILS regulatory framework by improving market infrastructure, strengthening risk
assessment and disclosure capabilities, facilitating regulatory compliance and encouraging
innovation.

(ii) The IFSCA (Book-keeping, Accounting, Taxation and Financial Crime Compliance
Services) Regulations, 2024 can support ILS/SPV structures with maintenance of books
and financial records, preparation and analysis of financial statements and accounting
support, and tax advice and filings.

For ILS transactions such as CAT Bonds, these services can help ensure transparent
financial reporting, proper accounting of premiums, collateral and investor payments, and
compliance with applicable tax requirements. Having regulated BATF service providers within
GIFT IFSC can therefore reduce operational complexity, improve investor and regulator
confidence, and support the development of a reliable ecosystem for ILS and catastrophe-
risk financing.

(iii) The IFSCA (Global In-House Centre) Regulations, 2025 can support ILS activities in
GIFT IFSC by enabling insurers, reinsurers and other global financial institutions to establish
dedicated GIC unit for ILS structuring, risk analysis, catastrophe modelling, accounting, data
management, legal and compliance support. This can help institutions manage the technical
and operational requirements of CAT Bonds and other ILS transactions from within GIFT
IFSC, reducing dependence on external service providers.

For the ILS ecosystem, GIC Units can therefore act as a specialised support infrastructure,
bringing global insurance and reinsurance expertise into GIFT IFSC. This can strengthen local
capabilities in catastrophe risk assessment, transaction management and regulatory
compliance, facilitate knowledge and technology transfer, and help attract global insurance
groups to GIFT IFSC. Over time, this would support the development of a deeper ILS
ecosystem and contribute to positioning GIFT IFSC as a regional hub for catastrophe risk
financing.

benchmarking the standards with best practices in other international jurisdictions. The Listing

4.9 Conclusion

The introduction of a carve-out for SPIs is recognition that prudential regulation should be
calibrated to the nature of the risks undertaken as an ILS SPI is a transaction-specific,
bankruptcy-remote vehicle whose obligations are fully secured by investor-funded collateral
from inception.

Replacing the statutory equity capital requirement with a mandatory regime of 100%
collateralisation, ring-fenced assets, restricted business scope and robust supervisory
oversight would, therefore, would align India's legal framework with internationally accepted
regulatory practice.

6. Suggested Draft Amendment under Section 2 of the Insurance Act, 1938, for
       IFSCA

The working group has been examining the feasibility of introducing a regulatory framework
for Insurance Linked Securities (ILS) within India, with a view to developing a competitive
global hub for innovative risk transfer solutions and alternative capital for the insurance sector.

ILS have emerged globally as an important mechanism for transferring insurance and
catastrophe risks from insurers and reinsurers to capital market investors. In certain global
jurisdictions, these structures have contributed towards enhancing insurance capacity,
diversifying sources of risk capital, strengthening resilience against catastrophic events and
deepening integration between insurance and capital markets.

Considering India’s increasing exposure to climate-related risks, natural catastrophes and
large-scale infrastructure and economic assets requiring risk protection, development of an
ILS ecosystem can provide significant long-term benefits. Such a framework would facilitate
access to diversified global capital, support domestic insurers and reinsurers in managing
peak risks, and contribute towards strengthening financial resilience and disaster risk
financing capabilities.

The working group recommends that “Special Purpose Insurer (SPI)” is required to be notified
by the Central Government under the Insurance Act, 1938 to provide the necessary legal
recognition and regulatory foundation for operationalization of the proposed framework for
ILS.

7. Suggested Alternative Route

The recent amendments to the Insurance Act, 1938, via the Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha
(Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act, 2025, have, inter alia, widened the definition of
‘insurance business’ by inserting Sec. 2(6D) in the Insurance Act.

As per the inserted definition under Sec. 2(6D), insurance business means the business of
effecting insurance contracts and includes any other form of contract as may be notified by
the Central Government in consultation with the Authority from time to time. Further, it is
explained that the expression “insurance contract” means the contract whereby the insurer,
on payment of premium, undertakes to assume risk and to pay to the insured person an
agreed compensation for loss, damage or liability arising from a contingent event on such
terms and conditions and subject to such limitations as may be agreed.

Since the underlying intent of an ILS transaction is to transfer risk from a risk carrier (cedant
/ sponsor) to a bankruptcy-remote special vehicle (called Special Purpose Insurer) by entering
into a contract (i.e. reinsurance contract), the new definition of insurance business provides
a comfortable space for the regulator (IFSCA or IRDAI) to work with the Central Government
and enable the issuance of ILS instruments without proposing any amendments in the
Insurance Act as it stands today.

As amending the Insurance Act is an elaborate exercise and may take considerable time, the
WG believes that notification route may be explored under Section 2(6D) of the Insurance Act
to recognize ILS contracts funded through insurance securitization or any such funding
mechanism, as may be specified by the Authority (IFSCA / IRDAI).

                                                                                                                                 Page 38 of 41
Thereafter the other desired relaxations such as capital requirement, solvency etc., may be
explored under Section 2CA of the Insurance Act, 1938. This can be immediately followed up
by notification of relevant regulations to detail the granular modalities for the
operationalisation of the ILS ecosystem.

Thus, as a cleaner alternative approach, the WG suggests the following draft of notification
in terms of Sec. 2(6D) of the Insurance Act for the IFSC jurisdiction –

9. Development of ILS Market Infrastructure and Data Ecosystem

Market participants should proactively engage with established commercial catastrophe-
modelling vendors to develop and maintain India-specific catastrophe-risk models covering
key perils such as cyclones, floods and earthquakes. Such engagement should focus not only
on the availability and suitability of models for Indian geography, but also on obtaining
appropriate licensing and access arrangements for their use in underwriting, pricing,
structuring and ongoing risk assessment of ILS transactions.

Given the importance of reliable underlying hazard and exposure data to the credibility of
catastrophe models, market participants should also establish appropriate mechanisms for
accessing relevant publicly available hazard, meteorological, geological, exposure and loss
data maintained by institutions such as the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA),
Indian Meteorological Department/meteorological agencies, National Remote Sensing
Centre (NRSC), Building Materials and Technology Promotion Council (BMTPC), and other
relevant public authorities.

Where required, IFSCA may facilitate institutional coordination and liaison with such agencies
to enable efficient access to reliable and standardised datasets, while ensuring appropriate
data-sharing, confidentiality and usage protocols.

The development of the ILS ecosystem in GIFT-IFSC may follow a phased approach, with
catastrophe bonds serving as the initial and principal product around which the market
infrastructure, expertise and regulatory capacity are developed. Once the foundational
ecosystem has matured, the scope may progressively be expanded to other forms of
insurance-linked and alternative risk-transfer structures, including reinsurance sidecars,
collateralised reinsurance and other sophisticated ART vehicles. This approach would allow
market participants and regulators to build experience progressively while establishing the
specialised service providers, modelling capabilities, legal expertise, transaction-
management infrastructure and investor base required for a deeper ILS market.

In parallel, GIFT-IFSC should ensure that ILS issued from the jurisdiction have efficient
access to internationally accepted clearing, settlement and custody infrastructure used by
global ILS investors. Connectivity with established international securities infrastructure and
custodians, including systems such as Euroclear, Clearstream and Depository Trust
Company (DTC), would facilitate participation by international institutional investors, reduce
operational friction in cross-border transactions and enhance the attractiveness and credibility
of GIFT-IFSC as an ILS issuance and investment centre. The regulatory and market

                                                                                                                                 Page 40 of 41
infrastructure framework should therefore seek to ensure that ILS securities can be issued,
held, settled and transferred through internationally familiar mechanisms, subject to
applicable regulatory and investor-eligibility requirements.
10. Conclusion
The recommendations contained in this report provide a roadmap for establishing an
internationally credible ILS framework in GIFT IFSC. The proposed approach is firmly
grounded in comparative international practice, consistent with the supervisory principles of
the IAIS and supported by a clear legal and prudential rationale for distinguishing fully
collateralised SPIs from conventional insurance companies. Such a framework would not only
integrate India into the global ILS market but also contribute to strengthening the country's
long-term financial resilience against increasingly frequent and severe natural catastrophes.
