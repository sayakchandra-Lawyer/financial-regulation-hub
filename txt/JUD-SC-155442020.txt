Facts

7.     Relevant facts may be briefly noted.

8.     Appellant is the proprietor of M/s Nikhra Warehouse

situated at Dabra in the district of Gwalior, Madhya Pradesh.

The warehouse was used to store agricultural produce of

farmers for which consideration was charged and paid. The

agricultural produce stored was hypothecated with the State

Bank of India, Dabra (SBI). For such hypothecation, it was

mandatory for the appellant to insure the stock of agricultural

produce stored in the warehouse. To insure the warehouse

building as well as the stock lying therein, appellant obtained

from the respondents Insurance Policy No. 1001/0066099 for

the period from 13.12.2006 to 12.04.2007 for a sum of

Rs. 50,00,000.00 covering the warehouse building and for a

further sum of Rs. 1,50,00,000.00 in respect of the stock lying

in the warehouse. Appellant had obtained another insurance

policy of equivalent amount for burglary.

9.     On 13.03.2007, at about 05:00 pm, Dabra and its

adjoining areas experienced a severe hailstorm followed by

       4
heavy rainfall. Because of the hailstorm, the asbestos sheet
roofing of the warehouse suffered extensive damage resulting in
rainwater seeping into the warehouse premises. Not only the
roofing was damaged extensively, but a part of the agricultural
produce stored in the warehouse got damaged by the rainwater.
As per the stock register maintained by the warehouse,
agricultural produce valued at Rs. 1,50,00,000.00 were stored
in the warehouse at the time of the incident. Appellant engaged
the services of a valuer M.L. Gupta who quantified the loss to
the agricultural produce at Rs. 36,83,955.00 and the damage
to the warehouse building at Rs. 6,41,268.00.

10.  The incident was reported to the respondents who

appointed M/s Cunningham Lindsey International Pvt. Ltd. as

the surveyor to assess the damage.

11.  Appellant also brought the matter to the notice of the

local revenue authorities. The Tehsildar assessed the loss

caused to the agricultural produce at Rs. 36,83,955.00 and the

damage to the warehouse building at Rs. 6,41,268.00.

agricultural produce stored in the warehouse were concerned,

the surveyor opined that the same belonged to third-parties and

were held by the appellant in trust. Therefore, the agricultural

produce which suffered damage were not covered under the

insurance policy in view of the General Exclusion Clause 5.

13.     At that stage, appellant instituted Consumer

Complaint No. 16 of 2007 before the State Commission seeking

total compensation of Rs. 50,45,223.00 with interest. The

complaint was resisted by the respondents who took the stand

that the agricultural produce stored in the warehouse and

which suffered damage were held in trust by the appellant as

those belonged to third-parties. As per the exclusion clause,

insurance claim did not cover the said damaged agricultural

stock.

         i. whether the complainant was entitled to be

15.      On the first issue, the State Commission held that

Commission held that the appellant was entitled to

compensation to that extent.

16.      In so far the second issue is concerned, the State

Commission considered the claim in respect of the agricultural

produce stored in the warehouse. It took note of the assessment

made by the Tehsildar quantifying the loss at Rs. 36,83,955.00

         7
payment of a special premium was not accepted. The State

Rs. 1,50,00,000.00 for burglary covering all the stock and
another policy for the warehouse building and stocks for
Rs. 1,50,00,000.00 (sic). The two insurance policies were not
obtained in the abstract which indicated the insurer’s
awareness of the nature of the property sought to be insured,
further observing that issuance of the two policies indicated
that the insurance arrangement extended to the warehouse and
to the stock therein. The State Commission held that the
appellant had an insurable interest in the agricultural produce
stored in the warehouse and that the loss sustained was
recoverable under the insurance policy. Consequently, the State
Commission allowed the complaint and directed the
respondents to pay Rs. 36,83,955.00 towards the damaged
agricultural produce and Rs. 6,41,268.00 for the damage to the
warehouse building, total compensation being Rs. 43,25,223.00
with interest at the rate of 8% per annum from the date of filing
of the complaint vide the order dated 22.07.2011.

Analysis

21.        Submissions made by learned counsel for the parties

have received the due consideration of the Court. The

judgments cited at the Bar have also been carefully perused and

analyzed.

22.        There is no dispute as to the facts. It is admitted that

there was a hailstorm followed by heavy rains on 13.03.2007 as

a result of which the warehouse and the agricultural stock

stored therein suffered extensive damage. There is also no

dispute as to the holding (existence) of insurance policy by the

appellant when the aforesaid occurrence took place. The

controversy boils down to applicability of the General Exclusion

Clause 5 to the agricultural produce stored in the warehouse,

the nature of the relationship between the appellant and the

owners of the agricultural produce, and the extent of the loss

           20
for which indemnification is payable under the insurance
policy.

23.       Therefore, the question which falls for our consideration

is whether the agricultural produce belonging to the farmers and

stored in the appellant’s warehouse should be taken as ‘goods held

in trust’ or ‘on commission’ within the meaning of the General

Exclusion Clause 5? Or, whether the relationship between the

appellant and the owners of such agricultural goods was one of

bailor and bailee arising out of a contractual bailment?

Corollary to the above question is, whether upon a proper

construction of the insurance policy, including the agreed bank

clause, the respondents-insurer are liable to indemnify the loss

suffered on account of the damage to the stored agricultural

produce?

24.       Before proceeding further, it would be useful to

examine the nature of an insurance contract or how an

insurance contract is to be interpreted. In Vikram Greentech

India Limited Vs. New India Assurance Company Limited8, a two-

8 (2009) 5 SCC 599

                                                         21
Judge Bench of this Court examined the nature of an insurance
contract and how its terms are to be interpreted in the following
manner:

       16. An insurance contract is a species of commercial
       transactions and must be construed like any other
       contract to its own terms and by itself. In a contract of
       insurance, there is requirement of uberrima fides i.e.
       good faith on the part of the insured. Except that, in other
       respects, there is no difference between a contract of
       insurance and any other contract.

       17. The four essentials of a contract of insurance are : (i)
       the definition of the risk, (ii) the duration of the risk, (iii)
       the premium, and (iv) the amount of insurance. Since
       upon issuance of the insurance policy, the insurer
       undertakes to indemnify the loss suffered by the insured
       on account of the risks covered by the insurance policy,
       its terms have to be strictly construed to determine the
       extent of liability of the insurer.

24.1.  Thus, what can be deduced from the above is that an

insurance contract is a species of commercial transactions. It

must be construed like any other contract having regard to its

terms and conditions. It is based on uberrima fides i.e. good

faith on the part of the insured. While the four essentials of an

insurance contract are: i) definition of the risk; ii) duration of

       22
the risk; iii) premium; and iv) amount of insurance, the terms
of the insurance policy would have to be strictly construed to
determine the extent of liability of the insurer.

24.2.  Proceeding further, this Court in Greentech opined

that the endeavour of the Court should always be to interpret

the words in which the contract is expressed by the parties.

Referring to earlier decisions of this Court, the Bench held that

the Court while construing the terms of a policy is not expected

to venture into extra liberalism that may result in re-writing the

contract or substituting the terms which were not intended by

the parties. The insured cannot claim anything more than what

is covered by the insurance policy. This Court held thus:

       18. The endeavour of the court must always be to
       interpret the words in which the contract is expressed by
       the parties. The court while construing the terms of policy
       is not expected to venture into extra liberalism that may
       result in rewriting the contract or substituting the terms
       which were not intended by the parties. The insured
       cannot claim anything more than what is covered by the
       insurance policy.

       23
25.  What follows is that while interpreting an insurance

contract, it has to be borne in mind that the rights and

obligations of the parties to the said contract i.e. the insurer

and the insured would have to be determined from the terms

and conditions of the policy itself. The policy has to be read as

a whole having regard to the nature of the insurance cover and

the purpose for which it was obtained. The task of the court is

neither to enlarge the contract by resorting to equitable

considerations nor to restrict its meaning by an isolated or

excessively literal reading of one or few of its stipulations. The

words of the policy must, therefore, be understood in their

proper setting so as to give meaningful effect to the contractual

undertaking of the parties.

26.  The principles governing interpretation of insurance

policies were further elucidated in Canara Bank. After referring

to earlier decisions of this Court, the Bench held as under:

     22. The principles relating to interpretation of insurance
     policies are well settled and not in dispute. At the same
     time, the provisions of the policy must be read and
     interpreted in such a manner so as to give effect to the
     reasonable expectations of all the parties including the

                             24
       insured and the beneficiaries. It is also well settled that
       coverage provisions should be interpreted broadly and if
       there is any ambiguity, the same should be resolved in
       favour of the insured. On the other hand, the exclusion
       clauses must be read narrowly. The policy and its
       components must be read as a whole and given a
       meaning which furthers the expectations of the parties
       and also the business realities. According to us, the
       entire policy should be understood and examined in such
       a manner and when that is done, the interpretation
       becomes a commercially sensible interpretation.

26.1.  Thus, what this decision tells us is that provisions of

an insurance policy must be read and interpreted in such

a manner so as to give effect to the reasonable expectations of

all the parties including the insured and the beneficiaries.

Coverage provisions should be interpreted broadly. If there is

any ambiguity, the same should be resolved in favour of the

insured. In so far an exclusion clause is concerned, it must be

read narrowly. The policy and its components must be read as

a whole and given a meaning which furthers the expectations of

the parties and also the business realities.

       25
27.  This Court in Shivram Chandra Jagarnath Cold

Storage Vs. New India Assurance Company Limited9, after

referring to earlier decisions of this Court, held that an

insurance policy must be construed only with reference to its

stipulations and no artificial meaning can be given to the words

of the policy. The exceptions to an insurance policy must be

construed strictly since they reflect the agreement between the

parties with respect to the losses that are covered by the

insurance policy. Any departure from this principle is possible

only if the terms of the policy are ambiguous or unclear. In the

event, two constructions are possible or if there is an ambiguity,

a construction that is beneficial to the insured should be

adopted consistent with the purpose of the policy. Another

instance where exception clauses may be interpreted to the

benefit of the insured is when the exception clauses are too wide

and not consistent with the main purpose or object of the

insurance policy. When the option is between taking a view

which will relieve the distress and agony of the victims or their

dependents on one hand and the equally plausible view which

9 (2022) 4 SCC 539

                                                         26
will reduce the profitability of the insurer in regard to the
occupational hazard undertaken by him by way of business
activity, there is hardly any choice. The court cannot but opt for
the former view. Even if one were to take a strict doctrinaire
approach, the very same conclusion would emerge by applying
the doctrine of ‘reading down’ the exclusion clause in the light
of the main purpose of the insurance contract so that the
exclusion clause does not cross swords with the main purpose.
The effort must be to harmonise the two instead of allowing the
exclusion clause to snipe successfully at the main purpose.

28.  Thus, what can be culled out from the above decision

is that while an exclusionary clause should not be disregarded

merely because it operates against the insurer, the task is to

ascertain the exact scope of the exclusion not only from the

language employed in the exclusion clause but from a

wholesome reading of the insurance policy itself. The exceptions

to an insurance policy has to be construed strictly. Where the

language admits of more than one interpretation, the construction which

is favourable to the insured should be preferred. It must always be borne

     27
in mind that an exclusion cannot be applied in a manner which defeats
the principal object of the insurance policy.

29.    In Texco Marketing Private Limited Vs. Tata AIG

General Insurance Company Limited10, a two-Judge Bench of

this Court examined as to how an exclusion clause in a contract

of insurance has to be interpretated and held as under:

       12. An exclusion clause in a contract of insurance has to
       be interpreted differently. Not only the onus but also the
       burden lies with the insurer when reliance is made on
       such a clause. This is for the reason that insurance
       contracts are special contracts premised on the notion of
       good faith. It is not a leverage or a safeguard for the
       insurer, but is meant to be pressed into service on a contingency,
       being a contract of speculation. An insurance contract by its very
       nature mandates disclosure of all material facts by both the parties.

29.1.  The Bench expressed the view that an exclusion

clause in a contract of insurance has to be interpreted

differently. If an insurer relies upon an exclusion clause, the

burden of establishing that such an exclusionary clause applies

to a claim rests upon the insurer. The significance of the

aforesaid principle lies in the fact that an insurer cannot escape

10 (2023) 1 SCC 428

                                                         28
liability merely by pointing out to the existence of an
exclusionary provision and placing reliance thereon. The
exclusion must be shown to govern the particular loss for which
indemnification is claimed. The responsibility of establishing
that the goods fall within the exclusion is on the insurer when
it places reliance on such an exclusionary clause, particularly
when effect of such exclusion is to deny the insurer the benefit
of the insurance coverage.

29.2.  Texco Marketing also examined the manner in which

an exclusionary provision has to be interpreted vis-à-vis

provisions of the principal contract which the Bench explained

in the following manner:

       13. An exclusion clause has to be understood on the
       touchstone of the doctrine of reading down in the light of
       the underlining object and intendment of the contract. It
       can never be understood to mean to be in conflict with
       the main purpose for which the contract is entered. A
       party, who relies upon it, shall not be the one who
       committed an act of fraud, coercion or misrepresentation,
       particularly when the contract along with the exclusion
       clause is introduced by it. Such a clause has to be
       understood on the prism of the main contract. The main
       contract once signed would eclipse the offending

                          29
     exclusion clause when it would otherwise be impossible
     to execute it. A clause or a term is a limb, which has got
     no existence outside, as such, it exists and vanishes
     along with the contract, having no independent life of its
     own. It has got no ability to destroy its own creator i.e.
     the main contract. When it is destructive to the main
     contract, right at its inception, it has to be severed, being
     a conscious exclusion, though brought either inadvertently or
     consciously by the party who introduced it. The doctrine of waiver,
     acquiescence, approbate and reprobate, and estoppel would
     certainly come into operation as considered by this Court in Union
     of India v. N. Murugesan.

30.  What emerges from Texco Marketing is that an

exclusionary provision cannot be isolated from the contract of

which it forms a part. Such a clause derives its meaning from

the policy and must operate consistently with the object and

intendment for which the parties have contracted for. The mere

fact that the insurer has incorporated an exclusion does not by

itself permit the exclusion to be construed in a manner which

defeats the very purpose of the policy. For this reason, the policy

as a whole has to be read so as to ensure that there is no conflict

between the exclusionary clause and the principal contract.

Both must align on the same trajectory.

     30
31.      Let us now turn to the terms of the insurance policy in the

present case. The policy in question is the Standard Fire and Special Perils

Policy, bearing policy No. 1001/0066099 issued by the respondent-

insurer in favour of Nikhra Warehouse. The insurance period commenced

on 13.12.2006 and continued till midnight of 12.04.2007. The incident in

question took place on 13.03.2007 and, therefore, fell within the insurance

period.

32.      The insuring clause of the Standard Fire and Special

Perils Policy provides for indemnification of the insured in

respect of loss or damage to the insured property on the

happening of any of the perils specified thereunder during the

period of insurance, which includes loss, destruction or damage

caused by storm, cyclone, typhoon, flood, inundation etc. The

liability of the insurer has to be determined with reference to

the insuring clause as well as to the schedule, conditions,

exclusions and endorsements forming part of the policy.

33.      A perusal of the schedule together with Annexure-B

shows that the policy covered both the warehouse building and

the stocks stored at the insured premises i.e. the warehouse.

The sum insured in respect of the building was Rs. 50,00,000.00 and in

         31
respect of the stock, it was Rs. 1,50,00,000.00, making the aggregate sum
insured at Rs. 2,00,00,000.00. The schedule also refers to the
hypothecation details as per Annexure-A and makes the policy subject to
clauses FA, FC, FG and FH attached thereto.

34.  The National Commission proceeded on the basis

that the sum insured in respect of the stock was

Rs. 1,00,00,000.00. However, Annexure-B to the policy shows

that the sum insured under this head was Rs. 1,50,00,000.00

which figure is also reflected in the counter affidavit filed by the

respondents before this Court. Therefore, the sum insured in

respect of the stock stored in the warehouse was Rs.

1,50,00,000.00 and not Rs. 1,00,00,000.00.

35.  There is another aspect of Annexure-B which needs

to be taken note of. It makes a separate mention of ‘stocks’ and

‘goods held in trust’. While ‘stocks’ are covered for a sum of

Rs. 1,50,00,000.00, the sum insured against ‘goods held in

trust’ is shown as nil. It is clear that the two expressions ‘stocks’

and ‘goods held in trust’ have thus been separately dealt with

in the policy.

                32
36.    Let us now turn to the General Exclusion Clause 5 of

the Standard Fire and Special Perils Policy, which reads thus:

       5. Loss, destruction or damage to bullion or unset precious
       stones, any curios or works of art for an amount exceeding
       Rs.10,000/-, goods held in trust or on commission,
       manuscripts, plans, drawings, securities, obligations or
       documents of any kind, stamps, coins or paper money,
       cheques, books of accounts or other business books,
       computer system records, explosives unless otherwise
       expressly stated in the policy.

36.1.  A reading of General Exclusion Clause 5 would

indicate that goods held in trust or on commission would stand

excluded from the insurance coverage unless otherwise

expressly stated in the policy.

37.    The National Commission relied upon the expression

‘goods held in trust or on commission’ appearing in the

aforesaid clause and held that the agricultural produce

belonging to the farmers were excluded from the insurance

cover. While doing so, the National Commission placed

considerable emphasis upon the fact that those goods did not

       33
belong to the appellant and held that the goods were held in
trust for which no premium was paid.

38.  However, as noticed supra, the exclusion clause does

not exclude all goods belonging to third parties. It specifically

refers to ‘goods held in trust or on commission’.

39.  We may now refer to clause FA in the schedule to the

policy. The schedule to the policy expressly makes the policy

subject to clauses FA, FC, FG and FH. Clause FA is the agreed

bank clause which has also been mentioned in the report of the

surveyor appointed by the respondents i.e. M/s Cunningham

Lindsey International Pvt. Ltd. Sum and substance of the

agreed bank clause is that upon any monies becoming payable

under the policy, the same shall be paid by the company

(respondents herein) to the bank (SBI herein) and such part of

any monies so paid as may relate to the interests of other

parties insured under the policy shall be received by the bank

(SBI herein) as agents for such other parties.

40.  It is the admitted position that the stocks lying in the

warehouse were hypothecated with the SBI, which mandated

the stocks to be insured. Respondents were aware of such

     34
hypothecation when the policy was issued. In the written
submissions before the State Commission, respondents had
stated that the agricultural produce belonging to various farmers were
stored in the warehouse and that the appellant charged fees from them for
such storage. Thus, the hypothecation of the stocks with the SBI
alongwith reference to clause FA in the policy schedule which is
the agreed bank clause are required to be taken into
consideration.

41.  That apart, the General Exclusion Clause 5 cannot

be considered in isolation. Annexure-B separately identifies

‘stocks’ and ‘goods held in trust’ providing an insurance cover

of Rs. 1,50,00,000.00 for the former and showing ‘nil’ against

the latter. The schedule also makes the policy subject to clause

FA identified by the respondent’s own surveyor as the agreed

bank clause. Further, General Exclusion Clause 5 itself

concludes with the expression ‘unless otherwise expressly

stated in the policy’. Therefore, the general exclusion clause has

to be read in the context of the overall policy including the

agreed bank clause. The agreed bank clause cannot be

construed de hors the policy. Similarly, the exclusion cannot be

     35
applied merely because the goods belonged to persons other
than the appellant.

42.        This boils down to the decisive question as to whether the

goods were held in trust or on commission or whether those were received

and stored in the warehouse under a contractual arrangement of

bailment?

43.        The National Commission while rejecting the claim of

the appellant in respect of the stock, proceeded substantially on

the basis that the goods belonged to farmers other than the

appellant and, therefore, the goods were held in trust within the

meaning of the General Exclusion Clause 5. In our view, such

an approach did not sufficiently examine the nature of the legal

relationship under which the goods were stored in the

warehouse. The National Commission ought to have examined

whether the goods were held in trust in the legal sense as

contemplated by the policy instead of treating third party

ownership as sufficient to attract the exclusion. The distinction

drawn in Annexure-B between ‘stocks’ and ‘goods held in trust’,

the reference to clause FA in the schedule, the effect of the

agreed bank clause and the express language of the General

           36
Exclusion Clause 5 were all relevant factors which escaped
consideration by the National Commission.

44.  The aspect regarding payment of additional premium

of Rs. 1500.00 also needs to be addressed. While appellant at

one point of time had contended that additional premium of Rs.

1500.00 was paid for the ‘goods held in trust’, respondents

denied receipt of any such premium which stand was accepted

by the National Commission. The material placed before this

Court indicates that the said amount related to an earlier policy

issued by the United India Insurance Company Limited and not

related to the present policy issued by the respondents for the

relevant period. Be that as it may, this disputed issue of

payment or non-payment of additional premium would pale into

insignificance in the teeth of the principal question which has

emerged in this case, which is, whether the goods were received

and stored in the warehouse under a contract of bailment?

45.  We may now examine the nature of the relationship

between the appellant and the farmers whose agricultural

produce were stored in the warehouse. The question is whether

such goods were held by the appellant in trust as contended by

     37
the respondents and accepted by the National Commission or
whether those goods were received and stored by the appellant
in the warehouse under a contractual arrangement of bailment?

46.     In this regard, we may refer to Section 148 of the

Indian Contract Act, 1872, which defines bailment, bailor and

bailee. Section 148 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 reads as

under:

        148. 'Bailment', 'bailor' and 'bailee' defined.—

        A 'bailment' is the delivery of goods by one person to
        another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall,
        when the purpose is accomplished, be returned or
        otherwise disposed of according to the directions of the
        person delivering them. The person delivering the goods is
        called the 'bailor'. The person to whom they are delivered
        is called the 'bailee'.

46.1.   As per the aforesaid provision, a bailment is the

delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose

upon a contract that when the purpose for which those goods

were received is accomplished, those shall be returned or

otherwise disposed of as per the directions of the person

delivering the goods. The person delivering the goods is called

        38
the bailor and the person to whom the goods are delivered is
called the bailee.

47.  Thus, the essential elements of bailment are the delivery of

goods by one person to another; such delivery being for some purpose; and

following a contract that upon accomplishment of such purpose the goods

would be returned or otherwise disposed of according to the directions of

the person delivering the goods. Such delivery of possession of goods

does not result in transfer of ownership in the goods. The bailee

obtains possession of the goods for the purpose contemplated

by the contract and has corresponding obligation towards the

bailor. Therefore, whether a particular arrangement constitutes

bailment or not, has to be determined with reference to the

substance of the contract and the obligations of the parties.

48.  The question whether goods belonging to farmers and

kept in a cold storage would fall within the expression ‘goods

held in trust or on commission’ was examined by this Court in

Canara Bank. In that case, the insurance company had taken

the stand that since the goods were held in trust by the cold

storage, the exclusion clause would operate and, therefore, no

liability could be fastened onto the insurance company. This

     39
Court rejected the said contention of the insurance company. It
was noticed by the Court that the farmers had agreed to pay
rent for keeping their produce in the cold storage and that the
goods were delivered for possession of the cold storage under a
contractual arrangement. The relationship between the farmers
and the cold storage was, therefore, one of bailor and bailee,
with the cold storage holding the produce as bailee for
consideration. This Court held thus:

     35. In the present case, as pointed out above, the farmer
     had agreed to pay consideration to the cold store and,
     therefore, the goods were not held in trust per se but the
     goods were held by cold store as bailee of the goods for
     consideration. The possession of the farm produce was
     handed over by the bailor i.e. farmer to the cold store i.e.
     the bailee, in terms of the contract. There may be inter se
     rights and liabilities between the farmer and the cold
     store but it cannot be said that the goods were held ‘in
     trust’. The goods were also not held ‘on commission’. No
     commission was payable and only rental was paid.
     Therefore, we reject this argument on behalf of the
     Insurance Company.

49.  The decision in Canara Bank makes it clear that the

mere fact that goods belonging to another person are kept in the

custody of a warehouse or cold storage would not mean that the

     40
‘goods were held in trust or on commission’. What is required to
be examined is the nature of the arrangement under which the
goods were received and the capacity in which those were
stored. In Canara Bank, this Court noticed that the farmers had
delivered their produce to the cold storage against payment of
rent; thus, the relationship was one of bailor and bailee.

50.  Applying the above principles to the facts of the

present case, there is no doubt that the agricultural produce

belonging to the farmers were stored in the appellant’s

warehouse against payment of storage charges. The purpose of

such delivery was storage and safe custody of the produce. The

warehouse was required to retain the goods for the purpose for

which those were delivered and thereafter to release or deal with

such goods in accordance with the directions of the farmers who

had handed over the goods. Such an arrangement is consistent

with the essential elements of bailment under Section 148 of

the Indian Contract Act, 1872. The fact that ownership of the

produce continued to vest in the farmers does not alter the

character of the transaction. On the materials on record, it is

evident that the relationship between the appellant and the

     41
owners of the agricultural produce was that of a bailee and
bailor and not one of trustee and beneficiary or agent and
principal holding goods on commission.

51.  In the present case, appellant is the proprietor of

Nikhra Warehouse. Agricultural produce belonging to different

farmers were stored in the warehouse. Respondents in the

written submission filed before the State Commission admitted

that the warehouse stored goods belonging to different farmers

for which the appellant charged fees from the farmers. It thus

establishes that the goods were not merely kept in the

warehouse gratuitously or in some informal arrangement but

were received in the course of the warehouse’s business against

payment of storage charges.

52.  Ownership of the goods is therefore not determinative

of the existence of an insurable interest. A bailee may have an

insurable interest in the goods entrusted to its custody by

reason of the possession and responsibilities undertaken in

respect of the bailment. The appellant, being the proprietor of

the warehouse in which the agricultural produce were stored

for consideration, had a legally cognizable interest in the

     42
preservation of the goods and in the performance of the
obligations arising from the storage arrangement. The fact that
the goods belonged to the farmers would not by itself deny the
appellant of an insurable interest in the goods or render the
insurance cover in respect of the stored stock redundant.

53.  Respondents have relied upon to a great extent on

the expression ‘goods held in trust’ used in the consumer

complaint filed by the appellant himself. While it is true that at

the initial stage, appellant had described the goods stored in the

warehouse as ‘goods held in trust’, but according to us, that by

itself, may not be determinative of the nature of the goods stored

in the warehouse or the character of the relationship between

the owner of the warehouse i.e. the appellant and the farmers

who had stored their agricultural produce in the warehouse on

payment of charges. For a proper appreciation, the complaint has

to be read as a whole and the nature of the transaction has to be

examined in the light of the pleadings, the documents on record,

including the policy, and the surrounding circumstances. Mere

use of the expression ‘held in trust’ in the complaint would not

constitute an admission that the agricultural produce of

     43
different farmers stored in the warehouse were held in trust
when the materials on record clearly show otherwise.

54.  The question of insurable interest also brings into

focus the position of the farmers whose agricultural produce

were stored in the warehouse. At the relevant point of time, the

appropriate legislation was the Consumer Protection Act, 1986.

We may refer to Section 2(1)(d) of the Consumer Protection Act,

1986 which defines the expression ‘consumer’. Section 2(1)(d)

reads as follows:

     2. Definitions. — (1) In this Act, unless the context
     otherwise requires, —
     (d) ‘consumer’ means any person who, —
     (i) buys any goods for a consideration which has been paid
     or promised or partly paid and partly promised, or under
     any system of deferred payment and includes any user of
     such goods other than the person who buys such goods
     for consideration paid or promised or partly paid or partly
     promised, or under any system of deferred payment, when
     such use is made with the approval of such person, but
     does not include a person who obtains such goods for
     resale or for any commercial purpose; or
     (ii) hires or avails of any services for a consideration which
     has been paid or promised or partly paid and partly
     promised, or under any system of deferred payment and

                   44
       includes any beneficiary of such services other than the
       person who hires or avails of the services for consideration
       paid or promised, or partly paid and partly promised, or
       under any system of deferred payment, when such services
       are availed of with the approval of the first mentioned
       person but does not include a person who avails of such
       services for any commercial purpose.

55.    The scope of the expression ‘consumer’ as defined

under the Consumer Protection Act, 1986, particularly with

reference to persons who are beneficiaries of an insurance

policy without being parties to the contract of insurance, came

up for consideration before this Court in Canara Bank. This

Court explained the width of the definition of ‘consumer’ under

the Consumer Protection Act, 1986 in the following manner:

       27. The definition of ‘consumer’ under the Act is very wide
       and it not only includes the person who hires or avails of
       the services for consideration but also includes the
       beneficiary of such services who may be a person other
       than the person who hires or avails of services.

55.1.  In so far the Consumer Protection Act is concerned,

it is not necessary that there should be a privity of contract

between the insurance company and the beneficiaries. The

       45
definition of consumer is wide enough to include not only the

person who has hired or availed of the services but also includes

any beneficiary of such services. It is not necessary that those

beneficiaries should be parties to the contract of insurance.

They are consumers not because they are parties to the contract

of insurance but because they are the beneficiaries of the policy

taken out by the insured. Relevant portion of the judgment in

Canara Bank is extracted hereunder:

                28. Taking the issue of privity of contract, we are of the
                considered view that as far as the Act is concerned, it is
                not necessary that there should be privity of contract
                between the Insurance Company and the claimants. The
                definition of ‘consumer’ under Section 2(d) quoted
                hereinabove is in two parts. Sub-clause (i) of Section
                2(1)(d) deals with a person who buys any goods and
                includes any user of such goods other than the person
                who buys such goods as long as the use is made with the
                approval of such person. Therefore, the definition of
                consumer even in the first part not only includes the
                person who has purchased but includes any user of the
                goods so long as such user is made with the approval of
                the person who has purchased the goods. As far as the
                definition of ‘consumer’ in relation to hiring or availing of
                services is concerned, the definition, in our view, is much
                wider. In this part of the section, consumer includes not

                                                46
     only the person who has hired or availed of the services
     but also includes any beneficiary of such services.
     Therefore, an insured could be a person who hires or
     avails of the services of the Insurance Company but there
     could be many other persons who could be the
     beneficiaries of the services. It is not necessary that those
     beneficiaries should be parties to the contract of
     insurance. They are the consumers not because they are
     parties to the contract of insurance but because they are
     the beneficiaries of the policy taken out by the insured.

56.  Let us now examine the report of the surveyor

appointed by the insurer. However, before adverting to the report,

it would be necessary to examine the statutory provisions

governing assessment of insurance claim by an approved surveyor.

57.  For this purpose, let us examine Section 64UM of the

Insurance Act, 1938 which deals with licensing of surveyors and

loss assessors. Sub-section (2) of Section 64UM, as it stood at

the relevant time, is relevant which reads as follows:

     64-UM. Licensing of surveyors and loss assessors–

     (1) *  *      *  *                                 *

     (2) No claim in respect of a loss which has occurred in
     India and requiring to be paid or settled in India equal
     to or exceeding twenty thousand rupees in value on any

               47
       policy of insurance, arising or intimated to an insurer at
       any time after the expiry of a period of one year from the
       commencement of the Insurance (Amend-ment) Act,
       1968, shall, unless otherwise directed by the Authority,
       be admitted for payment or settled by the insurer unless
       he has obtained a report, on the loss that has occurred,
       from a person who holds a licence issued under this
       section to act as a surveyor or loss assessor (hereafter
       referred to as ‘approved surveyor or loss assessor’):

       Provided that nothing in this sub-section shall be deemed
       to take away or abridge the right of the insurer to pay or
       settle any claim at any amount different from the amount
       assessed by the approved surveyor or loss assessor.

       *      *  *  *

57.1.  From a careful reading of the aforesaid provision, it

is evident that where the claim in respect of a loss is of the

prescribed value or above, assessment by an approved surveyor

or loss assessor is a statutory requirement before the claim is

admitted for payment or settled by the insurer. The proviso,

however, makes it clear that the insurer is not bound to settle

the claim at the amount assessed by the approved surveyor or

by the loss assessor. This provision, therefore, recognizes the

          48
importance of the surveyor’s assessment without making it
conclusive of the amount payable under the policy.

58.  The scope of Section 64UM(2) of the Insurance Act,

1938 and the evidentiary value of the surveyor’s report came up

for consideration before this Court in New India Assurance Co.

Ltd. Vs. Pradeep Kumar.11 This Court held that although the

assessment of loss by an approved surveyor is a pre-requisite

for payment or settlement of a claim of the prescribed value, the

surveyor’s report is not the last and final word. It may be the

basis or foundation for settlement of a claim but such a report

is neither binding upon the insurer or the insured. In an

appropriate case, it may be departed from where the materials

on record warrant such a course.

59.  In National Insurance Co. Ltd. Vs. M/s Hareshwar

Enterprises (P) Ltd.12, this Court reiterated the proposition that

while assessment of loss by an approved surveyor is a pre-

requisite for settlement of an insurance claim, the same would

11 (2009) 7 SCC 787
12 (2021) 17 SCC 682

                                                         49
however not be the last and final word. This Court observed as
under:

     14. … it is no doubt held that though the assessment of loss
     by an approved surveyor is a prerequisite for payment or
     settlement of the claim, the surveyor report is not the last
     and final word. It is not that sacrosanct that it cannot be
     departed from and it is not conclusive. The approved
     surveyor’s report may be the basis or foundation for
     settlement of a claim by the insurer in respect of loss
     suffered by insured but such report is neither binding upon
     the insurer nor insured. On the said proposition, we are
     certain that there can be no quarrel. The surveyor’s report
     certainly can be taken note as a piece of evidence until more
     reliable evidence is brought on record to rebut the contents
     of the surveyor’s report.

60.  What emerges from the above analysis is that the

report of an approved surveyor, though relevant for the

assessment of an insurance claim, is neither conclusive nor

binding upon the insurer or the insured. Discretion is vested

upon the insurer and the insured to make a departure

therefrom if more reliable evidence is brought on record.

Assessment made by the surveyor must therefore be considered

in the light of the overall materials available on record. In this

     50
regard, we may usefully extract the observations made by this
Court in M/s Hareshwar Enterprises (P) Ltd.:

     17. Having noted the said decisions, we are of the opinion that
     the same cannot alter the position in the instant case. On the
     proposition of law that the surveyor’s report cannot be
     considered as a sacrosanct document and that if there is any
     contrary evidence including investigation report, opportunity
     should be available to produce it as rebuttal material, we
     concur. However, the issue to be noted is as to whether the
     surveyor’s report in the instant case adverts to the
     consideration of stock position in an appropriate manner and
     in that circumstance whether an investigation report which is
     based on investigation that was started belatedly should take
     the centre stage. The fact remains that the surveyors report is
     the basic document which has statutory recognition and can
     be made the basis if it inspires the confidence of the
     adjudicating forum and if such forum does not find the need to
     place reliance on any other material, in the facts and
     circumstance arising in the case.

61.  While on surveyor’s report, we may also refer to a

decision of this Court in New India Assurance Co. Ltd. Vs. Mudit

Roadways13. This decision concerns a fire incident in a

warehouse and the competing reports regarding the cause of

13 (2024) 3 SCC 193

                                                         51
fire. While considering the evidentiary value of the surveyor’s
report, this Court observed thus:

     46. Guided by the above ratio, the situation in the present
     case is found to be similar. The surveyor’s report cannot be
     considered a sacred document and contrary evidence,
     including an investigation report, is subject to rebuttal.

62.  The aforesaid observation of this Court came in the

context of the controversy in Mudit Roadways. The principal

dispute therein related to the cause of the fire and the differing

conclusions recorded by the different agencies.

63.  In so far the present case is concerned, there is no

comparable controversy regarding the occurrence of hailstorm

and the damage to the warehouse and to the stock stored

therein occasioned thereby. There is no dispute to the incident

of hailstorm and the damage caused thereby. The dispute is

relating to the manner in which the claims under the two heads

were considered. The decision in Mudit Roadways reiterates the

principle that a surveyor’s report is open to rebuttal; the actual

assessment must necessarily depend upon the nature of the

dispute and the material available in each case.

     52
64.     Let us now examine the report dated 16.07.2007

submitted by M/s Cunningham Lindsey International Pvt. Ltd.

The report is in two parts, the first part deals with the damage

to the warehouse building whereas the second part deals with

liability of the insurance company to indemnify the loss for the

damaged agricultural produce stored in the warehouse. While

the surveyor’s report quantified the damage to the warehouse

building, it declined to assess the damage to the stock stored in

the warehouse on the ground that the goods belonged to third

parties and were held in trust. Therefore, in so far the claim

relating to agricultural produce is concerned, the surveyor’s

report does not disclose an assessment of the actual loss

suffered. The claim was declined on the basis of the surveyor’s

understanding of the policy exclusion relating to goods held in

trust.

65.     As regards the agricultural produce belonging to the

farmers and stored in the warehouse against payment of storage

charges are concerned, we have already held that the relationship

between the appellant and the owners of the produce was one of

bailment and not one of trust or commission. General Exclusion

        53
Clause 5, which refers to goods held in trust or on commission,
could not have been applied merely on the basis of third party
ownership. The principles explained by this Court in Canara
Bank have a direct bearing on the controversy before us.
Applying the same to the facts of the present case, we are unable
to sustain the view taken by the National Commission that the
damaged agricultural produce stood excluded from the
insurance coverage.

66.  Ordinarily, in such a case, we would have remitted

the matter back to the National Commission for an assessment

of the loss to the stock. But having regard to the fact that the

occurrence took place in the year 2007 with the appellant

instituting the complaint in 2007 itself which is more than 19

years ago, we are not inclined to remit the matter back to the

National Commission at this distant point of time. Moreover, the

damaged agricultural produce won’t be around for the surveyor

to make any assessment of the damage suffered.

67.  Assessment by the appellant’s surveyor and by the

revenue authorities are on record which can be looked into by

us since we are at the stage of appeal. All that we need to

     54
examine is whether those were properly made and are
acceptable.

Conclusions

71.  That being the position, we set aside the final order

dated 24.02.2020 passed by the National Commission in so far

rejection of the insurance claim regarding damage to the stored

agricultural produce is concerned and restore the order of the

State Commission dated 22.07.2011 passed in Consumer

Complaint No. 16 of 2007. On the other hand, as regards the

claim due to damage to the warehouse building is concerned,

we uphold the assessment of the compensation awarded and

quantified at Rs. 1,44,401.00 by the National Commission in its

order dated 24.02.2020. In this regard, the finding of the State

Commission in its order dated 22.07.2011 is set aside.

72.  Consequently, appellant is held entitled to the

following amounts from the respondents: i) damage to the

warehouse – Rs. 1,44,401.00; and, ii) damage to the stored

agricultural produce – Rs. 36,83,955.00, totaling to Rs.

38,28,356.00.
