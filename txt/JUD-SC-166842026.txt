1. Leave granted.

2. This appeal arises from the judgment and order dated 09.12.2025 passed by
      the High Court of Judicature at Bombay, by which the Criminal Appeal (ST)
      No. 23135 of 2025 filed by the appellant herein against the order dated
      29.09.2025 passed by the Designated Court under the Maharashtra
      Protection of Interest of Depositors (in Financial Establishments) Act, 1999
      (the “MPID Act”) declining permission to the appellant to appropriate Rs.
      3.5 crore obtained upon sale of the property mortgaged and securitized under
      the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement
      of Security Interest Act, 2002 (the “SARFAESI Act”) came to be dismissed.

A. FACTUAL MATRIX
3. The State Bank of India, i.e., the appellant herein, extended credit facilities

      to the respondent no. 2 company, for which the promoters thereof, i.e., the
      respondent nos. 3 and 4 respectively mortgaged two of their properties on
      16.09.2009.

4. The appellant bank classified the loan account of the respondent no. 2 as a
      ‘Non-Performing Asset’ (“NPA”) on 31.12.2012. The amount due to the
      appellant bank by the respondent no. 2 as on the said date was estimated at
      Rs. 67.82 crore. In such circumstances, the appellant bank exercised its

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 2 of 84
      security rights under the mortgage agreement and sought to auction the
      mortgaged properties by invoking the provisions of the SARFAESI Act.

5. It came to the knowledge of the appellant bank on 16.08.2014 that the
      respondent no. 2 was involved in transactions that amounted to duping
      depositors inter alia under Sections 3 and 4 of the MPID Act, respectively.
      Pursuant to the charges being filed under the State enactment, the respondent
      no. 1, i.e., the State of Maharashtra, seized the two properties mortgaged
      with the appellant bank on 29.06.2014.

6. The respondent no. 1 further advised the appellant bank to obtain permission
      from the Designated Court under the MPID Act for the purpose of putting
      the mortgaged properties up for sale. Accordingly, the appellant bank
      preferred an application dated 28.11.2014 before the Designated Court
      seeking permission to proceed with the auction sale and to appropriate the
      sale proceeds. The Designated Court vide its order dated 24.03.2015
      permitted the appellant bank to proceed with the auction sale and to deposit
      the proceeds from such sale in a Fixed Deposit Receipt (FDR). However,
      the Designated Court stipulated that the appellant bank was not at liberty to
      deal with the amount in the FDR till further orders.

7. The mortgaged properties were accordingly auctioned and sold for the
      amount of Rs. 18.22 crore on 27.03.2015. The amount received for the two

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 3 of 84
      properties cumulatively amounted to Rs. 19.43 crore (principal amount
      along with interest for delayed payment).

8. Thereafter, the appellant bank, which sought to appropriate the auction
      proceeds under the SARFAESI Act, preferred an application dated
      19.12.2015 before the Designated Court wherein it was averred that Section
      34 of the SARFAESI Act bars all courts from granting injunction in respect
      of any action taken by the bank under the said Act. Further, Section 35 of
      the SARFAESI Act stipulates that its provisions shall override other laws.
      Therefore, the appellant bank prayed that it may be allowed to adjust its
      entire claim from the proceeds of the auction sale and to deposit any excess
      amount with the Designated Authority.

9. The Designated Authority permitted the appellant bank to credit the sum of
      Rs. 15.93 crore in the loan account of the respondent no. 2 and to leave Rs.
      3.5 crore in the FDR. The said amount of Rs. 3.5 crore matured into a sum
      of Rs. 5.51 crore.

10. In the meantime, the respondent no. 1, by way of Notification dated
      16.05.2016, attached the two properties in question, as provided under the
      MPID Act.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 4 of 84
11. The respondent no. 1 received claims from 92 depositors for an aggregate
      amount of Rs. 4 crore, which was sought to be discharged from the corpus
      of Rs. 5.51 crore. The respondent no. 1 had already distributed Rs. 1.13
      crore, leaving a balance of Rs. 2.87 crore, which is yet to be paid.

12. Aggrieved by the disbursal of claims from the aforesaid corpus of Rs. 5.51
      crore, the appellant bank filed a miscellaneous application dated 21.12.2023
      before the City Civil and Sessions Court, Greater Bombay, praying that the
      appellant be permitted to appropriate Rs. 3.5 crore lying in the form of fixed
      deposit, towards the outstanding amount of the loan due and payable by the
      respondent no. 2, as the said amount was received after auctioning of the
      secured property under the SARFAESI Act.

13. The Sessions Court, vide its decision dated 29.09.2025, dismissed the
      application of the appellant by placing reliance on this Court’s judgment in
      National Spot Exchange Ltd. v. Union of India, reported in (2025) 8 SCC
      393 (hereinafter referred to as “NSEL”). It was held by the Sessions Court
      that the judgment in NSEL (supra) observed that the provisions of the MPID
      Act would override the provisions of the SARFAESI Act. Since the
      appellant bank approached the court for permission to auction the properties
      in question, only after registration of the crime under the MPID Act, the
      appellant could not be said to be entitled to the claim of Rs. 3.5 crore. Such

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 5 of 84
      amount along with the interest accrued thereupon would necessarily have to
      be used for the purpose of satisfying the claims of the depositors under the
      MPID Act.

14. The appellant bank, aggrieved by the aforesaid order, preferred an appeal
      before the High Court, praying that the same may be set aside. The High
      Court, vide the judgment dated 09.12.2025 (the “impugned judgment”),
      upheld the decision of the Sessions Court, observing that the judgment of
      this Court in NSEL (supra) clarified that the provisions of the MPID Act
      have to be given effect to irrespective of the provisions of the SARFAESI
      Act. Therefore, the claims under the MPID Act were not subservient to those
      of the appellant bank under the SARFAESI Act. The High Court further
      directed that the amount of Rs. 3.5 crore along with the interest obtained
      thereupon would be transferred to the bank account of the Competent
      Authority under the MPID Act.

15. In such circumstances referred to above, the Bank is before us with the
      present appeal.

B. SUBMISSIONS BY THE PARTIES
      a. Submissions on behalf of the appellant

16. Mr. Rajiv Shakhder, the learned senior counsel appearing on behalf of the
      appellant bank, addressed himself on the conflict between the Central

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 6 of 84
      legislation, i.e., SARFAESI Act and the State legislation, i.e., MPID Act.
      He submitted that the only issue that arose in the present appeal was whether
      the Central legislation would override the State legislation in cases of
      conflict between the two.

17. It was submitted that Chapter III of the SARFAESI Act opens with Section
      13, which confers upon a secured creditor the right to enforce its security
      interest without the intervention of the courts, notwithstanding anything
      contained in Section 69 or Section 69A of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882
      (the “TPA”), respectively. Therefore, the limitations placed upon a
      mortgagor under the TPA are removed by virtue of Section 13 of the
      SARFAESI.

18. The learned senior counsel submitted that the Competent Authority under
      the MPID Act had erroneously attached the properties in question which
      were mortgaged with the appellant. Further, the Authority sought to hold
      sway over the sale proceeds arising from the appellant's auction of the
      mortgaged property, with permission from the Designated Court under the
      MPID Act.

19. It was the submission of the learned senior counsel that once powers under
      Chapter III of the SARFAESI Act got triggered, no civil court, including the

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 7 of 84
      Designated Court under the MPID Act, could exercise jurisdiction over the
      secured assets, i.e., the properties in question.

20. It was explained that Chapter III of the SARFAESI Act provides a step-by-
      step statutory mechanism wherein, once the borrower’s account is declared
      a non-performing asset (“NPA”), the possession of its assets is taken over
      symbolically as well as physically. The process culminates in the recovery
      of dues through the public auction of the said assets, after allowing the
      borrowers or third parties to object and exercise remedies under Sections 17
      and 18, respectively, of the SARFAESI Act. It was submitted that the
      scheme of Chapter III indicates the legislative intent that the enforcement of
      security interests by secured creditors ought not to be interdicted until the
      steps under Section 13(4) are completed, which include the takeover of
      possession of the secured assets.

21. Mr. Shakhder highlighted the conflict concerning the same subject matter,
      posed by the operation of the MPID Act by taking us through the objects
      and reasons thereof. It was submitted that the MPID Act was enacted to
      protect the interest of depositors/investors who deposited their money or
      valuable commodities (inclusive of the interest promised thereupon) to a
      financial establishment, which the financial establishment has failed to or is
      unlikely to return.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 8 of 84
22. It was sought to be argued that the respondent no. 1, i.e., the State of
      Maharashtra, is empowered to pass an order of attachment under Section
      4(1) of the MPID Act upon such default. Upon the publication of such order,
      the attached property vests in the Competent Authority appointed by the
      State Government under the MPID Act pending further orders of the
      Designated Court. The power to appoint the Competent Authority under the
      MPID Act is conferred upon the State Government under Section 5 of the
      Act. Similarly, Section 6 of the MPID Act empowers the State Government
      to constitute Designated Courts with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of
      the High Court of Judicature at Bombay.

23. The learned senior counsel further highlighted that the powers of the
      Designated Court under the MPID Act are delineated under Section 7 of the
      thereof. The provision in sub-section (3) provides for issuance of notice inter
      alia to the person whose property is attached and also provides opportunity
      to any person who claims to have interest in the attached property, to object
      to the attachment notwithstanding the fact that such person may not have
      been served with the notice of attachment. Section 7(5) provides that in the
      event objections are placed on record, the attachment cannot be made
      absolute till such time an investigation is carried out by the Designated
      Court. Section 7(4) stipulates that the order of attachment be made absolute
      in case objections thereto are not lodged.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 9 of 84
24. It was submitted that the rules enacted under Section 16 of the MPID Act
      provide the mechanism of sale of the attached property, if deemed necessary.
      The aspect of attachment and sale of the attached property is an overlap
      between the SARFAESI Act and MPID Act respectively. Mr. Shakhder
      vehemently argued that such overlap presented a direct conflict between the
      Central and State legislation in respect of the same subject matter, i.e.,
      secured assets.

25. The learned senior counsel would argue that the decision in NSEL (supra),
      which forms the basis of the impugned judgment, does not deal with the
      sway that the central legislation, i.e., the SARFAESI Act, would have over
      secured assets. As a result, it does not, per se, address the conflict that would
      arise if the MPID Act is allowed to operate qua the same subject matter, i.e.,
      secured assets. In this regard, the judgment in NSEL (supra) does not flesh
      out the scope, import and impact of clause (1) of Article 246 of the
      Constitution of India.

26. Mr. Shakhder also brought to our notice, the decision of this Court in UCO
      Bank & Anr. v. Dipak Debbarma & Ors., reported in (2017) 2 SCC 585,
      which dealt with a similar issue about conflict between the SARFAESI Act,
      i.e., a Central legislation and the Tripura Land Revenue Act, i.e., a State
      legislation.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 10 of 84
27. It was submitted that when a conflict between the Central and State
      enactments arises, it can only be resolved by taking recourse to Article
      246(1) of the Constitution, as held by this Court in Innoventive Industries
      Ltd. v. ICICI Bank, reported in (2018) 1 SCC 407.

28. Therefore, although the Parliament and State legislature were competent to
      enact the two legislations, i.e., the SARFAESI Act and the MPID Act
      respectively, the latter could not have encroached upon the subject matter of
      secured assets, considering that the Central legislation governs the same.
      The learned senior counsel also submitted that the decision in NSEL (supra)
      failed to take into account that the SARFAESI Act was not only enacted
      later than the MPID Act but was also the dominant legislation governing the
      treatment of secured assets. As such, the MPID Act necessarily has to give
      way to the security rights created on such assets.

29. With the aforesaid averments as the backdrop of the dispute between the
      parties before us, the learned senior counsel presented the following issues
      that require answer by a larger bench:
      (a) Whether the State legislation, i.e., the MPID Act, which is otherwise
            constitutionally valid, can trench upon the subject matter of secured
            assets that fall within the purview of the Central legislation, i.e., the
            SARFAESI Act?

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 11 of 84
(b) Whether the exercise of powers under the MPID Act over secured
      assets would cause conflict with the SARFAESI Act, which would then
      trigger the provisions under Article 246(1) of the Constitution?

(c) Whether the subsequent decision of this Court in the case of NSEL
      (supra) is rendered per incuriam as it failed to notice and consider the
      binding precedent of another coordinate bench in UCO Bank (supra)?

(d) By force of the non-obstante clause in clause (1) of Article 246, would
      Parliamentary legislation, i.e., the SARFAESI Act, prevail
      notwithstanding the exclusive power of the State legislature to make a
      law with respect to a matter enumerated in the State List?

(e) Whether the provisions of the MPID Act would override any claim for
      priority of interest by the secured creditors in respect of the properties
      which have been attached under the MPID Act, to protect the interest
      of the investors?

(f) Whether the MPID Act, a State legislation, is required to yield to the
      SARFAESI Act, a central legislation traceable to Entry 45 of the List
      I, to the extent that it is inconsistent with the SARFAESI Act? Whether
      such inconsistent provisions be deemed to be invalid, since the
      SARFAESI Act is the dominant legislation?

30. As regards the observations of this Court in NSEL (supra), Mr. Shakhder
      submitted as follows:
      a) The principles laid down in NSEL (supra) required reconsideration, as
            the same were inconsistent with the constitutional and statutory
            provisions. It was submitted that the judgment ignored the principle of
            federal supremacy of the Parliament over the State legislations, duly
            recognized under Article 246 of the Constitution.
      b) The judgment in NSEL (supra) marked a significant departure from
            the principle of law laid down in Innoventive (supra), wherein the
            interplay between the Maharashtra Relief Undertakings Act, 1958 and
            the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (“IBC”) was under
            consideration. It was held therein that the IBC, being a complete and
            exhaustive code on insolvency matters, prevailed over the provisions
            of the State legislation which pertained to the same subject matter.
      c) Further, the decision in NSEL (supra) overlooked the fact that the
            MPID Act was enacted under Entries 1 and 30 of List II which pertain
            to Public Order and Money Lending respectively. Though the Act may
            regulate depositors and fraudulent schemes by financial
            establishments, yet, when the State Government under the MPID Act
            seeks to take control of assets secured with banks, it encroaches upon

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 13 of 84
      the subject matter of secured assets, which the SARFAESI Act, a
      Central legislation, governs.
d) It was also submitted that the SARFAESI Act, having been enacted
      later, would prevail over the MPID Act.
e) Similarly, if the dominant purpose test is applied, then the provisions
      of the SARFAESI Act would also prevail, considering that the said
      enactment seeks to recover debts from defaulting debtors, as opposed
      to the purpose of the MPID Act, which is to secure siphoned deposits
      made by the depositors.
f) As such, the judgment in NSEL (supra) ignores the fact that the
      enforcement of security interest is one of the primary objectives
      underlying the SARFAESI Act. While the said objective does not
      overlap with that of the MPID Act, yet the permissibility of attaching
      already secured properties under the MPID Act disrupts the functioning
      and operation of the Central legislations, i.e., SARFAESI Act and IBC
      respectively. This results in apparent repugnancy, and it is settled law
      that whenever such repugnancy arises, the Parliamentary enactments
      would prevail.
g) Therefore, the decision in NSEL (supra) did not correctly apply Article
      246. It allowed State enactment to override or dilute Central legislation

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 14 of 84
            enacted under Entry 45 of List I. This is in contravention of the doctrine
            of federal supremacy, which has been held to mean that State
            legislation cannot encroach on matters exclusively assigned to the
            Parliament under Schedule VII to the Constitution. Therefore, the
            attachment provisions of the MPID Act are in direct, irreconcilable
            conflict with the provisions governing the enforcement of security
            interests under the SARFAESI Act, rendering compliance with both
            impossible.

31. Mr. Shakhder submitted that the judgment in NSEL (supra) is liable to be
      declared per incuriam as the judgment of this Court in UCO Bank (supra),
      which has a direct bearing on the issue involved, was not considered. The
      learned senior counsel placed reliance on paragraphs 11 and 18 of UCO
      Bank (supra) respectively to submit that if the courts cannot establish the
      mutual existence of two legislations which are in conflict, then by virtue of
      Article 246(1) of the Constitution, the Parliamentary legislation would
      prevail over the State enactment even though the latter was legislated
      competently within the field demarcated under List II of the Schedule VII.

32. It was also submitted that the judgment in NSEL (supra) did not take into
      account the observations of this Court in Hoechst Pharmaceuticals Ltd. v.
      State of Bihar, reported in (1983) 4 SCC 45, in relation to the scope and

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 15 of 84
      effect of Article 246(1) of the Constitution. It was submitted that paragraph
      69 of the said judgment observed that in case of overlap between List II and
      List I or III, the State enactment under the former would be considered to be
      ultra vires in light of the non obstante clause in Article 246(1) read with the
      opening words of Article 246(3). For this reason, the judgment in NSEL
      (supra) is liable to be declared per incuriam.

33. The learned senior counsel submitted that the appellant bank acts as a
      custodian of public funds and operates primarily on the interest generated
      from loans and deposits. In order to safeguard the financial interests of the
      banks, it is vital to maintain an underlying security interest.

34. Lastly, it was submitted that the SARFAESI Act is a critical parliamentary
      statute designed to protect both the banks and their customers by expediting
      the debt recovery process, without the need for court intervention. Despite
      such legal frameworks, banks, including the appellant bank, continue to
      carry substantial non-performing assets (“NPA”). The learned senior
      counsel submitted that if the judgment in NSEL (supra) is not revisited, it
      could adversely impact the broader financial interests and stability of the
      banking sector and exacerbate the impact of NPAs on the banks.

      b. Submissions on behalf of the respondent State
35. Mr. Shayam Mehta, the learned senior counsel appearing on behalf of the

      respondent State addressed himself on the scope and operation of Article
      246 of the Constitution. It was submitted that before a Central legislation
      relating to List I can be said to override a State legislation relating to List II,
      it must be shown that the State legislation has transgressed upon the
      legislative field(s) mentioned in List I of the Schedule VII to the
      Constitution. For this purpose, it would be necessary to determine the pith
      and substance of the State legislation, and so long as the same is found to be
      referable to the fields in List II, the State enactment would be held to be
      legislated competently, even if some provisions may incidentally transgress
      any entry provided in List I of the Schedule VII.

36. It is well settled that in pith and substance, the MPID Act is referable to
      Entries 1, 30 and 32 of the List II. This aspect was considered by this Court
      in K.K. Baskaran v. State, reported in (2011) 3 SCC 793, wherein the
      legislative competence of the Tamil Nadu Protection of Interest of
      Depositors (in Financial Establishments) Act, 1997 (“TNPID Act”) was in
      question. The argument that the TNPID Act encroached upon the field
      specified in Entry 45 of List I was rejected. It was observed therein that the
      TNPID Act does not pertain to banking transactions or acceptance of
      deposits, rather, it is designed to protect the public from fraudulent financial

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 17 of 84
      establishments. The Statement of Objects and Reasons (“SOR”) of the
      TNPID Act indicate that the Act does not concern itself with the
      incorporation, regulation or winding up of banking corporations. Instead, the
      SOR of the Act states that its avowed objective is to punish fraudulent
      financial institutions and return the money of gullible depositors who were
      defrauded. The attachment of the properties of fraudulent financial
      establishments under the TNPID Act is intended to provide an effective and
      speedy remedy for aggrieved depositors to realise their dues.

37. It was submitted that this Court in K.K. Baskaran (supra) authoritatively
      opined that similar to the TNPID Act, the pith and substance of the MPID
      Act was also relatable to Entries 1, 30 and 32 of the List II respectively. It
      was further observed therein that the definition of ‘financial establishments’
      under the MPID Act contained in Section 2(d) thereof excluded banking
      companies as defined in the Banking Regulation Act, 1949. Therefore, the
      MPID Act was in no way relatable to Entry 45 of List I of the Schedule VII
      to the Constitution.

38. Based on the judgment in K.K. Baskaran (supra), Mr. Mehta submitted that
      the MPID Act did not even incidentally transgress Entry 45 of List I, and no
      conflict between the MPID Act and the SARFAESI Act exists. Therefore,
      Article 246(1) of the Constitution has no relevance in the present matter.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 18 of 84
39. As regards the issue whether the MPID Act was repugnant to the SARFAESI
      Act and hence, was void in terms of Article 254(1) of the Constitution, the
      learned senior counsel submitted that any question in this regard would be
      wholly misconceived. He submitted that Article 254(1) had no application
      to the facts of the present matter since the legislations in consideration before
      us were not enacted in relation to List III of the Schedule VII to the
      Constitution.

40. Mr. Mehta further submitted that Section 4 of the MPID Act, which pertains
      to the attachment of the property of financial establishments, opens with a
      non obstante clause, making it effective notwithstanding any other law. On
      the other hand, Section 13(1) of the SARFAESI Act opens with a non
      obstante provision that applies only to Sections 69 and 69A of the TPA.
      Therefore, a perusal of the two provisions in consonance would indicate that
      Section 13(1) of the SARFAESI Act would not override Section 4 of the
      MPID Act, even if there is a conflict between the two enactments.

41. It was submitted that the prevalence of the MPID Act is further established
      by Section 37 of the SARFAESI Act, which inter alia provides that the
      SARFAESI Act and the Rules thereunder shall be in addition to and not in
      derogation of any other law for the time being in force. This indicates that
      the SARFAESI Act does not bar the application of the MPID Act.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 19 of 84
42. Insofar as the overriding provisions of both the enactments are concerned,
      the learned senior counsel submitted that no application of the same as

      i. Whether there is a conflict between the provisions of the SARFAESI

D. ANALYSIS
45. Before adverting to the conspectus of facts before us, it is necessary to look

      into a few relevant provisions of the MPID Act and understand its avowed
      purpose.

 I. Scope and purpose of the MPID Act.
46. The respondent State enacted the MPID Act to address the menace of

      fraudulent transactions by certain financial establishments that took deposits
      of the common people without any intention to refund the same along with
      the promised interest thereon, or to render the services guaranteed in

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 21 of 84
      exchange for such sum. This presented a law and order problem for which

      suitable action was required. The respondent State enacted the MPID Act to

      objective of ensuring effective remedy for the depositors who may have lost

      their money to such entities. The Statement of Objects and Reasons of the

      MPID Act reads thus:

               “There is a mushroom growth of Financial Establishments
               in the State of Maharashtra in the recent past. The sole
               object of these establishments is of grabbing money
               received as deposits from public, mostly middle class and
               poor on the promises of unprecedented high attractive
               interest rates of interest or rewards and without any
               obligation to refund the deposit to the investors on
               maturity or without any provision for ensuring rendering
               of the services in kind in return, as assured. Many of these
               Financial Establishments have defaulted to return the
               deposits to public. As such deposits run into crores of
               rupees, it has resulted in great public resentment and
               uproar, creating law and order problem in the State of
               Maharashtra, especially in the city like Mumbai which is
               treated as the financial capital of India. It is, therefore,
               expedient to a make a suitable legislation in the public
               interest to curb the unscrupulous activities of such
               Financial Establishments in the State of Maharashtra.”

      compensating them out of the properties of the financial establishments that
      defrauded them. For this purpose, the Act permits the attachment of
      properties of the financial establishment in Section 4 thereof and requires
      the Designated Court under Section 7 to make the order of attachment
      absolute so as to enable the State government to auction the property.

      have secured the same to secured creditors under the SARFAESI Act. In
      such circumstances, we find it apposite to examine the position of law as
      regards the competing interests of the secured creditors and the depositors
      under the MPID Act and whether a reconciliation between the two is
      possible.

II. Competing interests of the secured creditors under SARFAESI Act and
      depositors under the MPID Act.
      (i) Analysis of the judgment in NSEL (supra).

56. The petitioner in NSEL (supra) was an exchange trading platform that
      defaulted in payment to its trading clients who had deposited their money
      therewith to be invested. Therefore, an FIR was filed by such persons under
      inter alia, the MPID Act. Accordingly, the State of Maharashtra attached
      movable and immovable properties of the petitioner company therein to
      ensure recovery of monies allegedly lost by genuine trading clients who
      deposited the same on the petitioner’s platform.

57. Thereafter, insolvency proceedings against the petitioner came to be
      instituted, and the moratorium under Section 14 of the IBC started operating
      in respect of suits, disputes and transactions as regards the properties of the
      petitioner. After the initiation of the insolvency proceedings, the secured
      creditors of the petitioner submitted before this Court that they would have
      priority of interest over the properties attached by the State of Maharashtra
      under the MPID Act. It must be noted that it was not disputed by either side
      that the attachment under the MPID Act predated the initiation of the
      insolvency proceedings.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 29 of 84
58. In this backdrop, this Court enquired into the question whether the secured
      creditors would have priority of interest of charge over assets already
      attached under the MPID Act.

59. The Bench observed that this Court, in Sonal Hemant Joshi & Ors. v. State

of Maharashtra & Ors., reported in (2012) 10 SCC 601, has upheld the

constitutional validity of the MPID Act, while relying on K.K. Baskaran

(supra), which had upheld the constitutionality of the TNPID Act. It was

held that the MPID Act contains similar provisions as in the TNPID Act,

and as a consequence, the former was referable to Entries 1, 30 and 32 of

List II of the Schedule VII in pith and substance. Hence, it was held that the

MPID Act does not encroach on the legislative field occupied by Entry 45

of List I of the Schedule VII. The relevant portion of the judgment in NSEL

(supra) reads thus:

“52. This Court in Sonal Hemant Joshi [Sonal Hemant

Joshi v. State of Maharashtra, (2012) 10 SCC 601 : (2013)

1 SCC (Civ) 543 : (2013) 1 SCC (Cri) 1084] had upheld

the constitutional validity of the MPID Act in view of the

decision                     in K.K.  Baskaran v. State [K.K.

Baskaran v. State, (2011) 3 SCC 793 : (2011) 2 SCC (Civ)

90], in which the Court was dealing with the identical

legislation enacted by the State of Tamil Nadu, namely, the

T.N. Protection of Interest of Depositors (in Financial

Establishments) Act, 1997, enacted with the object to

ameliorate the situation of the depositors from the clutches

of fraudulent financial establishments, who had duped the

investor/public by offering high rates of interest on

Act was in pith and substance relatable to Entries 1, 30
and 32 of the State List (List II) of the Seventh Schedule. It
further held that the financial institutions/establishments
as contemplated in the Tamil Nadu Act did not come either
under the Reserve Bank of India Act or the Banking

not focussed on the transaction of banking or acceptance
of deposit, but was focussed on remedying the situation of
the depositors who were deceived by the fraudulent
financial establishments. The said Act was intended to deal
with neither the banks which did the business of banking
and were governed by the Reserve Bank of India Act and
the Banking Regulation Act, nor the non-banking financial
companies enacted under the Companies Act. In the case
of the Tamil Nadu Act, the attachment of properties was
intended to provide for an effective and speedy remedy to

Hence, the Reserve Bank of India Act, the Banking
Regulation Act or the Companies Act did not occupy the
field which the impugned Tamil Nadu Act occupied,

: 2005 SCC OnLine Bom 1069] of the Bombay High Court,
whereby the MPID Act was held unconstitutional.
Subsequently, the Court in Sonal Hemant Joshi [Sonal
Hemant Joshi v. State of Maharashtra, (2012) 10 SCC 601
: (2013) 1 SCC (Civ) 543 : (2013) 1 SCC (Cri) 1084],
specifically relied upon the said judgment in K.K.

               Maharashtra v. 63 Moons Technologies Ltd. [State of

               54. In view of the above, there remains no shadow of doubt
               that the State of Maharashtra was within its legislative

60. Having observed that the MPID Act was in pith and substance referable to

      the State List and not Entry 45 of the Union List, this Court made the

      following observations as regards the question whether the secured creditors

      would have priority of interest in the properties attached under the MPID

      Act:

      a) First, reliance was placed on this Court’s decision in Union of

          India v. Delhi High Court Bar Assn., reported in (2002) 4 SCC 275

          wherein it was observed that the recovery of dues is an essential function

          of any banking institution. For this purpose, the Parliament is empowered

          to provide the mechanism by which the monies due to the banks would

          be recovered. In exercise of such power, the SARFAESI Act and the

          Recovery of Debts and Bankruptcy Act, 1993 (“RDB Act”) respectively

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 32 of 84
were enacted, both of which provided mechanisms for banks to recover

their dues. However, merely because the SARFAESI Act had been

enacted by the Parliament in respect of Entry 45 of List I, it could not be

said that it can override the provisions of the MPID Act which is another

competently enacted legislation referable to Entries 1, 30 and 32 of List

II. Such an interpretation would violate the federal structure of the

Constitution, as it would amount to depriving the State legislatures of the

power to enact and enforce legislation within their exclusive domain. The

relevant portion of the judgment in NSEL (supra) is reproduced below:

        “57. As held by the Constitution Bench in Union of

        to the conduct of business by the banks. Recovery of
        dues is an essential function of any banking institution.
        In exercise of its legislative power relating to banking,
        Parliament can provide the mechanism by which monies
        due to the banks and financial institutions can be
        recovered.

        58. However, merely because the Sarfaesi Act and the
        RDB Act which are enacted in respect of the subject-

        matter falling in List II — State List. If such an
        interpretation is permitted to be made, it would amount
        to denuding the State of its legislative power to enact
        and enforce legislation, which is within the exclusive

   cannot be bypassed on the basis of the principle of parliamentary

   supremacy. In the situation where there is an incidental overlap between

   a central legislation and state legislation such that it causes conflict

   between the two, it would be apposite to determine which of the two

   enactments is the dominant legislation [See: Paragraph 94 of ITC

d) Fourthly, this Court considered Section 26E of the SARFAESI Act

   which provides thus:

            “26. Priority to secured creditors.––
            Notwithstanding anything contained in any other law
            for the time being in force, after the registration of
            security interest, the debts due to any secured creditor
            shall be paid in priority over all other debts and all

    As regards the argument that in light of Section 26E of the SARFAESI

    Act, the dues of the secured creditors shall be given priority in respect of

    the properties attached under the MPID Act, it was held that the same

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 36 of 84
was liable to be rejected. This Court observed that for the rights of

secured creditors to be given priority under Section 26E, two conditions

must be met. First, the creditor's security interest must be registered

under Section 26D of the SARFAESI Act. Secondly, the “debt” which

could be overridden by the interest of the secured creditor must be

payable to the government or local authority. It was observed that the

attachment of property under the MPID Act did not meet the second

condition because even though such property, comes to be vested in the

Competent Authority appointed by the State government, yet the

attachment thereof is for the purpose of recovery of deposits to be paid

to the depositors/investors whom the financial establishment allegedly

defrauded. Therefore, it was held that the rights of the secured creditors

could not be given priority over the rights of the depositors under the

      (ii) Conflict between the SARFAESI Act and MPID Act
64. The position of law is well settled as regards the question whether the MPID

      Act encroaches into the Parliament’s field of legislation under Entry 45 of
      List I such that the doctrine of pith and substance would hit it. The decisions
      of this Court in Sonal Hemant Joshi (supra) as well as K.K. Baskaran

      referable to Entries 1, 30 and 32 of List II and does not present such a degree
      of invasion into the Parliament’s legislative field provided under Entry 45

      of List I that would require it to be declared unconstitutional. Therefore, the
      encroachment is merely incidental and ancillary.

      specific area of ‘recovery of dues’ by way of attaching properties under the
      MPID Act causes an impediment to the process of ‘recovery of dues’
      provided in the SARFAESI Act and RDB Act, which provide for the banks
      to enforce their security interests. Though the encroachment may be
      ancillary for the purposes of determining legislative competence, it
      constitutes a significant hindrance insofar as the operation and
      implementation of the statutes are concerned.

      the SARFAESI Act and the MPID Act. As such, we cannot permit that the
      provisions of either the SARFAESI Act or the MPID Act become dead letter
      law in the absence of any resolution of the conflict as regards the
      implementation of such provisions for the objective of “recovery of dues”.

      the provisions of both the statutes is possible so as to offer a feasible
      resolution.

       (i) Statutory scheme of the SARFAESI Act and MPID Act.

 70. Section 13 of the SARFAESI Act empowers a secured creditor to enforce

       the security rights created in its favour without the intervention of the courts

       if the liability of the debtor is declared a non-performing asset and continues

       this judgment, we may take a closer look at sub-section (4) of Section 13,

               money as is sufficient to pay the secured debt. (…)”

71. It is worth noting that Section 13(4) aims to realise the secured asset by
      providing the secured creditor with the right to take possession of the
      secured asset for transfer by way of lease, assignment or sale, or managing
      the said asset. What can be discerned from the aforesaid provision is that the
      enforcement of security revolves around the secured asset, without which
      the existence of security rights in favour of a creditor stands ineffective and
      futile.

72. On the other hand, a bare textual reading of Section 4 of the MPID Act
      would indicate the following:

      • First, once there is a default committed in terms of Section 3 of the
          MPID Act, the initial course of action for the State Government would
          be to attach the money or property believed to be acquired in the name
          of the financial establishment or any other person, out of the deposits
          collected (hereinafter referred to as “Category I properties”).

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 47 of 84
      • Secondly, if the money or property acquired out of the deposits is
          unavailable or insufficient to repay the depositors, then the State
          Government may attach the other properties of the financial
          establishment, or its promoters, directors or partners (hereinafter
          referred to as “Category II properties”).

      • Lastly, the properties so attached would come to be vested in the
          Competent Authority appointed by the State Government pending the
          orders of the designated court under the MPID Act.

73. It is abundantly clear from the perusal of Section 4 of the MPID Act that
      apart from the Category I properties, any other property which would fall in
      Category II is not specifically identified for the discharge of dues under the
      MPID Act. It can be any property which, in the opinion of the State
      government, would be sufficient to repay the interest of the depositors.

74. Section 13 of the SARFAESI Act provides the secured creditors with the
      right to enforce their interest on the property in question whereas Section 4
      of the MPID Act empowers the State government to attach the very same
      property for achieving the objects of the Act. Both proceedings intersect at
      the rights sought to be enforced simultaneously in respect of the defaulter's
      singular property.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 48 of 84
75. In such a scenario, the issue that arises is whether the interest of the secured
      creditor would gain priority over the interests of the depositors which the
      MPID Act seeks to protect.

76. To answer this issue, we find it apposite to understand the statutory scheme
      of both the enactments to determine whether the legislature itself has
      provided any remedy to such conflict. In this regard, we may refer to Section
      35 of the SARFAESI Act which reads thus:
               “35. The provisions of this Act to override other laws.—
               The provisions of this Act shall have effect,
               notwithstanding anything inconsistent therewith contained
               in any other law for the time being in force or any
               instrument having effect by virtue of any such law.”

77. A bare textual reading of Section 35 of the SARFAESI Act indicates that
      the provisions thereunder would prevail in a situation where there is a
      conflict or an inconsistency posed by any other law. It is a well-settled
      position of law that the non-obstante clause under Section 35 cannot be
      invoked to stultify the provisions of every statute. The expression “any other
      law for the time being in force” would cover only the legislations that operate
      in the same field as the SARFAESI Act [See: Vishal N. Kalsaria v. Bank of
      India, reported in (2016) 3 SCC 762].

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 49 of 84
78. Therefore, another issue which crops up is whether the non-obstante clause
      under Section 35 of the SARFAESI Act effectively prioritizes the rights of
      the secured creditors in relation to the rights of depositors under the MPID
      Act. However, we need not delve into this question in light of Section 14 of
      the MPID Act, which provides that the MPID Act would have an overriding
      effect in case of inconsistency with any other law. Section 14 of the MPID
      Act reads thus:
               “14. Act to override other laws.—Save as otherwise
               provided in this Act, the provisions of this Act shall have
               effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent therewith
               contained in any other law for the time being in force or
               any custom or usage or any instrument having effect by
               virtue of any such law.”

79. Since the non-obstante clauses under both Section 35 of the SARFAESI Act
      and Section 14 of the MPID Act are couched in strict language, a clear
      conclusion as regards which overriding provision would prevail cannot be
      discerned from the plain language of the said provisions. Therefore, the
      tension between the recovery mechanisms of both the statutes in respect of
      their implementation continues to remain. In such circumstance, we may
      refer to the principles which are germane to the determination of precedence
      of a statute over another in case of conflict between the two.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 50 of 84
      (ii) Principles for determination of precedence of a statute over another in
            case of conflict.

80. At this juncture, we find it apposite to clarify the principles enunciated by
      this Court to determine which statute will have precedence in case of a
      conflict. In Kumaon Motor Owners' Union Ltd. v. State of U.P., reported
      in 1965 SCC OnLine SC 117, this Court highlighted three elements to
      resolve conflict between two statutes, which are as follow:

       • If there is a conflict between the provisions of two statutes and nothing
            is repugnant, the provisions in the later statute would prevail.

       • While resolving such conflict, the court must look into the object
            behind the two statutes. In other words, what is to be looked at is what
            necessitated the legislature to enact a particular provision later in time,
            which may be in conflict with the provisions of an earlier statute.

       • The court must look into the language of the provisions. If the language
            of a particular provision is found to be more emphatic, the same would
            be indicative of the intention of the legislature that the same shall
            prevail over other statutes.

       The relevant paragraphs of the judgment in Kumaon Motor (supra) are
       reproduced hereinbelow:

               “12. This argument is met on behalf of the State by
               reference to Section 43 of the Act which lays down that
               “the provisions of this Act or any rule made thereunder or
               any order made under any such rule shall have effect

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 51 of 84
notwithstanding anything inconsistent therewith contained
in any enactment other than this Act or in any instrument
having effect by virtue of any enactment other than this
Act”. It does appear that there is some apparent conflict
between Section 43 on the one hand and Section 68-B of
the Motor Vehicles Act read with Section 6(4) of the Act on
the other, and that conflict has to be resolved. The only
way to do it is to decide whether in such a situation, Section
43 of the Act will prevail or Section 68-B of the Motor
Vehicles Act will prevail. We are of opinion that Section
43 of the Act must prevail. In the first place. Section 43
appears in an Act which is later than the Motor Vehicle
Act and therefore in such a situation unless there is
anything repugnant the provisions in the later Act must
prevail. Secondly, if we look at the object behind the two
statutes, namely, the Act and the Motor Vehicles Act, there
can be no doubt that the Act, which was passed to meet an
emergency arising out of the Chinese invasion of India in
1962, must prevail over the provisions contained in
Chapter IV-A of the Motor Vehicles Act which were meant
to meet a situation arising out of the taking over of motor
transport by the State. Thirdly, if we compare the language
of Section 43 of the Act with Section 68-B of the Motor
Vehicles Act we find that the language of Section 43 is
more emphatic than the language of Section 68-B. Section
43 provides that the provisions of the Act or any rule made
thereunder shall have effect notwithstanding anything
inconsistent therewith contained in any enactment other
than the Act. This would show that the intention of the
legislature was that the Act shall prevail over other
statutes. But we do not find the same emphatic langauge in
Section 68-B which lays down that the provisions of
Chapter IV-A would prevail notwithstanding anything
inconsistent therewith contained in Chapter IV of the
Motor Vehicles Act or in any other law for the time being
in force. The intention seems to be clear in view of the

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 52 of 84
               collocation of the words “in Chapter IV of this Act” with
               the words “in any other law for the time being in force”
               that Chapter IV-A was to prevail over Chapter IV of the
               Motor Vehicles Act or over any other law of the same kind
               dealing with motor vehicles or for compensation. On the
               other hand Section 43 of the Act emphatically says that the
               Act will prevail over any enactment other than the Act, and
               this suggests that the legislature intended that the
               emergency legislation in the Act will be paramount if there
               is any inconsistency between it and any other provision of
               any other law whatsoever. Such a provision is
               understandable in view of the emergency which led to the
               passing of the Act.”

                                                             (Emphasis supplied)

81. Similarly, in Solidaire India Ltd. v. Fairgrowth Financial Services Ltd.,

      reported in (2001) 3 SCC 71, this Court observed that in case of conflict

      between two special legislations, the later must prevail. It was held so

      because, at the time of the later statute's enactment, the legislature could be

      said to have been aware of the previous statute and its non-obstante clause.

      In such a case, if the legislature still conferred the later statute with a non-

      obstante clause, the inference ought to be that it was the intention of the

      legislature that the later enactment prevailed [See: Paragraph 11 of Solidaire

      (supra)].

82. What is discernible from the aforesaid expositions is that the bright-line test

      for determining precedence of one statute over another in the event of a

conflict which does not amount to repugnancy involves testing on the
following touchstones:
1) Provision enacted later in time: The resolution of implementational

      conflict between the SARFAESI Act and the MPID Act using the
      principle of ‘primacy of subsequent legislation’ is not difficult, since
      the SARFAESI Act was enacted later than the MPID Act. However,
      before we arrive at a conclusion in respect of the issue of primacy of
      the SARFAESI Act over the MPID Act, we must satisfy ourselves as
      regards the other two principles discernible from the dictum of this
      Court in Kumaon Motor (supra).

2) Objective underlying the conflicting statutes: The decision of this
      Court in NSEL (supra) clarifies the objectives underlying the MPID
      Act as well as the SARFAESI Act.
      (a) The Statement of Objects and Reasons of the MPID Act and the
            language of the provisions thereunder indicate two objectives:
            firstly, punishment for fraudulent default by the Financial
            Establishments, and secondly, recovery of the deposits by
            attachment of assets of the Financial Establishment and other
            associated persons and its return to the common public.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 54 of 84
(b) On the other hand, the objective underlying the SARFAESI Act is
      the speedy recovery of debts which have been declared as non-
      performing assets by banks through enforcement of security rights
      that such banks hold in securitized properties of the defaulting
      borrower. The purpose of speedy recovery, without the need to go
      through lengthy civil litigation, lies at the core of the SARFAESI
      Act.

(c) We are conscious of the fact that the avowed objectives of both
      the statutes are in the interest of welfare of the public. However,
      in our considered opinion, the provisions of the SARFAESI Act
      must prevail over the provisions of the MPID Act. We say so
      because even though both the statutes have been enacted for
      recovery of monies, yet ‘recovery of dues’ by banks stands at a
      higher pedestal since banks are the custodians of public money
      [See: Om Sakthi Sekar v. V. Sukumar, reported in 2026 SCC
      OnLine SC 368 and SBI v. Sayed Arman Rabbani, reported in
      2024 SCC OnLine NCDRC 733].

(d) In such view of the matter, we find it vital to the interests of the
      public at large that the objective of ‘recovery of dues’ of secured
      creditors is allowed to take precedence over the recoveries sought
      to be made under the MPID Act.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 55 of 84
      3) Emphatic language of conflicting provisions: As discussed in the
            aforesaid part of this judgment (more particularly paragraphs 78 and 79
            hereof), the non-obstante clauses under both Section 35 of the
            SARFAESI Act and Section 14 of the MPID Act, are couched in strict
            language. Hence, a decision in respect of which overriding provision
            would gain precedence is not discernible from the limited perusal of
            the said sections. As such, we find it necessary to conduct a further
            examination of the statutory scheme of the two enactments.

83. We may clarify at this stage that though our observations as regards the first
      and second principles, expounded hereinabove, may render such
      examination academic yet, we find it indispensable for addressing
      comprehensively the issues raised in the present litigation and the
      observations of this Court in NSEL (supra).

84. Before we delve into the study of the statutory schemes of the SARFAESI
      Act and MPID Act, it is apposite that we explain briefly the nature of
      security interests that the SARFAESI Act governs.

      (iii) Nature of the security interest created by secured creditors and its effect
            on attachment under the MPID Act.

85. The term “security interest” has been defined in Section 2(zf) of the
      SARFAESI Act and reads thus:

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 56 of 84
               “2. Definitions.––
               (…)
               (zf) “security interest” means right, title or interest of any
               kind, other than those specified in section 31, upon
               property created in favour of any secured creditor and
               includes—
               (i) any mortgage, charge, hypothecation, assignment or
               any right, title or interest of any kind, on tangible asset,
               retained by the secured creditor as an owner of the
               property, given on hire or financial lease or conditional
               sale or under any other contract which secures the
               obligation to pay any unpaid portion of the purchase price
               of the asset or an obligation incurred or credit provided to
               enable the borrower to acquire the tangible asset; or
               (ii) such right, title or interest in any intangible asset or
               assignment or licence of such intangible asset which
               secures the obligation to pay any unpaid portion of the
               purchase price of the intangible asset or the obligation
               incurred or any credit provided to enable the borrower
               to acquire the intangible asset or licence of intangible
               asset;”

86. The plain text of the aforesaid provision makes it clear that interest of any

      kind constitutes a security interest and specifically includes inter alia

      mortgage, charge, hypothecation, assignment of a right or any other kind of

      right, title or interest. Such interest in the property that is created in favour

      of the secured creditor is therefore, in the nature of an encumbrance on the

      property.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 57 of 84
87. It is a settled position of law that encumbrance, being a charge on the
      property, must run with it and become the liability of the person or the
      authority in the hands of which rights to the property lie [See: AI
      Champdany Industries Ltd. v. Official Liquidator, reported in (2009) 4
      SCC 486 and State of Karnataka v. Shreyas Papers (P) Ltd., reported in
      (2006) 1 SCC 615].

88. Having regard to the aforesaid position of law, we may say without any
      manner of doubt that though there may be a security interest in favour of the
      secured creditor that puts a charge on the property, yet such charge places
      no bar on the attachment of the said property under the MPID Act.

89. However, such attachment is subject to the caveat that the security interest
      in favour of the secured creditor, being a charge on the property, shall run
      with it, i.e., the property cannot be attached encumbrance-free. We say so
      because the MPID Act, more particularly, Sections 4 and 7 thereof, which
      deal with attachment of property, do not stipulate that the attachment of the
      property shall be free of encumbrances. In other words, the interest of the
      secured creditor is not defeated by the factum of attachment of property
      under the MPID Act.

90. At this juncture, it is apposite to understand that the expression “attachment”
      has no definite connotation and it does not by itself create a charge on the

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 58 of 84
      property attached. The limited purpose of attachment is to take the property
      into custody of the law to seize or hold the property for the payment of the
      debts of the owner of such property or to prohibit the transfer, conversion,
      disposition or movement of the property [See: Kerala State Financial
      Enterprises Ltd. v. Official Liquidator, reported in (2006) 10 SCC 709].

91. In our considered view, the purpose of attachment in the MPID Act is not
      dissimilar. Section 4(2) of the MPID Act stipulates that on publication of the
      notice of attachment, the properties of the Financial Establishment or any
      other persons, as mentioned in such notice, shall forthwith vest in the
      Competent Authority under the MPID Act pending further orders from the
      designated court. The MPID Act vests the attached property in the
      Competent Authority to restrain alienation thereof by the Financial
      Establishment. Such vesting is cum onere and does not remove any prior
      charges or interests in the property. We say so because the MPID Act does
      not provide for attachment and vesting free from all encumbrances, as is the
      case, say, in the National Highways Act, 1956, or the Right to Fair
      Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and
      Resettlement Act, 2013.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 59 of 84
92. Therefore, the factum of ‘vesting’ of the property in the Competent
      Authority under the MPID Act does not defeat or hamper the security
      interests and rights of the secured creditor in the property.

93. The discussion in the aforesaid paragraphs clarifies two things: (a) the
      tension as regards the conflict in simultaneous implementation of the
      SARFAESI Act and the MPID Act remains unresolved despite the provision
      of non-obstante clauses in both the statutes; (b) the attachment of secured
      property under the MPID Act is not prohibited, and the security rights and
      interest in such property would run with it despite attachment.

94. The question that naturally follows the above discussion is that even though
      the attachment and vesting of secured property under the MPID Act is
      insufficient to defeat the interest of the secured creditor, would it still have
      any detrimental effect on the right of the secured creditor to receive its dues
      from the secured property? In other words, would the secured creditors
      continue to have priority of interest in the property despite its attachment
      under the MPID Act?

      (iv) Priority of interest for secured creditors under the SARFAESI Act.
95. Since the attachment of the secured property under the MPID Act is

      permissible so long as the security interests run with the property attached,
      the issue that lies at the root of the conflict is whether it is the secured

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 60 of 84
      creditor who will have priority of interest over the proceeds from the
      property or the depositors under the MPID Act.

96. We may refer to Section 26E of the SARFAESI Act in this regard. Section
      26E provides that the “debts due to any secured creditors shall be paid in
      priority over all other debts and all revenues, taxes, cesses and other rates
      payable to the Central Government or State Government or local
      authority”.

97. We may clarify at the outset to obviate any confusion that we are not
      concerned with the expression “all revenues, taxes, cesses and other rates
      payable to the Central Government or State Government or local authority”
      appearing in Section 26E of the SARFAESI Act. The sole focus of the
      present examination is the expression “all other debts” appearing in Section
      26E of the SARFAESI Act.

      • Meaning of ‘debt’ appearing in the expression ‘all other debts’ in
          Section 26E of the SARFAESI Act.

98. The term “debt” is used twice in Section 26E – first, to describe the debts
      which are due to the secured creditors and have been defined in Section 2(ha)
      of the SARFAESI Act read with Section 2(g) of the RDB Act; and secondly,
      to describe such debts which may be due to anyone else other than the
      secured creditors.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 61 of 84
99. We may take a look at Section 2(g) of the RDB Act which reads thus:

               “2. Definitions.––

               (…)

               (g) “debt” means any liability (inclusive of interest) which
               is claimed as due from any person or a pooled investment
               vehicle as defined in clause (da) of section 2 of the
               Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956, by a bank or
               a financial institution or by a consortium of banks or
               financial institutions during the course of any business
               activity undertaken by the bank or the financial institution
               or the consortium under any law for the time being in
               force, in cash or otherwise, whether secured or unsecured,
               or assigned, or whether payable under a decree or order
               of any civil court or any arbitration award or otherwise or
               under a mortgage and subsisting on, and legally
               recoverable on, the date of the application and includes
               any liability towards debt securities which remains unpaid
               in full or part after notice of ninety days served upon the
               borrower by the debenture trustee or any other authority
               in whose favour security interest is created for the benefit
               of holders of debt securities or; ”

                                                             (Emphasis supplied)

100. A bare textual reading of the aforesaid provision indicates that the term

      ‘debt’ as defined under Section 2(g) of the RDB Act includes only such dues

      that are claimed by banks or financial institutions during the course of

      banking activity. Therefore, there is no manner of doubt in our minds that

      the expression “debts due to any secured creditor” includes those debts that

      fall within the definition under Section 2(g) of the RDB Act. However, could

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 62 of 84
      it be said that the term ‘debt’ appearing in the expression “all other debts”
      includes only those dues which can be claimed by banks and financial
      institutions involved in banking? In our considered view, the answer to the
      aforesaid question must be an emphatic ‘No’.

101. We say so because Section 26E seeks to give priority to the debts due to
      secured creditors over “all other debts”. If we restrain “all other debts” to
      those that fall within the definition provided under Section 2(g) of the RDB
      Act read with Section 2(ha) of the SARFAESI Act, then we may be
      narrowing down the scope of the priority clause under Section 26E of the
      SARFAESI Act without any legislative indication to that effect. We do not
      see any reason for the legislature to restrict the priority provision to only
      those “other debts” which may be due to banks or financial institutions,
      especially when Section 26E also prioritizes the interests of the secured
      creditor over “all revenues, taxes, cesses and other rates payable to the
      Central Government or State Government or local authority”. We say so
      because the amounts due to the government or crown debts are also not such
      debts that arise during the course of banking activities. If secured debts are
      prioritized over crown debts (non-banking debts), then to say that they will
      not be prioritized over such “other debts” that are also non-banking debts,
      would be erroneous in our considered opinion, especially when there is no
      indication by the legislature to that effect.

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 63 of 84
102. Therefore, with all humility at our command, we disagree with the
      observation in NSEL (supra) that Section 26E of the SARFAESI Act is
      inapplicable insofar as the ‘recovery of the deposits’ under the MPID Act is
      concerned, as the same cannot be said to be ‘debts’ which are covered under
      Section 26E. While we are in complete agreement with the first prong of the
      decision in NSEL (supra), that the priority clause under Section 26E would
      come into effect only when the security interest has been registered in terms
      of Section 26D of the SARFAESI Act, we do not find ourselves in
      agreement with the second prong of its analysis of this aspect, that the debts
      of the secured creditor would not be discharged in priority over the
      repayment of deposits under the MPID Act.

103. However, we may also clarify with a view to obviate any confusion that the
      factual conspectus in which the aforesaid observation was made in NSEL
      (supra) was different from the one before us. The facts therein required this
      Court to decide the question of priority of interest of secured creditors who
      asserted priority of repayment during insolvency proceedings against the
      debtor and prayed that the properties attached under the MPID Act therein
      be included in the insolvency pool. We do not know, for certain, whether the
      properties therein were securitized. Therefore, our disagreement as regards
      the finding in NSEL (supra) that the ‘repayment of deposits’ under the
      MPID Act cannot be included within ‘all other debts’ appearing in Section

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 64 of 84
      26E of the SARFAESI Act is a general observation. In our considered view,
      this aspect requires further deliberation and clarification by a larger bench.

      • ‘Priority of interest’ of secured creditors vis-à-vis the right of first
          charge for other statutory dues.

104. Having raised our doubts regarding the aforesaid aspect, we find it apposite
      to deal with the question of ‘priority of interest’. The non-obstante clause in
      Section 26E is worded broadly and provides overriding effect in relation to
      any other law for the time being in force. On the other hand, a perusal of the
      MPID Act indicates no such provision that would expressly provide the
      depositors with the ‘right of first charge’ over the attached property of the
      Financial Establishment or any other person. In other words, a juxtaposition
      of the SARFAESI Act and the MPID Act as regards the question of ‘priority
      of interest’ shows that the claim of the secured creditors would be prioritized
      over the claims of the depositors under the MPID Act.

105. We say so in light of the settled position of law that the dues of any person
      or authority that become payable as a result of statutory right may be
      prioritized over the debts due to the secured creditor when such statute
      creates the right of first charge in respect of such claims. In other words,
      when repayment is sought from the attachment of property and enforcement
      of rights therein as provided by way of a statutory enactment, the same is
      subservient to the enforcement of security rights and repayment under the

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 65 of 84
      SARFAESI Act unless and until the statute so enacted provides clear priority

      over the repayment to secured creditors under the SARFAESI Act and RDB

      Act.

106. The observations of this Court in Jalgaon District Central Coop. Bank Ltd.

      v. State of Maharashtra, reported in 2025 SCC OnLine SC 2513, provide

      sufficient clarity on the question whether the statutory first charge provision

      prevails over the security rights sought to be enforced under the SARFAESI

      Act. The relevant portion of the judgment in Jalgaon District Central Coop.

      Bank (supra) reads thus:

               “25. Hence, when there are two enactments conferring

               the time in which the statute was enacted or the provision
               was incorporated, assumes significance and the provision
               latter in time would prevail. However, if there is a first

               clause conferring priority over other debts, the statutory

107. A careful perusal of the aforesaid exposition reveals two principles to be
      adhered to when looking at the question of ‘priority of interest’:
       i. Where there are two enactments containing non obstante clauses
             which conflict with each other, the later enactment ordinarily prevails.
       ii. However, where the competing enactments deal with priority in the
             satisfaction of debts, the above principle does not automatically apply.
             The Court must first ascertain whether a statutory first charge has been
             validly created dehors the non obstante clause. If such a statutory first
             charge, dehors the non obstante clause, exists, it will prevail. In other
             words, a non obstante clause conferring priority in recovery must
             yield to a statutory provision independently creating a first charge.

111. At the outset, we may clarify with a view to obviate any confusion that the
      aforesaid exposition predated the insertion of Section 26E of the SARFAESI
      Act. One may then question why reference has been made to the
      observations in SICOM (supra). Our answer is simple – this Court, in the
      said decision, observed in clear terms that the debts which are secured would
      always constitute a first charge over the assets in question and relegated even
      crown debt to the status of an unsecured debt over which the repayment of
      secured debt shall be given priority. This position preceded the insertion of
      Section 26E which, in light of the dictum in SICOM (supra), can be said to
      be the codification of an already existing principle.

112. The concurrent reading of this Court’s decisions in Jalgaon District Central
      Coop. Bank (supra), Punjab National Bank (supra) and SICOM (supra)
      would indicate that to ensure primacy of the repayment of any other dues
      over and above the recovery of secured debts, the enactment of a statutory
      provision to that effect is a requisite. What has been conveyed by this Court

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 71 of 84
      in so many words is that such provision must create an independent right of
      first charge, couched in language so emphatic as to expressly highlight the
      legislature's intention to prioritize the amounts due under the statute over the
      repayment of secured debts under the SARFAESI Act.

113. On a plain reading of the MPID Act, it is limpid that no such independent
      right of first charge has been created that would provide priority to the
      recovery of deposits thereunder by way of attachment of the property. On
      the other hand, the Parliament has codified the position of law expounded in
      SICOM (supra) by including Section 26E in the SARFAESI Act, which
      mandates that priority be given to the recovery of debts due to secured
      creditors. Even otherwise, had the Parliament not inserted Section 26E, the
      repayment of secured debts ought to have been prioritized in light of the
      decision in SICOM (supra).

114. In such view of the matter, we can say without any manner of doubt in our
      minds that the repayment of the secured debt of the appellant herein must
      necessarily be prioritized over the ‘recovery of deposits’ under the MPID
      Act, which in our considered opinion fall in the category of unsecured debts.

115. We may, at this juncture, revisit the questions that we sought to address
      ourselves on in paragraph 63(c) of this judgment. We observed that if the
      conflict between the implementation of the recovery mechanisms under the

      SARFAESI Act and MPID Act is not possible by harmonious construction,
      then we shall delve into the question of which enactment is the dominant
      legislation.

116. In our considered view, Part III of this exposition sufficiently clarifies that
      a harmonious construction of the statutory schemes of both the SARFAESI
      Act and the MPID Act is possible. Through such exposition, we have
      observed that priority must be given to the recovery of debts due to secured
      creditors under the SARFAESI Act.

117. However, we are also conscious that our observations in the aforesaid parts
      of this judgment may not be congruent with the dictum of the two-Judge
      bench of this Court in NSEL (supra). Therefore, for a comprehensive
      analysis which may aid a larger bench in seisin of the issues addressed
      herein, we may look at the question of primacy of SARFAESI Act over the
      MPID Act from one another angle. In other words, we find it apposite to
      also discuss the issue before us on the touchstone of the doctrine of dominant
      legislation.

IV. SARFAESI Act – the dominant legislation as regards ‘recovery of dues’.
118. This Court in ITC Limited (supra), observed that where an incidental

      encroachment by one legislation causes conflict with the other legislation,
      the courts must determine which enactment has been legislated by the

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 73 of 84
      dominant power. If the Parliamentary enactment is the dominant legislation

      and the State enactment has the effect of incidentally encroaching thereon,

      the principle of federal supremacy would require that the Central legislation

      prevail.

119. However, the principle of dominant legislation should not be taken to mean

      that Central legislation would, in all situations, be accorded primacy over

      State legislation in cases of incidental encroachment. The dictum in Dipak

      Debbarma (supra) clarifies that the federal structure of the Constitution can

      also nullify any incidental inroads that a Parliamentary enactment may make

      into a State legislation. Therefore, where it is determined by the courts that

      the dominant power is the State, the provisions of statute enacted by the State

      legislature would be given primacy. The relevant paragraphs of the

      judgment in Dipak Debbarma (supra) read thus:

               “13. The federal structure under the constitutional scheme
               can also work to nullify an incidental encroachment made
               by the parliamentary legislation on a subject of a State
               legislation where the dominant legislation is the State
               legislation. An attempt to keep the aforesaid constitutional
               balance intact and give a limited operation to the doctrine
               of federal supremacy can be discerned in the concurring

120. In light of the aforesaid expositions in ITC Limited (supra) and Dipak

      Debbarma (supra), we find it apposite to examine whether it is the

      SARFAESI Act or the MPID Act that is the dominant legislation. It is

      apposite to clarify that the overlap between the two enactments pertains to

      the ‘recovery of dues’, for the secured creditors under the SARFAESI Act

      on the one hand and, for the depositors under the MPID Act on the other

      hand. The crux of the conflict, however, remains the same – priority of

      recovery for which group of debtors.

121. Having identified the root cause of the conflict, we may refer with profit to

      the dictum of this Court in Delhi High Court Bar Assn. (supra) wherein it

      was observed that the recovery of dues is an essential function of any

      215. The observation in Delhi High Court Bar Assn. (supra) reads thus:

               “14. (…) Entry 45 of List I relates to “banking”. Banking
               operations would, inter alia, include accepting of loans
               and deposits, granting of loans and recovery of the debts
               due to the bank. There can be little doubt that under Entry
               45 of List I, it is Parliament alone which can enact a law
               with regard to the conduct of business by the banks.
               Recovery of dues is an essential function of any banking
               institution. In exercise of its legislative power relating to
               banking, Parliament can provide the mechanism by which
               monies due to the banks and financial institutions can be
               recovered.”

                                                             (Emphasis supplied)

122. Therefore, it is as clear as a noon day that the process of ‘recovery of dues’

      is essential to the activity of banking which falls directly within the

      legislative purview of the Parliament by virtue of Entry 45 of List I to the

      Schedule VII.

123. In our considered view, the aspect of ‘recovery of dues’ cannot be said to be

      as essential to Entries 1, 30 and 32 of List II to the Schedule VII, as it

      evidently is to Entry 45 of List I. In such view of the matter, could it be said

      that it is the State legislature which is the dominant power insofar as the

      aspect of ‘recovery of dues’ is concerned? In our considered opinion, the
      answer to the aforesaid question must be an emphatic ‘No’.

124. Therefore, the MPID Act, which has been enacted in exercise of the
      legislative powers of the State under Entries 1, 30 and 32 of List II, cannot
      be held to be the dominant legislation when compared with the SARFAESI
      Act or RDB Act, respectively, which have been enacted under Entry 45 of
      List I.

125. However, the aforesaid discussion in respect of the doctrine of dominant
      legislation is largely academic since, we have, in no uncertain terms,
      observed that the SARFAESI Act would overrise the provisions of the
      MPID Act, by harmonious construction of the statutory schemes of the two
      enactments.

126. As such, there remains no doubt in our minds that the priority over the
      amounts received from the attachment and enforcement of rights in the
      properties herein must go towards discharging the secured debt due to the
      appellant herein. It is only if surplus monies remain after satisfying the debt
      of the appellant that the deposits under the MPID Act can be recovered.

E. SUMMARY OF OBSERVATIONS
127. We may summarize our observations in the aforesaid parts of this judgment

      as follows:
      a) While both the SARFAESI Act and MPID Act have been legislated

            competently by the Parliament and the State legislature respectively,
            yet the incidental overlap as regards ‘recovery’ mechanisms presents a
            conflict when the two statutes are sought to be implemented
            simultaneously.

      b) The harmonious construction of the statutory provisions of the
            SARFAESI Act and the MPID Act respectively, indicates that the
            recovery of secured debts under the former must be prioritized over the
            recovery of deposits under the latter for the following reasons:

             • First, the non-obstante clause under the SARFAESI Act, having
                  been enacted at a later point in time, must provide overriding
                  effect insofar as the recovery provisions under the MPID Act are
                  concerned.

             • Secondly, the objective of speedy recovery of secured debts under
                  the SARFAESI Act must be given precedence over the objective
                  of recovery of deposits under the MPID Act, as the banks are
                  custodians of public monies. If speedy recovery of secured debts

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 79 of 84
            is made subservient to the recovery provisions under the MPID
            Act, it would have a detrimental impact on the interests of the
            public at large.
       • Lastly, though the non-obstante provision under Section 35 of the
            SARFAESI Act is equally matched by the non-obstante clause
            under Section 14 of the MPID Act, yet the insertion of Section
            26E in the SARFAESI Act strengthens the claim of priority in
            respect of recovery of secured debts. Section 26E codifies the
            position of law expounded in SICOM (supra) that the security
            interests of the secured creditors would always prevail over the
            interests of the unsecured creditors.

c) The SARFAESI Act is the dominant legislation insofar as the aspect of
      ‘recovery of dues’ is concerned. We have said so because ‘recovery of
      dues’ is essential to the activity of banking, which falls in the legislative
      field of the Parliament under Entry 45 of List I to the Schedule VII of
      the Constitution. Therefore, it is the Parliament that is the dominant
      power in this regard, and State legislation, i.e., the MPID Act, must
      bow to it.

d) As regards the applicability of Section 26E to situations in which
      recovery under the MPID Act is involved, we have, with all humility

SLP (Crl.) No. 6710 of 2026  Page 80 of 84
            at our command, disagreed with the dictum of this Court in NSEL
            (supra) wherein it was held that the ‘deposits’ under the MPID Act
            cannot be treated as ‘debt’ for the purposes of Section 26E. However,
            in our considered view, the ‘deposits’ under the MPID Act would
            squarely fall within the category of “all other debts” over which
            Section 26E provides priority to secured debts. As such, this aspect
            requires clarification by a larger bench of this Court.

      e) As regards the observation of this Court in NSEL (supra) that ‘recovery
            of deposits’ under the MPID Act would gain precedence over the
            recoveries under the SARFAESI Act and RDB Act, we find it apposite
            to observe that in our considered opinion, the resolution of conflict
            between the two legislations was not adequately approached. In all
            humility, we have sought to answer the questions that remained
            unresolved in NSEL (supra), i.e, the questions in respect of harmonious
            construction of the two statutes and dominance of legislation. We find
            it necessary to refer these aspects to the larger bench for clarification.

F. CONCLUSION
128. In view of what has been pointed out hereinabove, we are of the opinion

      that, for a comprehensive consideration and an authoritative pronouncement
      after taking into account all aspects, including those dealt with hereinabove,

      the matter needs to be placed before Hon’ble the Chief Justice of India to
      constitute an appropriate three-Judge Bench.

129. The three-Judge Bench constituted may address itself to the following
      questions:
      (i) Whether the ‘deposits’ sought to be recovered under the MPID Act
               would fall within the ambit of the expression “all other debts”
               appearing in Section 26E of the SARFAESI Act?

      (ii) Whether the harmonious construction of the statutory schemes of the
               SARFAESI Act and the MPID Act indicates that the ‘recovery of
               dues’ under the former should be given priority over the latter?

      (iii) Whether it is the Parliament which is the dominant power insofar as
               the aspect of ‘recovery of dues’ is concerned because the same is
               essential to the activity of banking which falls within the legislative
               field of the Union under Entry 45 of List I to the Schedule VII? In
               other words, whether the SARFAESI Act is the dominant legislation
               and, as such, would prevail over the MPID Act?

132. Before we close this matter, we would like to say something as regards the
      litigation which has unfolded before us. The SARFAESI Act was legislated
      and brought into effect in 2002, approximately three years after the MPID
      Act was enacted by the State of Maharashtra. As has been discussed in the
      aforesaid parts of this judgment, this fact by itself plays a vital role in
      determining which enactment overrides the other. However, we may,
      nevertheless point out, that despite the SARFAESI Act being enacted later
      in point of time, it does not provide any clarity as regards the competing
      priorities between the secured creditors under the SARFAESI Act vis-à-vis

      rise to several litigations including the matter before us.

      appropriate amendment to Section 26E of the SARFAESI Act whereby an
      explanation of the expression “all other debts” may be provided
      authoritatively. In our considered view, this would provide much clarity &
      respite to the banking and financial sector from the plethora of litigations
      that seek to judicially eradicate the ambiguity in this regard. We find it
