       THE IMPUGNED JUDGMENT

12. The High Court vide judgment dated 22.08.2025, on the basis
       of the principle that a decree is executed by the Court within
       whose jurisdiction the property is situate, held that DRT,
       Madurai alone has jurisdiction to execute the recovery
       certificate. The High Court concluded as follows: (i) the transfer
       of the original application to Chennai was at the instance of the
       Borrower, (ii) the grievance of the Borrower is not about the
       forum of adjudication but about the execution against the
       property outside the limits of DRT, Chennai, (iii) the issuance of
       the recovery certificate was well within the jurisdiction of DRT,
       Chennai, and (iv) for the execution of the recovery certificate,
       the same ought to have been transmitted to DRT, Madurai. The
       High Court, however, rejected the contention of the Borrower

                                                                                                                              8
       that the recovery certificate was vitiated as it had been issued
       against a deceased person, on the ground that the objection had
       been considered and rejected by the Recovery Officer and the
       legal representatives had subsequently been brought on record.
       Accordingly, the High Court partly allowed the Writ Petition. By
       an order dated 06.01.2026, this Court issued notice and stayed
       the operation of the impugned judgment.

IN RE: ISSUE NO. (i)
APPLICABILITY OF THE CODE TO RECOVERY PROCEEDINGS

17. We propose to deal with Issue No. (i) by adverting to the object
       and purpose of the enactment of the 1993 Act, the provisions of
       the 1993 Act and the decisions of this Court.

(a) Object and purpose of enactment of the 1993 Act :-
18. Prior to the enactment of the 1993 Act, the Banks and Financial

       Institutions were required to pursue recovery proceedings

                                                                                                                             15
       before the ordinary civil courts. The resulting delays and
       enormous pendency of litigation led to a substantial sum of
       public money remaining locked in unproductive assets, which
       adversely affected the financial system and the efficient
       utilisation of funds. The need for a specialised and expeditious
       mechanism for recovery of debts due to Banks and Financial
       Institutions had been recognised by the Tiwari Committee in
       1981, which recommended the establishment of special
       tribunals following a summary procedure. Subsequently, the
       Narasimham Committee in 1991 also emphasised the
       importance of a specialised adjudicatory forum and speedy
       recovery as an essential component of financial sector reforms.
19. The magnitude of the problem was evident from the fact that,
       as on 30.09.1990, more than 15 lakh cases filed by the public
       sector banks and about 304 cases filed by the financial
       institutions were pending in various courts. The amounts
       involved were approximately Rs.5622 crores due to the public
       sector banks and Rs.391 crores due to the financial
       institutions.
20. In this backdrop, Parliament enacted the 1993 Act with the
       principal object of establishing specialised Debts Recovery

                                                                                                                             16
       Tribunals and Debts Recovery Appellate Tribunals to ensure
       speedy adjudication and expeditious recovery of debts due to
       banks and financial institutions. The legislation was enacted to
       overcome the procedural delays inherent in ordinary civil
       litigation and to facilitate the productive circulation of public
       funds. It is not merely another forum for adjudication of the
       dues of the public sector banks and financial institutions, but
       is a specialised, expeditious and effective mechanism for
       adjudication and recovery of debts due to banks and financial
       institutions. The constitutional validity of the 1993 Act was
       upheld by this Court in Union of India v. Delhi High Court
       Bar Association11. The 1993 Act has since been amended from
       time to time to strengthen the recovery machinery, notably by
       Act No. 1 of 2000, Act No. 30 of 2004 and Act No. 44 of 2016.
       The last of these, inter alia, enlarged the modes of recovery
       available to the Recovery Officer and enabled the bank to file its
       application where the account in which the debt is outstanding
       is maintained.

24. The scheme of the 1993 Act brings out the following

       distinguishing features: (i) the Debts Recovery Tribunal is a

       statutory adjudicatory body and not a civil court, (ii) the

       proceedings before it are initiated by way of an application and

                                                                                                                             21
       not by institution of a suit, (iii) the procedure prescribed before
       the Tribunal is neither rigid nor elaborate, and the Tribunal is
       vested with the flexibility to regulate its own procedure subject
       to adherence to the principles of natural justice, (iv) the
       Tribunal, upon adjudication of the claim of a bank or a financial
       institution, issues a recovery certificate specifying the amount
       due, and (v) the Recovery Officer proceeds to recover the amount
       due in accordance with the provisions of the 1993 Act.
       Thus, the mechanism contemplated under the 1993 Act
       comprises two distinct stages, namely, adjudication of the claim
       by the Tribunal and execution of the recovery certificate through
       the Recovery Officer.
25. This Court in Grapco Industries Ltd. (supra), while
       interpreting the expression “shall not be bound by” used in
       Section 22 of the 1993 Act, held that the Code does not denude
       the Tribunal of the power to exercise powers analogous to those
       contained in the Code; rather, the Tribunal can travel beyond
       the Code subject to the requirement of compliance with natural
       justice. A two-Judge Bench of this Court in MSTC Ltd. (supra),
       while considering Sections 20, 22 and 34 of the 1993 Act,
       reiterated the aforesaid view and held that the 1993 Act is a

                                                                                                                             22
       complete and exhaustive code and the provisions of the Code
       cannot be imported where they are inconsistent with the
       statutory scheme. It was held that Order XLVII Rule 7 of the
       Code does not apply to proceedings before the Tribunal, as
       Section 20 of the 1993 Act provides an appeal against every
       order of the Tribunal. Another two-Judge Bench of this Court
       in Satyawati Tondon (supra) held that the 1993 Act had
       created a special procedural mechanism for speedy recovery
       and had “freed” the Tribunals and the Appellate Tribunals from
       the procedural constraints of the Code.
26. Thus, by taking into account the object of the 1993 Act, the
       relevant provisions of the 1993 Act and the decisions of this
       Court, it is axiomatic that the provisions of the Code which are
       inconsistent with the scheme of the 1993 Act cannot be
       imported into the 1993 Act so as to curtail the powers of the
       Tribunal. The principles of the Code may, however, be drawn
       upon to aid and enable the Tribunal to exercise its powers in
       consonance with the purpose and provisions of the 1993 Act.
       For the aforementioned reasons, Issue No. (i) is answered by
       holding that the provisions of the Code do not apply to the

                                                                                                                             23
execution proceedings under the 1993 Act so as to restrict the

powers of the Tribunal.

IN RE: ISSUE NO. (ii)  EXECUTION:  DISTINCT  TERRITORIAL
ADJUDICATION AND
FIELDS

27. Section 19(1) of the 1993 Act governs the territorial jurisdiction

of the Tribunal for entertaining an original application

instituted by a Bank or Financial Institution for recovery of its

dues. A plain reading of the provision makes it evident that the

situs of the mortgaged property is not one of the statutory

criteria for determining the territorial jurisdiction of the

Tribunal for entertaining an original application under Section

19(1) of the 1993 Act. The territorial jurisdiction of the Tribunal

is determined by reference to statutory parameters expressly

prescribed under Section 19(1) of the 1993 Act, which include

the location of the relevant bank branch or office, the residence

or place of business of the defendant, or the place where the

cause of action arises wholly or in part.

28. There is a conscious omission of the situs of the mortgaged

property as a criterion for determining the jurisdiction under

Section 19(1) of the 1993 Act, in contrast with the jurisdiction

of the Civil Court under Section 16 of the Code, wherein the

                                             24
       location of immovable property assumes significance for
       determining the territorial jurisdiction of the Civil Court. Thus,
       Section 19(1) contemplates a situation where the Tribunal may
       be required to deal with a property situated beyond its territorial
       limits. Section 19(13) of the 1993 Act, which enables the
       Tribunal to attach the property in the circumstances specified
       therein, also does not refer to the situs of the property. Section
       19(22) provides for issuance of the recovery certificate by the
       Presiding Officer who has exercised the jurisdiction under
       Section 19(1) of the 1993 Act. The recovery certificate may,
       therefore, relate to properties of the defendant situated within
       or outside the territorial limits of the Tribunal which issues it.
29. The adjudication of the claim of a bank or financial institution
       and the execution of the recovery certificate are two distinct
       stages, though forming part of one statutory process. The
       territorial jurisdiction of the Tribunal to adjudicate the claim of
       the bank or financial institution is governed by Section 19(1) of
       the 1993 Act, whereas the execution of the recovery certificate
       by the Recovery Officer is governed by Chapter V of the 1993
       Act, and its transmission to another Tribunal by Section 19(23).

                                                                                                                             25
       This Court in Allahabad Bank v. Canara Bank12 recognised

       that the adjudication of liability and the recovery of the amount

       by execution of the certificate fall, respectively, within the

       exclusive jurisdiction of the Tribunal and the Recovery Officer.

       The location of an immovable property is not a relevant criterion

       while adjudicating a claim under Section 19(1) of the 1993 Act,

       whereas the same may have an impact on the execution of the

       certificate of recovery. Thus, there is a distinction between the

       territorial jurisdiction of the Tribunal in adjudicating the claim

       of the bank or financial institution and the territorial reach of

       the execution of the recovery certificate by the Recovery Officer

       of the Tribunal. Accordingly, the Issue No. (ii) is answered.

IN RE: ISSUE NO. (iii)
SCOPE OF SECTION 19(23)

30. At this stage, it is apposite to take note of Section 19(23) of the

       1993 Act, which reads as under:-

                        “19(23)-Where the Tribunal, which has
                        issued a certificate of recovery, is
                        satisfied that the property is situated
                        within the local limits of the jurisdiction
                        of two or more Tribunals, it may send
                        the copies of the certificate of recovery
                        for execution to such other Tribunals
                        where the property is situated:

                                                                                                                             26
                          Provided that in a case where the
                          Tribunal to which the certificate of
                          recovery is sent for execution finds
                          that it has no jurisdiction to comply
                          with the certificate of recovery, it shall
                          return the same to the Tribunal
                          which has issued it.”
       (A) TEXTUAL FEATURES OF SECTION 19(23)
31. The provision has five textual features which bear upon its
       construction. First, the power is conferred only upon “the
       Tribunal, which has issued a certificate of recovery”. No other
       Tribunal can set the provision in motion, and the certificate
       holder cannot, by its own choice, determine whether the
       certificate is to be retained or transmitted. Secondly, the power
       arises when that Tribunal “is satisfied” as to the location of the
       property. Thirdly, what is sent are “the copies of the certificate
       of recovery”. The Tribunal issuing the recovery certificate
       retains the same and remains in seisin of the recovery
       proceeding. Transmission does not divest such Tribunal of its
       jurisdiction but enables execution to proceed, simultaneously if
       need be, in another territory. Fourthly, the certificate may be
       sent only “to such other Tribunals where the property is
       situated”. Fifthly, the proviso commands that a Tribunal to
       which the certificate is sent, if it “finds that it has no jurisdiction

                                                                                                                             27
       to comply with the certificate of recovery”, “shall return the
       same” to the Tribunal issuing the recovery certificate.
32. It is noteworthy that the expression “property” employed by the
       legislature in Section 19(23) of the 1993 Act is required to be
       read in the context of Section 13(2) of the General Clauses Act,
       1897, which provides that words in the singular include the
       plural, unless there is anything repugnant in the subject or
       context. There is nothing repugnant in Section 19(23). On the
       contrary, a recovery certificate ordinarily covers several
       properties of the certificate debtors, as it does in the present
       case. The word “property” must, therefore, be read as
       “properties”.
       (B) PURPOSIVE CONSTRUCTION
33. Upon careful consideration of the statutory scheme, it is evident
       that Section 19(23) of the 1993 Act contemplates a mechanism
       for facilitating the execution of a recovery certificate in a case
       involving properties falling within the territorial jurisdiction of
       more than one Tribunal. Section 19(23) does not expressly bar
       the jurisdiction of the Tribunal which has issued the recovery
       certificate in dealing with the property situate outside its
       territorial limits. The conclusion that the local limits of the

                                                                                                                             28
       Tribunal do not by themselves circumscribe the reach of its
       recovery mechanism is also the one which furthers the object of
       the 1993 Act. This Court has held that statutory Tribunals,
       though creatures of the statute, are vested by necessary
       implication with such incidental powers as are essential to give
       full effect to the jurisdiction expressly conferred on them.13 A
       statute must be construed in the light of its scheme and
       purpose, and not in a manner which defeats them.14 Therefore,
       Section 19(23) of the 1993 Act has to be construed in a manner
       which advances the object of the legislation, namely,
       expeditious and effective recovery of debts due to banks and
       financial institutions.
       (C) “MAY” VERSUS “SHALL”
34. Now we advert to the contention whether the word “may” in
       Section 19(23) of the 1993 Act can be read as “shall”. The words
       “may” and “shall” are not to be read in isolation. Whether a
       provision which uses the word “may” is discretionary or whether
       it casts a duty depends upon the context, the object of the
       enactment and the consequences which would follow from the

14 RBI v. Peerless General Finance & Investment Co. Ltd., (1987) 1 SCC 424

       one construction or the other.15 Ordinarily, “may” in a provision

       conferring a power signifies discretion. It is construed as “shall”

       only where the power is conferred for the benefit of a person

       who has a legal right to its exercise, so that the power is coupled

       with a duty to exercise it in a particular way. Equally, a

       provision which prescribes no consequence for its non-

       observance, and which relates to the convenience of

       administration, is ordinarily regarded as directory. In

       Bachahan Devi v. Nagar Nigam, Gorakhpur16, this Court

       summarised the position thus:

                     “18. … As a general rule, the word “may”
                     is permissive and operative to confer
                     discretion and especially so, where it is
                     used in juxtaposition to the word “shall”,

                     imposes a duty. Cases, however, are not
                     wanting where the words “may”, “shall”
                     and “must” are used interchangeably. In
                     order to find out whether these words are
                     being used in a directory or in a
                     mandatory sense, the intent of the
                     legislature should be looked into along
                     with the pertinent circumstances.”

       The same principle was reiterated in Vidarbha Industries

       Power Ltd. v. Axis Bank Ltd.17, where it was held that the

       the scope of the enactment and the consequences flowing from

35. Applying these tests, we find that the word "may" in Section
       19(23) cannot be read as "shall", for the following reasons:
       (i) The legislature has used "may" in the main part and "shall"

               in the proviso. The contrast shows that Parliament used
               the two words advisedly. Where it intended compulsion, it
               said so. The proviso compels the transferee Tribunal; it
               does not compel the issuing Tribunal to transmit.
       (ii) The 1993 Act does not provide for any consequence for
               non-transmission of the certificate. Had Parliament
               intended that execution by the issuing Tribunal against
               property beyond its limits would be a nullity, it would have
               said so in clear terms.
       (iii) The object of the 1993 Act is speedy recovery. A mandatory
               reading would compel the Tribunal to split the certificate
               among several Tribunals whenever the securities lie at
               different places, irrespective of the stage of execution or
               the steps already taken. This would cause delay, multiply

                                                                                                                             31
               proceedings, expose the process to avoidable objections
               and frustrate the object of the Act. Execution proceedings,
               as this Court has emphasised, must not be permitted to
               become a means of delaying the fruits of an adjudication.18
36. At the same time, the discretion conferred by Section 19(23) is
       not unfettered. It is a judicial discretion, which must be
       exercised according to the rules of reason and justice, not
       according to private opinion; according to law, and not
       humour19. It must be legal and regular, and not arbitrary, vague
       or fanciful20. It follows that the discretion vests in the Tribunal,
       that is, the Presiding Officer, and not in the Recovery Officer,
       and that it cannot be exercised by the certificate holder by the
       mere filing of an affidavit before the Recovery Officer.

       court which passed a decree could itself execute it against

       (4) of Section 39, inserted by Act No. 22 of 2002 with effect from

       conflict arose because the word “may” in Section 39 had been
       read by some High Courts as permitting such execution, and
       that the Commission recommended an express provision to the
       contrary. Where Parliament intended a territorial restriction on
       execution under the Code, it enacted one expressly. No such
       provision is found in the 1993 Act and, for the reasons assigned
       while answering Issue No. (i), it cannot be imported.
40. We may also notice why the submission of the learned Amicus
       Curiae cannot be accepted without qualification. If the issuing
       Tribunal could execute against properties anywhere as a matter
       of course, without any judicial consideration of the question,
       the choice would, in practice, rest with the certificate holder,
       since the forum of adjudication under Section 19(1) is chosen
       by it. The certificate debtor, and third parties such as tenants,
       occupants, claimants and intending purchasers, might be
       compelled to contest attachment, valuation, possession and
       sale before a distant forum, before which the appeal under
       Section 30 would also lie. The Recovery Officers of metropolitan
       Tribunals, where the bulk of the applications are filed, would

       borne by the certificate debtor. There would also be a risk of
       conflicting proceedings where the same property is under
       attachment by the Recovery Officer of the local Tribunal at the
       instance of another creditor. These difficulties are avoided not
       by reading “may” as “shall”, but by recognising that the
       discretion is a judicial discretion of the Tribunal, to be exercised
       on the parameters laid down hereinafter.
41. For the foregoing reasons, Section 19(23) of the 1993 Act
       permits the Tribunal to execute a recovery certificate issued by
       it in relation to a property situate outside its local limits, and
       that the word “may” in Section 19(23) confers a judicial
       discretion on the Tribunal to retain the execution or to transmit
       the certificate, which is to be exercised on the parameters set
       out hereinafter. In the light of the construction which we have
       placed on Section 19(23) of the 1993 Act, we approve the view
       taken by the Gujarat and Bombay High Courts, subject to the
       parameters which we lay down hereinafter, and do not agree
       with the view expressed by the High Courts of Delhi, Punjab &
       Haryana and Calcutta.

IN RE : ISSUE NO. (iv)
PARAMETERS FOR RETENTION OR TRANSMISSION

   (A) RELEVANT CONSIDERATIONS
43. In determining whether the recovery certificate should be

       transmitted to another Tribunal or whether the execution
       should be continued by the Recovery Officer attached to the
       issuing Tribunal, several relevant considerations would
       necessarily arise. The question is not merely one of territorial
       jurisdiction. It involves several practical and procedural
       considerations which may affect the efficacy of the recovery
       mechanism. For instance, the Recovery Officer attached to the
       Debts Recovery Tribunal at Delhi may face difficulties in
       proceeding against a property situated in Mumbai, especially
       when physical possession, removal of occupants and delivery of
       possession to the auction purchaser are required.
44. There is a difference between the jurisdiction of the Tribunal
       and the territorial reach of the Recovery Officers. Where
       different properties are situated in different jurisdictions,
       separate Recovery Officers may become involved, which may
       give rise to difficulties concerning the priority and sequence of
       attachment, common valuation standards, coordination of

                                                                                                                             37
       auction dates, disclosure of amounts realised, adjustment of
       partial recoveries, calculation of the outstanding balance and
       prevention of recovery exceeding the amount specified in the
       recovery certificate.
45. A property may also be subject to multiple claims and
       proceedings, including prior attachments, claims of other
       secured creditors, statutory dues, claims of tenants or
       occupants, pending litigation, and insolvency or liquidation
       proceedings. A Recovery Officer situated in one State may
       encounter practical difficulties in taking possession of a
       property situated in another State. Local police assistance,
       revenue records, municipal records, physical inspection and
       identification of occupants may be required. These practical
       difficulties may justify transmission or local execution
       assistance, depending upon the facts of the case.
46. The practical consideration in transmitting a recovery certificate
       for execution would be to ensure that the transmission would
       facilitate the possession, valuation and sale of the assets more
       effectively. The practical and procedural difficulties can be
       understood with reference to the following illustrations: -

                                                                                                                             38
Illustration (A): A bank obtains a recovery certificate for Rs.100
crores from the Debts Recovery Tribunal at Mumbai. The
certificate debtor owns (1) a commercial building in Chennai,
which is let out to several tenants; (2) an industrial unit in
Bengaluru, which is a running concern; and (3) a vacant
residential plot in Hyderabad. All three properties are available
for recovery. The Tribunal, in such a case, should consider
whether separate execution proceedings are necessary or
whether a coordinated execution can be undertaken through
the Recovery Officer of the issuing Tribunal with appropriate
local assistance. The vacant plot at Hyderabad may well be sold
by the Recovery Officer at Mumbai with local assistance. The
tenanted building in Chennai, which may involve claims of
tenants and proceedings for possession at a great distance, may
call for transmission to the Tribunal having jurisdiction there.
The industrial unit at Bengaluru may require a decision
whether it should be sold as a going concern. If separate
Tribunals are entrusted with execution, the issuing Tribunal
should ensure that: (i) the total recoverable amount is clearly
specified; (ii) each executing authority is informed of the
amounts realised by the others; (iii) the outstanding liability is

                                                                                                                   39
periodically reconciled; (iv) there is no duplication of recovery;
and (v) the interests of all the creditors and affected parties are
protected.
Illustration (B): A large industrial undertaking consists of
contiguous parcels of land situated partly within the territorial
jurisdiction of the Debts Recovery Tribunal at Mumbai, and
partly within the territorial jurisdiction of another Tribunal. The
undertaking may be commercially indivisible, and its separate
sale may substantially diminish its value. In such a case, the
Tribunal must consider whether the properties are capable of
being treated as one integrated asset for execution purposes
and whether separate execution proceedings would lead to
conflicting orders or diminution in value. Ordinarily, such an
undertaking would be best sold as a whole by a single executing
authority.
Illustration (C): A property situated in another State is vacant,
undisputed and identifiable. In such a case, where the
execution only requires valuation, attachment and sale, the
same can be coordinated through the Recovery Officer and the
local authorities, and no transmission of the recovery certificate
may be required.

                                                                                                                   40
       Illustration (D): The recovery certificate is issued by the Debts
       Recovery Tribunal at Chennai. The only property available is the
       residential house of the certificate debtor at Coimbatore, in
       which he resides with his family. A co-owner disputes the extent
       of the debtor's share, and a third-party claims to be in
       possession as a tenant. The execution would require local
       inquiry into the claims, eviction of the occupants and police
       assistance. In such a case, the Tribunal would ordinarily
       transmit the certificate to the Tribunal within whose limits the
       house is situated.
       Illustration (E): The property covered by the certificate has
       already been attached at the instance of another creditor by the
       Recovery Officer of the Tribunal within whose limits it is
       situated. The issuing Tribunal should ordinarily transmit the
       certificate, so that the competing claims are dealt with by one
       executing authority and conflicting orders are avoided.
47. The Tribunal must examine whether the property is situate
       wholly or partly within the territorial jurisdiction of another
       Tribunal, whether the execution proceedings require physical
       intervention at the location of the property, whether
       attachment, possession, valuation and sale can be effectively

                                                                                                                             41
       undertaken through the existing recovery machinery, and
       whether transmission would facilitate execution or, instead,
       result in avoidable multiplicity and delay. It would also be
       necessary to bear in mind that, where the properties forming
       part of the same recovery certificate are situate in different
       territorial jurisdictions, uncoordinated execution proceedings
       may result in duplication of efforts, inconsistent orders and
       difficulties in accounting for the amounts realised. The
       statutory mechanism should, therefore, be administered in a
       manner which ensures coordination amongst the executing
       authorities and prevents recovery in excess of the amount
       specified in the recovery certificate.
48. We are, therefore, of the considered view that the discretion
       under Section 19(23) of the 1993 Act must be exercised upon
       an objective assessment of the relevant circumstances,
       supported by reasons. Neither the geographical location of the
       property, viewed in isolation, nor the convenience of one of the
       parties can constitute the sole basis for determining the
       appropriate course of execution. The paramount consideration
       must be whether the proposed course would facilitate lawful,
       effective and expeditious enforcement of the recovery certificate,

                                                                                                                             42
       with due regard to the rights of the certificate debtor and of third
       parties.
49. The ultimate object of the provision is to ensure that territorial
       considerations do not frustrate or unduly impede the recovery
       mechanism, while simultaneously ensuring that execution is
       undertaken by an authority competent in law to do so, and in a
       manner which is fair to the certificate debtor.
 (B) THE STRUCTURED TEST
50. A structured test which could be applied by every Tribunal
       whenever the question of transmission of a recovery certificate
       arises can be stated as follows:
       (i) The Tribunal should determine the exact location, the nature

            of ownership of the property and the question of territorial
            jurisdiction.
       (ii) The Tribunal must identify the nature of the execution,
            namely, it should ascertain whether the same requires
            attachment, possession, appointment of a receiver, valuation
            or sale, and whether the property is occupied, tenanted, the
            residential house of the certificate debtor, or the subject of
            claims by third parties or of attachment by another Tribunal.

                                                                                                                             43
       (iii) The Tribunal should examine whether its existing
            Recovery Officer can effectively execute the certificate with
            appropriate local assistance.

       (iv) The Tribunal must consider the balance of convenience,
            expedition, cost, avoidance of multiplicity, the stage of the
            execution, the conduct of the parties and protection against
            prejudice to any of the parties or to third parties.

       (v) The Tribunal must pass a reasoned order while deciding the
            issue whether the execution should be retained or
            transmitted.

   (C) SAFEGUARDS
51. In addition, the Debts Recovery Tribunals shall observe the

       following safeguards:
       (i) The decision under Section 19(23) shall be taken by the

            Tribunal ordinarily at the time of issuing the recovery
            certificate or at the earliest thereafter, and in any event
            before any coercive step is taken against a property situated
            outside the limits of the Tribunal.
       (ii) The decision shall be taken after notice to the certificate
            holder and the certificate debtors. Either party may apply for

                                                                                                                             44
            such a decision, and the Tribunal may also act on its own
            motion. Such an application shall be decided expeditiously.
       (iii) The Recovery Officer attached to the Tribunal which has
            issued the recovery certificate shall scrupulously comply
            with Chapter V of the 1993 Act and the Second Schedule to
            the Income-tax Act, 1961.
       Accordingly, Issue No. (iv) is answered.
IN RE: ISSUE NO. (v)
APPLICATION TO THE PRESENT CASE
52. In the instant case, the Bank had filed an application under
       Section 19 of the 1993 Act before the DRT, Madurai. On the
       application of the Borrower, the proceedings were transferred to
       DRT-III, Chennai. The DRT, Chennai allowed the claim of the
       Bank and issued the recovery certificate, which was forwarded
       to the Recovery Officer attached to it. Admittedly, the properties
       are situated at Tiruchirappalli, within the territorial jurisdiction
       of DRT, Madurai, and it is not in dispute that the Borrower, and
       in the case of the Schedule A property the legal representatives
       of his late wife, are the owners of the same.
53. Both the properties are situated in a single district in the same
       State, and the proclamation of sale is to be made in the same
       language. The Recovery Officer, DRT, Chennai has already

                                                                                                                             45
attached both the properties, registered the attachments with
the Sub-Registrar, K. Sathanur, and taken possession of the
Schedule B property on 06.01.2023. There is no material on
record to indicate that either property is in occupation of a third
party, that it is the subject of any prior attachment by another
Tribunal, or that it has been encroached upon by anyone. The
identity of the properties is not in dispute. The original
application was instituted at Madurai and was transferred to
Chennai at the instance of the Borrower himself, and the
proceedings have been pending for sixteen years. What remains
is the valuation and sale of the properties and possession of the
Schedule A property, which is the residential house of the
Borrower and his family. These steps can be carried out by the
Recovery Officer attached to DRT, Chennai with appropriate
local assistance, subject to the safeguards laid down above and
to the protection of the legal representative who is stated to have
an intellectual disability. Therefore, the High Court erred in
holding that DRT, Madurai alone has jurisdiction and in
directing transmission of the recovery certificate to the DRT,
Madurai. Accordingly, Issue No. (v) is answered in the negative.

                                                                                                                   46
       It is held that the impugned judgment passed by the High Court
       cannot be sustained.
       OTHER CONTENTIONS OF THE BORROWER
54. Insofar as the submission made by the Borrower, who appeared
       in person, that the Recovery Officer of DRT, Chennai lacked
       territorial jurisdiction, is concerned, suffice it to say that a
       person who takes such a plea must also demonstrate that the
       lack of territorial jurisdiction has resulted in prejudice to him
       and in a failure of justice.21 The Borrower was unable to
       demonstrate any prejudice caused to him, particularly when it
       was at his own instance that the proceedings were transferred
       to Chennai. The Borrower has also raised the following
       contentions, namely, (i) the recovery certificate is void as it was
       issued after the death of his wife, (ii) the proceedings abated
       since the legal representatives were not brought on record
       within time, (iii) the proceedings are a nullity qua a legal
       representative who is said to have a certified intellectual
       disability of 70% and for whom no guardian was appointed, and
       (iv) the proceedings are vitiated by fraud. The first of these
       contentions was considered and rejected by the High Court, and

v. Oriental Insurance Co. Ltd., (2009) 2 SCC 244

       the Borrower has not challenged that finding before this Court;
       a review application filed by him is stated to be pending before

       the impugned judgment, which is confined to the question of

       CONCLUSIONS
55. We summarise our conclusions as follows:
       (i) The provisions of the Code do not apply to execution
       proceedings under the 1993 Act so as to restrict the powers of
       the Tribunal.

                                                                                                                             48
(ii) The territorial jurisdiction of the Tribunal to adjudicate is
governed by Section 19(1), whereas the execution of the
recovery certificate is governed by Chapter V and its
transmission by Section 19(23) of the 1993 Act. Thus, there is
a distinction between the territorial jurisdiction of the Tribunal
and the territorial reach of the Recovery Officer.
(iii) The Tribunal which has issued the recovery certificate has
the authority to execute the same in relation to properties
situated outside its local limits. The word “property” in Section
19(23) includes “properties”, and the provision is attracted
whenever any property covered by the certificate is situated
outside those limits.
(iv) The word "may" in Section 19(23) cannot be read as "shall".
It confers a judicial discretion on the Tribunal, and not an
option on the certificate holder or the Recovery Officer.
(v) The Tribunals should be guided by the structured test and
safeguards laid down by us in paragraphs 50 and 51 of this
judgment while deciding the issue of transmission of recovery
certificates.
(vi) The decisions in Balbir Kumar Paul (supra), Tushar P.
Shah (supra) and GSL (India) Ltd. (supra) of the Gujarat and

                                                                                                                   49
       Bombay High Courts correctly interpret Section 19(23) of the
       1993 Act, subject to the parameters laid down by us, and we
       approve of the view taken by them. The decisions in Amish Jain
       (supra), Harjinder Singh (supra) and Bizmart Projects (supra)
       of the Delhi High Court, Punjab & Haryana High Court and
       Calcutta High Court do not lay down the correct law.
       DIRECTIONS
56. For the reasons aforesaid, we hold that the High Court erred in
       holding that DRT, Madurai alone has jurisdiction to execute the
       recovery certificate. DRT, Chennai, which adjudicated the claim
       of the Bank on the transfer of the proceedings and which issued
       the recovery certificate, is competent to execute it through its
       Recovery Officer against the mortgaged properties at
       Tiruchirappalli, and its retention of the execution is supported
       by a proper and just nexus.
57. We accordingly pass the following order:
       (i) The judgment dated 22.08.2025 of the High Court of

            Judicature at Madras, Madurai Bench in W.P. (MD) No. 5840
            of 2023 is quashed and set aside.

                                                                                                                             50
       (ii) The recovery proceedings in pursuance of Recovery
            Certificate No. 14 of 2022, including the attachment order
            dated 13.01.2023 and all consequential steps, are restored.

       (iii) The Recovery Officer, DRT-III, Chennai shall proceed with
            the execution in accordance with law from the stage at which
            it was interdicted, in compliance with the structured tests
            and the safeguards laid down in paragraphs 50 and 51
            above, and shall endeavour to complete it expeditiously,
            preferably within six months from the date of receipt of a
            copy of this judgment.

58. In the result, the appeal is allowed. There shall be no order as
       to costs.
