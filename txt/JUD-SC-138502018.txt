2. The present batch of appeals arises from judgments of the High
      Courts of Delhi, Calcutta and Telangana. Though the matters
      spring from distinct factual settings, they converge upon a common
      question: the composition of the Adjudicating Authority (AA) under
      the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 (PMLA), and its
      competence to discharge its functions through a single Member,
      more particularly one who is not a Judicial Member. The same
      question is raised in Writ Petition (Civil) No. 186/2025, instituted
      under Article 32 of the Constitution by one Mr. Pavel
      Aleksandrovich Prozorov.

3. The law against money laundering is, by its nature, a law of
      pursuit. It follows money through every disguise it is made to wear,
      across accounts, entities and borders, until the proceeds of crime
      can be traced back to their source. A law of pursuit must
      necessarily be a law of reach, and the PMLA does not want for it.
      Property may be attached, premises searched, assets frozen and
      persons arrested, often upon a belief recorded in writing by a single
      officer, and well before any court has had occasion to pronounce
      upon guilt.

                                                                                                                       Page 2 of 55
4. A statute that permits the State to reach into a citizen’s estate
      before guilt is proved must, in the same breath, tell the citizen
      before whom he may answer. The PMLA operates through the AA.
      A person whose property has been attached first presents their case
      before the AA. Throughout the investigation or trial, the AA’s order
      determines whether that person retains their property or must
      surrender possession to the Union. Put simply, the PMLA casts a
      broad net and the AA must determine what that net may lawfully
      encompass.

5. The authority of such a forum is conferred by the statute. Its
      credibility, however, must be earned by the competence of those
      who sit in it. The present batch asks whether that competence is
      assured when the AA speaks through a single Member who is not
      drawn from the field of law. The answer depends on what the AA is
      actually called upon to do: whether it is merely the keeper of the
      sealed cover, or whether it must also hold the scales.

6. The present batch tests the second of these aspects. It asks whether
      the AA may discharge its functions through a single Member who
      does not have legal background. The answer, as we shall explain,
      does not lie in the text of Section 6 alone. It turns on a closer
      understanding of what the AA is actually called upon to do: whether
      it is merely the keeper of the sealed cover, or whether it must also

                                                                                                                       Page 3 of 55
        hold the scales; and, if it does both, whether the same answer must
        govern each. It is with these considerations in view that we turn to
        the facts.

        the partners of the firm and certain unidentified bank officials. It

7.8. By its judgment dated 11.01.2018, reported as J. Sekar v. Union

        following terms:

        (i) The writ petitions were maintainable before the Delhi High

              a Bench thereof, for the discharge of its functions under the
              PMLA; and

C. ISSUES FOR CONSIDERATION
11. We have heard learned Senior Counsel and learned counsel for the

12. We must note at the outset that although the Union of India has

      Delhi High Court and on the legal necessity for the AA to forward

      Section 5(1), neither side has advanced substantive submissions
      on these aspects. This is all the more so because the Respondent
      in that appeal, J. Sekar, has not entered appearance before this
      Court. Moreover, since the filing of that Special Leave Petition in
      2018, this Court has upheld the vires of the second proviso to
      Section 5(1) in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary (supra), with specific
      reference to J. Sekar (supra). In these circumstances, we consider
      it appropriate to refrain from expressing any opinion on these

       i. Whether the AA can discharge its functions through a single,

D. ANALYSIS
D.1 The Legislative Architecture of the Adjudicating Authority
14. Before we construe the provisions of the PMLA in the light of rival

      contentions, it is necessary to understand the place that the AA
      occupies within the scheme of the statute.

15. The PMLA was enacted to prevent money laundering and to provide
      for the confiscation of the monies and properties involved in it. The
      identification, securing and eventual confiscation of such property
      is, as the objects of the Act themselves make clear, central to its
      design. It is to this end that Section 6 constitutes the AA as a civil

                                                                                                                     Page 18 of 55
      adjudicatory authority, entrusted with various functions in relation

      to the properties, assets and records seized or attached by the ED

      in exercise of its powers under the same statute.

16. As originally enacted, the PMLA contemplated more than one AA,

      the Central Government being empowered to appoint ‘one or more

      Adjudicating Authorities’. The legislative design, evidently, was that

      there could be several AAs, each consisting of a Chairperson and

      two Members.

17. That design was altered by the Prevention of Money Laundering

      (Amendment) Act, 2009 (2009 Amendment), which replaced every

      reference to multiple AAs with the singular. There is, therefore, now

      only one Adjudicating Authority. Section 6, as it presently stands,

      reads as follows:

              “6. Adjudicating Authorities, composition, powers, etc.–
              (1) The Central Government shall, by notification, appoint an
              Adjudicating Authority to exercise jurisdiction, powers and
              authority conferred by or under this Act.
              (2) An Adjudicating Authority shall consist of a
              Chairperson and two other Members:
              Provided that one Member each shall be a person having
              experience in the field of law, administration, finance or
              accountancy.
              (3) A person shall, however, not be qualified for appointment as
              Member of an Adjudicating Authority,—

                     (a) in the field of law, unless he—
                            (i) is qualified for appointment as District Judge; or
                            (ii) has been a member of the Indian Legal Service and
                            has held a post in Grade I of that service;

                     (b) in the field of finance, accountancy or administration
                     unless he possesses such qualifications, as may be
                     prescribed.

                                                                                                                     Page 19 of 55
              (4) The Central Government shall appoint a Member to be
              the Chairperson of the Adjudicating Authority.
              (5) Subject to the provisions of this Act,—

                     (a) the jurisdiction of the Adjudicating Authority may
                     be exercised by Benches thereof;
                     (b) a Bench may be constituted by the Chairperson of
                     the Adjudicating Authority with one or two Members
                     as the Chairperson of the Adjudicating Authority may
                     deem fit;
                     (c) the Benches of the Adjudicating Authority shall
                     ordinarily sit at New Delhi and at such other places
                     as the Central Government may, in consultation with
                     the Chairperson, by notification, specify;
                     (d) the Central Government shall, by notification,
                     specify the areas in relation to which each Bench of
                     the Adjudicating Authority may exercise jurisdiction.
              (6) Notwithstanding anything contained in sub-section (5),
              the Chairperson may transfer a Member from one Bench to
              another Bench.
              (7) If at any stage of the hearing of any case or matter it
              appears to the Chairperson or a Member that the case or
              matter is of such a nature that it ought to be heard by a
              Bench consisting of two Members, the case or matter may
              be transferred by the Chairperson or, as the case may be,
              referred to him for transfer, to such Bench as the
              Chairperson may deem fit.
              (8) The Chairperson and every Member shall hold office as such
              for a term of five years from the date on which he enters upon
              his office: Provided that no Chairperson or other Member shall
              hold office as such after he has attained the age of sixty-five

D.2 The Text of Section 6: Who May Sit, and in What Number
21. The submissions of the parties proceed on two planes: the textual

      import of Section 6 of the PMLA, and the necessity of legal
      objectivity in the functioning of AA. We consider it prudent to
      address the former first, since it is the text of the statute that marks
      the outer limits within which any further inquiry must proceed.

22. Nothing in the PMLA or in the 2007 Rules requires the Chairperson
      of the AA to be drawn from the field of law. The contrast with the
      provisions that governed the AT is instructive. Section 6(3) merely
      prescribes the eligibility of the Member from the field of law, and
      Section 6(4) does not require that such Member alone be appointed
      as the Chairperson. Section 28(1), on the other hand, as it stood
      before Sections 27 and 28 were omitted by the Finance Act, 2016,
      specifically provided that only a person who has been, or is
      qualified to be, a Judge of the Supreme Court or of a High Court
      could be appointed as the Chairperson of the AT. Where Parliament
      intended the head of an adjudicatory body under this very Statute
      to be a judicially trained person, it has said so in categorical terms.
      Its silence in the case of the AA is, therefore, not an inadvertent

                                                                                                                     Page 22 of 55
      omission on the part of the draftsman, but a conscious and
      deliberate legislative choice.

23. As to the constitution of a ‘Bench’ under Section 6(5)(b), the Private
      Appellants have strenuously argued that the expression ‘one or two
      Members’ refers only to the other Members, and is exclusive of, and
      in addition to, the Chairperson. The Union of India and the ED, per
      contra, contend that the expression means that a Bench may
      consist of ‘one or two Members’ in all, with or without the
      Chairperson. In essence, the Private Appellants claim and the
      Official Respondents repel the submission that ‘Bench’ cannot
      mean a Single-Member Bench.

24. Such contention of the Private Appellants, however, fails to pass
      muster, both literally and contextually.

25. On the first count, we must observe that the word ‘Bench’ does not
      always signify a panel of judges. It can, in appropriate cases, also
      refer to a Single-Member Bench. The Oxford Advanced Learners
      Dictionary,5 inter alia, defines the word as “a judge in court or the
      seat where he/she sits; the position of being a judge or magistrate”.
      It is also described as “the raised area occupied by the judge in its
      official capacity” or “the court considered in its official capacity” by

5 Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary (11th edn, OUP 2026).

                                                              Page 23 of 55
      the Black’s Law Dictionary.6 The import of the term ‘Bench’,

      evidently, is towards the position and role of the Court or other

      adjudicatory body, not to plurality of judges executing such role.

      Hence, in a lexicographical sense, it cannot be said that the use of

      the expression ‘Bench’ would invariably denote multiple members

      of a Tribunal. This understanding, in the context of the Rules of the

      High Court at Patna, was approved by a coordinate Bench of this

      Court vide judgement in Rajendra Prasad Jain v. Sheel Bhadra

      Yajee,7 holding as follows:

              “2. … The first is that the word “Bench” used in the referring
              order cannot be interpreted as necessarily indicating that the
              case must be laid before two or more Judges. In this connection,
              the language of Rule 1(xi) and Rule 3 of Chapter II of the Rules of
              the High Court at Patna is significant. Under Rule 1(xi), a case
              under the Indian Companies Act is to be heard by a Single Judge;
              and Rule 3 indicates the nature of one of the orders which can
              be passed by a Bench hearing the case under Rule 1(xi). Thus,
              in Rule 3 of the Rules of the High Court at Patna itself a Single
              Judge is referred to as a Bench. In fact, it is well-known that,
              when referring to Judges of the High Court sitting to decide a
              case, the expressions frequently used are Single Bench and
              Division Bench. The word “Bench” used in the referring
              order, even in its ordinary connotation, would, therefore,
              include a Single Judge. …”

                                                                     [Emphasis supplied]

26. A contextual reading of Section 6 of the PMLA makes it further clear

      that the Bench need not necessarily include the Chairperson. The

      term ‘Member’ includes the Chairperson. Sub-section (2) of Section

      6 provides that the AA shall consist of “a Chairperson and two other

6 Black's Law Dictionary (12th edn, Thomson Reuters 2024).
7 1967 SCC OnLine SC 258.

                                                            Page 24 of 55
      Members,” while sub-section (4), read with Rule 5 of the 2007
      Rules, makes it clear that the Chairperson is one of the Members,
      appointed to that office. Sub-section (7), similarly, enables the
      transfer and assignment of a case to a Bench of at least two
      Members, leaving no doubt that a ‘Bench’ may consist of one or two
      Members. If a Single-Member Bench is of the view that the case
      assigned to it is required to be heard by a two-Member Bench, the
      Member may refer the matter to the Chairperson for transfer. The
      Chairperson may also exercise such power suo motu, if he deems
      fit. This reading also accords with the manner in which the terms
      ‘Chairperson’ and ‘Member’ of the AT are defined in Sections 2(1)(g)
      and 2(1)(o) of the PMLA.

27. Were the Chairperson to be excluded from the expression ‘one or
      two Members’, as urged by the Private Appellants, a Bench of ‘two
      Members’ sitting with the Chairperson would mean that the AA sit
      and hear cases en bloc, rending Section 6(5) of PMLA redundant.
      There is no gainsaying that such could not have been the intention
      of the legislature. The expression ‘one or two Members’ cannot,
      therefore, be read so as to place the Chairperson in a class apart
      from the Members.

28. We, thus, find greater force in the construction advanced by the
      Union of India, namely, that a Bench may consist of one Member,

                                                                                                                     Page 25 of 55
      being either the Chairperson or one of the other Members, or of two
      Members, being either the Chairperson and one other Member, or
      the two Members other than the Chairperson.

29. Equally, nothing in the text of the PMLA mandates the Member
      from a judicial or legal background to sit on every Bench.

30. The bare text of the PMLA, therefore, lends no support to the
      submissions of Private Appellants. That, however, does not
      conclude the inquiry. The Private Appellants urge, in the
      alternative, that the nature of the functions entrusted to the AA
      itself demands the participation of a Judicial Member, and that
      such a requirement must be read into the statute. It is to this
      contention that we now turn.

D.3 The Functions of the AA and the Need for a Legal Mind

      categories. In the first, the AA receives and preserves the reasons
      recorded, and the material relied upon, by officers of the ED when
      they attach property, conduct searches and seizures, freeze assets
      or records, or arrest persons. In the second, the AA adjudicates
      upon the complaints and applications filed by the ED, and
      determines whether the property in question is involved in money
      laundering, with consequential orders following upon that
      determination. In the former, the AA is the keeper of the sealed
      cover; in the latter, it holds the scales.

      contend that the absence of a Judicial Member would render the
      objectivity of the AA nugatory. In our considered view, the two roles
      differ substantially in their nature and consequence, and the
      submissions must, therefore, be evaluated separately in relation to
      each.

40. Section 8 thus contemplates a process of adjudication in three

41. At the first stage, the AA considers only the contents of the
      complaint or application filed by the ED, for the purpose of
      ascertaining whether there is reason to believe that the person has
      committed the offence of money laundering, or that the property in
      question is involved in money laundering. Any such view is formed
      solely for the purpose of issuing a notice to the persons concerned
      and eliciting their version. Since the AA has before it nothing
      beyond what the ED has supplied, its view at this stage is prima
      facie, and remains subject to the final hearing of the parties. Nor
      does the mere issuance of a show-cause notice visit any prejudice
      or other consequence upon the noticee. By its very nature, a notice
      under Section 8(1) is directed only at securing a response. The
      application of mind, and the weighing of competing factual claims,
      at this stage is, therefore, necessarily limited.

      which are inextricably intertwined.

      Civil Procedure, 1908, shall be guided by the principles of natural
      justice. Such an emphasis is not without reason, for three
      considerations distinguish this stage from the one that precedes it.

44. First, the finding that the AA is obligated to record is whether the
      property in question is involved in money laundering. To arrive at
      that finding, the AA must evaluate the rival versions, weigh the
      material placed before it, apply the prescribed standards of proof,
      including the specific presumptions enacted in the PMLA, and
      identify the true source of income from which the property was
      acquired. These tasks necessarily call for the involvement of a legal
      mind in the process of adjudication, particularly in identifying and
      applying the correct standards of evidence and the burden of proof.

45. Second, the determination will regularly require the interpretation
      and application of the provisions of the PMLA, notably expressions
      such as ‘property’ and the definition of ‘money-laundering’. This, in
      turn, requires an appreciation of judicial precedent, and a
      discerning reading of a considerable body of pronouncements, in
      order to arrive at a consistent standard for determining whether the
      property or record is liable to be attached, retained, or frozen, or
      ought to be restored to the person interested.

                                                                                                                     Page 37 of 55
46. Finally, the adjudication under Section 8 carries substantial
      consequences for the civil and constitutional rights of the persons
      concerned. If AA finds that the property is involved in money
      laundering, the rigours of Section 8(4) are attracted, and
      possession of the property is taken over by the Government of
      India. Such dispossession may well endure until the conclusion of
      the trial for the offence of money laundering, depriving the person
      of the fruits of the property for a considerable period before the
      offence is proved.

47. In our considered opinion, the AA performs, in this role, a function
      not unlike that of a regular court. At the stage of confiscation, a
      similar determination is made by the Special Court, albeit upon
      evidence led in the course of the criminal trial. For the duration of
      that trial, however, Parliament has entrusted to a specialised body,
      in the form of AA, the determination whether properties found to
      be involved in money laundering shall remain under attachment in
      the interim. In that sphere, Section 41 of the PMLA has also
      excluded the jurisdiction of civil courts. The AA thus occupies a
      field from which the ordinary courts have been withdrawn.

48. The determination under Sections 8(2) and 8(3) of the PMLA
      involves a close analysis of legal provisions, demands the
      appreciation of facts, and entails considerable civil consequences.

                                                                                                                     Page 38 of 55
      The presence of a Judicial Member on the Bench undertaking the
      same ensures legal objectivity in the determination recorded by AA.
      The lifetime of experience of the Administrative and Financial
      Members in the AA would undoubtedly render them to be
      exceptional experts in their fields, but impartial and legally sound
      adjudication necessitates judicial temperament and training.

49. It does not bear reiteration that such requirement is the foundation
      of various pronouncements by this Court, whether in the context
      of Tribunals, which essentially replace the jurisdiction of a regular
      Court,8 or bodies having both administrative and adjudicatory
      functions.9 Most notably, the Constitution Bench’s decision in
      Union of India v. Madras Bar Assn.10 espoused the principle that
      a person is entitled to have his rights adjudicated by a forum which
      exercises judicial power consistent with the recognised principles
      of adjudication. There is no doubt that these postulates would
      directly apply to the AA’s adjudicatory responsibilities.

50. To the above extent, we are satisfied that the Member drawn from
      the field of law must comprise or be a part of the Bench of AA

      under Section 8(3) of PMLA.

51. We may also allude, at this juncture, to another submission made
      by Mr. Anil Kaushik, learned ASG, through his pleadings. He
      sought to agitate that the application of a judicial mind at the stage
      of adjudication by the AA may not be necessary, since the PMLA
      provides for appeals up to the High Court. His specific contention
      was that an order of the AA, if flawed on a point of law, would in
      any event be subjected to judicial scrutiny. In our opinion, this
      submission rests upon a fallacy, namely, that the availability of
      judicial correction in appeal dispenses with the need for objective
      decision-making at the original stage.

52. This Court has, in a consistent line of decisions, held that
      tribunals, particularly when called upon to interpret and apply
      statutes, must function through Benches that include at least one
      Judicial Member. The availability of an appeal to the High Court or
      to this Court affords no ground to dilute that settled requirement;
      an argument to the contrary was specifically repelled by this Court
      in State of Gujarat v. Utility Users’ Welfare Assn.11 Under the
      scheme of the PMLA, once the officers of the ED have taken the
      initial action, it is the AA that finally decides upon the attachment,

11 (2018) 6 SCC 21.

      freezing or retention of property and records. The AT and the High
      Court act only as the first and second tiers of appeal, respectively.
      An appellate forum may correct the errors in a decision; it cannot
      supply what the original forum lacked. If the proceedings before the
      AA are defective for want of a Judicial Member, that defect is not
      cured merely because an appeal lies to a judicial forum.

53. We also find that the Union of India’s argument, equating the
      provisions for an AA under PMLA with those for specialised bodies
      under the NDPS, SAFEMA, Benami Act, and Customs Act, is
      misplaced. Though the latter legislations provide statutory
      mechanisms for attachment, confiscation, and forfeiture of
      property in their respective fields of operation, the constitution of
      the authorities under them is not pari materia with the AA. These
      purportedly comparable legislations do not provide that at least one
      of the members of the subject authority must be a Judicial Member,
      i.e., a person having experience in the field of law. Section 6(2) and
      (3) are, on the other hand, express in their mandate for one of the
      three Members of the AA to have legal background, indicating a
      conscious decision of the Legislature to ensure the availability of a
      Judicial Member in the AA. Further, the consequences of
      attachment of property under the PMLA are much more far-
      reaching than in the other legislations, even leading to

                                                                                                                     Page 41 of 55
      dispossession from the property upon confirmation of attachment.
      Given these vital distinctions, we are of the considered view that
      the Union of India cannot claim any parity with such legislations to
      justify vesting of adjudicatory powers in a Bench not including the
      Judicial Member.

54. In light of the above, especially in the interest of protecting legal
      objectivity in the process of adjudication under Section 8(2) and
      8(3) of the PMLA, we find it necessary for a person with judicial or
      legal background be part of the bench of the AA making the final
      determination.

D.3.3 Reconciling Textual Silence with the Need for Legal
Objectivity
55. This Court is, thus, faced with an apparent dissonance between the

      legislative text of Sections 6 and 8 of the PMLA and the safeguards
      necessary for objectivity of determination therein. On one hand,
      Parliament has lucidly and deliberately refrained from mandating
      that the Judicial Member sit on specific benches or hold the
      position of Chairperson. On the other hand, we find that the
      presence of the Judicial Member to be necessary for objective and
      legally sound adjudication under Section 8(3).

56. This variance has been the basis of the Private Appellants’
      arguments challenging the vires of Section 6(5)(b) and 6(7),

                                                                                                                     Page 42 of 55
      contending that such provisions are bad in law for permitting the
      AA to function without the Judicial Member present.

57. It is a settled principle of our jurisprudence that while considering
      any constitutional defects in a legislation, Courts must firstly
      attempt to reconcile the applicable principles with the text of the
      legislation under scrutiny. In other words, the Court must seek to
      harmonise and inculcate Constitutional rights and safeguards
      within the existing legislation. Only if such a balancing fails to bear
      fruit would the Court be inclined to strike down the particular
      provision or hold it to be completely ultra vires the Constitution.
      The ‘reading down’ of a provision, involving interpretation of the
      legislation in a manner which removes the otherwise incurable
      lacunae and rendering the provision workable and effective, is a
      well-known judicial innovation.

58. In the case at hand, we are called upon to similarly syncretise
      Section 6 of the PMLA and accommodate, therein, a mandate for
      involvement of Judicial Member in the final adjudication under
      Section 8 of the said Act.

59. This is not the first occasion for this Court to reconcile such
      provisions. In L. Chandra Kumar (supra), a Constitution Bench
      of this Court considered a similar provision in the Administrative
      Tribunals Act, 1985. It was recognised that the functions of the

                                                                                                                     Page 43 of 55
      Administrative Tribunals would entail the interpretation of the
      constitutional framework and of the applicable statutes and
      regulations, which would in turn require the presence and
      participation of a Judicial Member. The Court, thus, needed to
      ‘read down’ the text of the legislation to enable the application of a
      legally trained mind to such issues.

60. In that context, this Court made use of the enabling power
      contained in the proviso to Section 5(6) of the Administrative
      Tribunals Act, 1985, which stipulated that “…if at any stage of
      the hearing of any … case or matter it appears to the
      Chairman or such Member that the case or matter is of such
      a nature that it ought to be heard by a Bench consisting of
      two Members the case or matter may be transferred by the
      Chairman or, as the case may be, referred to him for transfer
      to, such Bench as the Chairman may deem fit.” The seven-
      Judge Constitution Bench, thus, held that the above proviso must
      be brought into action by the Chairman and any case involving the
      interpretation of statutes be placed before a Bench of at least two
      Members, one of whom must be a Judicial Member.

61. Section 6 of the PMLA, we find, contains enabling provisions
      analogous to those put to use in L. Chandra Kumar (supra). Sub-
      section (7) of thereof enables the Chairperson to assign cases to

                                                                                                                     Page 44 of 55
      different Benches, while sub-section (6) empowers the Chairperson
      to transfer Members from one Bench to another. The statute, thus,
      already furnishes the means by which the requirement we have
      identified can be given effect. We, therefore, consider it appropriate
      to issue directions along the lines laid down by the Constitution
      Bench.

62. Consequently, we hold that while the issuance of a show-cause
      notice under Section 8(1) does not require the participation of the
      Judicial Member of AA, the Bench that undertakes the hearing and
      final adjudication under Sections 8(2) and 8(3) must necessarily
      include the Judicial Member. It is, therefore, imperative that the
      Chairperson of the AA issue the necessary administrative circular,
      in exercise of the statutory powers conferred by Section 6 of the
      PMLA, to ensure that, hitherto, every Bench passing an order under
      Section 8(3) shall include a Judicial Member.

D.3.4 Determinations Ancillary to Adjudication: Sections 9, 20
and 21
63. We also take note of two specific functions of the AA which fall

      outside the general adjudication under Section 8: (i) the
      determination, under the first proviso to Section 9 of the PMLA, of
      the encumbrance or leasehold interest subsisting over attached
      property; and (ii) the determination, under Sections 20 and 21 of

                                                                                                                     Page 45 of 55
      the PMLA, of whether specified property and records are necessary
      for adjudication and ought, consequently, to be retained.

64. Questions of this nature, where they arise in a given case, are
      intrinsically bound up with the adjudication under Section 8 of the
      PMLA. They must, therefore, be decided by the same Bench of AA
      that adjudicates upon the prayer for confirmation of attachment,
      freezing or retention, that is to say, a Bench which includes the
      Judicial Member.

65. We hasten to clarify that we do not, by these findings, mandate that
      adjudication under Section 8 of the PMLA be undertaken only by a
      Bench of at least two Members. In the light of the express textual
      framework of Section 6, we reiterate that the Chairperson may list
      matters for adjudication under Section 8 before a Bench consisting
      of a single Member, provided that such Member is the Judicial
      Member of the AA.

D.3.5 Judicial Member being the Chairperson – Not an Adjudicatory
Necessity
66. While we have noticed and accepted the need for mandating that

      the Judicial Member be part of the AA’s Bench involved in the
      adjudicatory process, we are unable to accede to the submission

                                                                                                                     Page 46 of 55
      that for the Chairperson of the AA must have a Judicial
      Background.

67. We may derive clarity on this aspect from the decision in Utility
      Users’ (supra), wherein this Court was called upon to interpret
      Section 84 of the Electricity Act, 2003. Sub-section (1) thereof, inter
      alia, provided that the Members, including the Chairperson, of the
      State Commission shall be drawn from any of the areas of expertise
      of engineering, finance, commerce, economics, law, or
      management, while sub-section (2) stipulated that the Chairperson
      may be a sitting or retired High Court Judge. Giving a contextual
      interpretation to sub-section (2) with reference to sub-section (1),
      this Court accepted the directory nature of the word “may” and held
      that it was not mandatory to appoint of a sitting or retired High
      Court Judge as Chairperson. In doing so, this Court expressly
      rejected the notion that the Chairperson of a composite body
      performing executive as well as quasi-judicial functions is required
      to belong to legal background.

68. The application of an independent judicial mind is, in our opinion,
      adequately ensured by the presence of a Judicial Member in the
      Bench undertaking adjudication under Sections 8(2) and 8(3) of
      PMLA. The role of the Chairperson of AA, evidently administrative
      in nature, does not require similar application of mind by a person

                                                                                                                     Page 47 of 55
      from the legal field. There is, thus, no mandate for the Judicial
      Member of AA to be appointed as its Chairperson.

E. CONCLUSIONS AND DIRECTIONS
69. In the light of the foregoing analysis, we record our conclusions and

      issue the consequential directions as follows:

      (i) There is no legal mandate that only the Judicial Member be
             appointed as the Chairperson of the AA;

      (ii) Sections 6(5)(b) and 6(7) of the PMLA are valid, and provide
             that a Bench of the AA shall consist of one or two Members,
             which may or may not include the Judicial Member. Subject to
             clauses (iii) and (iv) below, such a Bench may discharge any or
             all of the functions of the AA;

      (iii) The adjudication under Sections 8(2) and 8(3) of the PMLA
             involves the interpretation of key statutory provisions and the
             appreciation of evidentiary material, and shall be undertaken
             only by a Bench of the AA which consists of, or includes, the
             Judicial Member;

      (iv) The determinations under Sections 9, 20(4), and 21(4) of the
             PMLA are ancillary to the adjudication under Sections 8(2) and
             8(3), and shall likewise be assigned to a Bench competent to
             undertake adjudication in terms of clause (iii) above;

F. OPERATION OF THIS JUDGMENT
70. Having so held, we consider it necessary to clarify the bearing of

      this pronouncement upon cases already decided by the AA.

71. The law declared today is not intended to unsettle what has already
      been done in good faith under the statute as it was then
      understood. It would serve neither the interests of justice nor the
      cause of expedition to set aside every order passed under Section
      8(2), Section 9, or Sections 20 and 21 by a Bench of the AA
      consisting of a single, non-Judicial Member. Parties aggrieved by
      such orders passed before the date of this judgment remain at
      liberty to challenge them on their merits by way of the statutory
      appeal available in law. We, therefore, clarify that the law laid down
      in this judgment shall not furnish a ground to question the
      jurisdiction of a Bench of the AA in respect of any order passed by
      it before the date of this judgment.

      nature of adjudication, to our mind, puts an enormous pressure on
      the Members of the AA, especially those involved in the process of
      adjudication.

      originally enacted, wherein the Parliament had envisaged that there
      would be more than one AA, with each AA constituted of an
      Administrative, Judicial, and Financial Member, and a Chairperson
      from among them. With the passing of the 2009 Amendment,

      however, this position was changed and only one AA was

      cases being filed before the AA and the fact that the AA would need
      to complete the proceedings for confirmation of a PAO within 180
      days, the 2009 Amendment appears to have created an
      undesirable, if not unsolvable, lock-jam of cases at the level of the
      AA. Such circumstances would induce the AA to make hurried and
      hasty decisions and have a lasting impact on ability of the
      stakeholders involved to access justice. We are afraid that such
      conditions create a situation entirely contrary to the scheme and
      underlying legislative objectives of PMLA.

78. Given the above situation, this Court would ordinarily have
      exercised its powers under Article 142 of the Constitution and
      directed the Union of India to notify additional Adjudicating
      Authorities. Not only would it bolster the timely conclusion of
      adjudication under Section 8(3) of the PMLA, but it would also
      enable the creation of Regional AAs.

79. However, we are faced with the deliberate and unequivocal
      legislative expression contained in the 2009 Amendment, whereby
      the scheme of multiple AAs was substituted with a singular AA. If,
      then, this Court were to issue such directions, they would amount

                                                                                                                     Page 53 of 55
      to entirely negating the impact of the 2009 Amendment. We are
      equally cognisant regarding the fact that we are not considering a
      challenge to the 2009 Amendment.

80. That being so, we recommend to the Union of India that it
      appropriately consider the suitability and desirability of repealing
      the 2009 Amendment and reverting to the original framework of
      multiple Adjudicating Authorities and take necessary steps for the
      same.

H. EPILOGUE
81. Before we finally conclude, we take the liberty to pen down that the

      PMLA, at its very core is a law of exceptional rigour, enacted to meet
      a mischief of exceptional gravity. Its rigour is tempered, at the very
      first point of contest, by the requirement that a person whose
      property has been attached be heard by an authority independent
      of the agency that attached it. Independence of position is, however,
      only one half of that assurance. Where the authority is called upon
      to weigh evidence, apply statutory presumptions and construe the
      law, the assurance is fully honoured only when the person who
      decides is trained in the discipline of the law. Our directions are
      confined to securing that much, and no more.
