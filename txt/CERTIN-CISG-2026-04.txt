1. Preamble

Artificial Intelligence has evolved from a specialised technology into an important component of
digital services, business processes, public administration and critical infrastructure. AI systems
are increasingly being used in banking & financial services, healthcare, telecommunications,
education, transportation, manufacturing, law enforcement, defence, citizen-service delivery and
other sectors to generate predictions, recommendations, decisions and content, and in certain
cases to autonomously initiate actions in physical or digital environments.

While AI offers significant opportunities for innovation, efficiency and improved service delivery,
its adoption also introduces risks that are not fully addressed through conventional information
technology and cybersecurity assurance mechanisms. Such risks include manipulation of training
data and models, adversarial attacks, unauthorised model access, leakage of sensitive
information, algorithmic bias, lack of explainability, model and data drift, insecure third-party
dependencies, misuse of generative and agentic AI, deepfakes, hallucinated outputs and
inadequate human oversight. These risks may affect the confidentiality, integrity, availability,
safety, fairness, reliability and accountability of AI-enabled systems.

The Artificial Intelligence Security Auditing Guidelines have been developed by Sub-group 03 of
the Working Group on “Indian Standards for Cybersecurity Auditing” constituted by the Indian
Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In). The Guidelines establish a common, risk-
based and lifecycle-oriented framework for assessing the governance, security, privacy, fairness,
transparency, resilience and auditability of AI systems. They are intended to facilitate consistent
security auditing practices across sectors while supporting responsible innovation, indigenous
capability development and alignment with applicable Indian legal, regulatory and policy
requirements and internationally recognised AI governance and cybersecurity standards.

The Guidelines recognise that AI governance cannot be addressed solely through ethical
principles or conventional cybersecurity controls. Effective assurance requires organisational
accountability, technical testing, data and model governance, continuous monitoring, human
oversight, incident response and third-party assurance. Accordingly, the framework provides
auditable requirements proportionate to the nature, scale, autonomy and potential impact of each
AI system.

CERT-In has also taken initiatives to strengthen coordinated cybersecurity monitoring, threat
assessment, technical analysis through the establishment of a dedicated AI cyber defence War
Room bringing together relevant technical capabilities for coordinated assessment and response.
The framework further draws upon the operational experience and institutional capabilities
developed through such initiatives, providing a practical foundation for strengthening the
implementation of risk-based AI security auditing and continuous assurance practices.

2. Executive Summary

The Artificial Intelligence Security Auditing Guidelines provide a structured framework for
conducting security and governance audits of AI systems developed, procured, deployed or
operated by organisations in India, as well as AI systems processing Indian data or providing AI-
enabled services to Indian users.

The framework adopts a risk-based approach under which AI systems are classified into four risk
tiers—Low, Moderate, High and Critical based on factors such as impact severity, autonomy of
decision-making, data sensitivity, scale and exposure, transparency, Explainable AI (XAI),
incident response readiness, reversibility and recoverability, operational/sector criticality,
adversarial exposure and complexity/auditability. The assigned risk tier determines the depth,
frequency and independence of the audit, the extent of documentation required, the level of
human oversight and the applicable monitoring, reporting and assurance requirements.

The Guidelines establish nine core audit domains:

   i. AI Governance and Accountability

  ii. Data Lifecycle, Quality and Privacy

  iii. Model Risk Management

 iv. AI Ethics, Bias and Fairness

  v. Explainability and Transparency

 vi. Security, Adversarial Resilience and Incident Reporting

 vii. Continuous Monitoring, Drift Management and Validation

viii. Human Oversight and Decision Accountability

 ix. Third-Party and AI Supply-Chain Assurance

These domains collectively cover the entire AI lifecycle, including design, procurement, data
collection, model development, training, validation, deployment, operation, retraining, monitoring
and decommissioning. The framework supplements these domains with dedicated requirements
for advanced AI architectures, including Generative AI, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG),
Agentic AI, Multimodal AI, Multi-Agent AI and Autonomous/Cyber-Physical AI. These
requirements address prompt injection, sensitive-information disclosure, excessive agency, data
and model poisoning, supply-chain risks, vector and embedding weaknesses, unsafe tool use,
persistent-memory risks, inter-agent communication, multimodal attacks, misinformation and
improper output handling.

The Guidelines also prescribe a structured audit methodology covering engagement initiation,
risk assessment, scoping, audit planning, evidence collection, technical testing, finding

classification, reporting, remediation and follow-up verification. Audit findings are classified as
Critical, High, Moderate or Low/Observation, with corresponding containment, remediation,
escalation and reporting requirements. A five-level maturity model enables organisations to
assess their current AI governance capability and plan progressive improvement from ad hoc
practices to measured and continuously optimised governance.

AI-assisted audit procedures may support evidence analysis, control mapping, technical testing
and report preparation. Their use is subject to approved scope, confidentiality controls, tool
validation, traceable evidence and competent human review. AI-generated outputs shall not
replace sufficient and appropriate evidence or the auditor’s independent judgement.

The framework is aligned with recognised standards and frameworks, including CERT-In’s
Blueprint for Reducing Exposure and Defending against AI-Assisted Vulnerabilities Exploitation
in Digital Infrastructure, India AI Governance Guidelines by MeitY, ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC
23894, ISO/IEC 27001, NIST AI RMF, MITRE ATLAS and OWASP guidance for machine learning
and large language model applications. The framework also provides a structured mechanism for
identifying, assessing and mapping applicable CERT-In requirements, sector-specific
cybersecurity obligations, contractual security requirements and other external requirements into
the audit criteria and Statement of Applicability.

The Guidelines are intended for use by government organisations, public sector entities, critical
infrastructure operators, regulated entities, private organisations, AI developers, AI service
providers, internal and external auditors, CERT-In empanelled cybersecurity auditing
organisations, risk and compliance functions and other stakeholders involved in the development,
procurement, deployment or oversight of AI systems.

4. Purpose & Scope

    This section defines the objectives, boundaries and applicability of the AI Security Auditing
    Guidelines. It establishes what the framework aims to achieve, who it applies to, which AI systems
    and data categories fall within scope.

   4.1 Purpose

    The purpose of these Guidelines is to establish a consistent, risk-based and auditable mechanism
    for evaluating the security, governance and responsible operation of AI systems throughout their
    lifecycle.
    The framework aims to:

      Ensure secure, ethical and auditable AI adoption: Define consistent audit requirements
      applicable to AI systems regardless of whether they are developed internally, procured from
      third parties, consumed through APIs or cloud services, or derived from open-source or
      foundation models.

      Enable risk-based assurance: Determine the depth, frequency and technical intensity of an
      audit based on the AI system’s potential impact, autonomy, data sensitivity, scale, criticality,
      adversarial exposure and explainability.

4.9 Exclusions and Boundaries

While this framework is deliberately comprehensive, the following boundaries apply:

Research and academic AI: AI systems used exclusively for academic research within
recognised educational or research institutions, where the system does not make decisions
affecting individuals and is not deployed in a production or public-facing capacity, are outside
the mandatory compliance scope. However, such systems are encouraged to adopt the
framework’s principles voluntarily. Once a research AI system transitions to pilot deployment or
production, it enters full scope.

Non-AI automation: Purely rule-based automation systems (e.g., traditional RPA without
machine learning components, deterministic business logic engines) that do not involve statistical
learning, pattern inference, or adaptive behaviour are not AI systems under this framework and
are therefore excluded. However, hybrid systems that combine rule-based logic with ML
components are fully within scope.

Consumer personal-use AI: AI tools used by individuals for personal, non-commercial purposes
(e.g., consumer-grade virtual assistants, personal photo editing AI) are not subject to this
framework. However, the providers of such AI tools operating in India remain within scope as AI
service providers.

4.10 How to Use These Guidelines

Organisations should first identify and document all AI systems within the applicable
organisational and technical boundaries. Each AI system should then be classified using the risk-
tiering methodology specified in these Guidelines. The assigned risk tier shall determine the
applicable audit domains, depth of assessment, evidence requirements, audit frequency and level
of auditor independence.
Auditors should use the Core Audit Domains as the principal control baseline and apply the
supplementary requirements for generative AI, large language models, agentic AI, autonomous
systems or other specialised AI deployments, wherever applicable. The Audit Planning and
Scoping Methodology should be used to determine engagement boundaries, technical testing
requirements and evidence collection procedures, while the Audit Reporting and Remediation
section should be followed for classification, reporting and closure of findings.
Applicable CERT-In requirements, recognised standards, contractual security obligations and
other external requirements shall be identified during audit scoping, assessed for applicability and
incorporated into the audit criteria and Statement of Applicability. Where a finding or incident
triggers an external reporting obligation, the applicable reporting mechanism and prescribed
timeline shall be followed.
The principal audit workflow is planning and scoping, assessment of the core audit domains,
applicable advanced-AI and cybersecurity testing, human decision-review testing, and reporting,
remediation and follow-up. The standards catalogue and national-governance mappings are
supporting reference material, not a substitute for control testing or evidence-based assurance.

5. Applicability Matrix & Risk Tiering

    This section establishes the risk-based classification system that determines audit intensity,
    compliance obligations, and governance requirements for each AI system under this framework.
    The tiering model ensures that audit and control requirements increase in proportion to the
    potential security, safety, operational, data, critical-infrastructure and systemic impact of the AI
    system.

   5.1 Rationale for a Risk-Based Approach

    A uniform, one-size-fits-all compliance model would impose disproportionate burden on low-risk
    AI deployments while potentially under-governing high-impact systems. This framework adopts a
    tiered,risk-proportionate governance model where:

    Audit intensity scales with risk: Higher-risk AI systems undergo more rigorous, more frequent,
    and more independent auditing.

    Compliance obligations are calibrated: Documentation, reporting, testing, and governance
    structure requirements increase with tier level.

    Innovation is protected: Low-risk and experimental AI systems face streamlined compliance
    pathways, enabling MSMEs, startups, and research organisations to innovate responsibly without
    excessive regulatory overhead.

    Sectoral context is incorporated: The same AI technology (e.g., a recommendation engine)
    may fall into different risk tiers depending on the sector and use case in which it operates.

evidence collection and technical testing may be proportionate to system complexity and risk.
Proportionality shall not remove mandatory security controls or prevent the auditor from obtaining

6.1.1 Layer Interdependencies
The four governance layers form a cascading hierarchy where each layer depends on and
reinforces the others:

  Principles shape controls: Every technical and operational control in Layer 2 is derived from
  one or more foundational principles in Layer 1. An auditor assessing a specific control can trace

                                                                                                                   Page 29 of 107
                                                                                                                             AI Security Auditing Guidelines

it back to the principle it serves.

Controls support external requirements: Technical and operational controls defined in Layer
2 may be mapped to applicable external requirements identified in Layer 3. This enables
integrated assurance, reduces duplication and helps demonstrate how implemented controls
address relevant external requirements without treating all referenced standards or guidance
as mandatory.

Audit verifies control effectiveness: Audit and continuous-assurance mechanisms in Layer
4 provide independent verification of whether applicable controls are appropriately designed
and operating effectively and whether identified external requirements have been addressed
within the defined audit scope.

Feedback drives evolution: Audit findings, incident reports, regulatory developments,
maturity assessments and emerging AI risks provide continuous feedback for periodic
refinement of the framework, ensuring that its controls, methodologies, and assurance
requirements remain aligned with the evolving AI technology, threat, and governance
landscape.

6.2 Guiding Principles for Responsible AI Governance

from and harmonise NITI Aayog’s Responsible AI principles, the OECD AI Principles, UNESCO’s
Ethics of AI, and the requirements of ISO/IEC 42001:2023.
Every audit requirement, control objective, and compliance obligation in this framework traces to
one or more of these principles. They are not aspirational—they are operationalised through
specific, auditable controls in the subsequent sections.

7.9 Scope Limitations and Alternative Assurance Evidence

    Where complete technical access is unavailable due to proprietary models, AIaaS delivery
    models, third-party restrictions or other limitations, the auditor shall:

         identify the unavailable evidence; assess the effect on audit coverage; obtain alternative
             evidence where possible; perform black-box/API-based testing; review independent
             assurance reports or attestations; evaluate controls implemented by the deploying
             organisation; document residual risk; and determine whether the limitation affects the audit
             conclusion or assurance opinion.

         Absence of access shall not automatically be treated as evidence of conformity.

7.10 AI-Assisted Audit Procedures

    AI-assisted auditing is the use of AI tools by an audit team to support audit procedures; it is not
    the same as auditing an AI system. Such tools may assist document review, control mapping,
    evidence classification, log analysis, test-case preparation and report drafting. Their use shall be
    proportionate to engagement risk and shall not reduce the required audit scope, evidence quality,
    independence or professional scepticism.

    appropriateness of evidence, findings, severity ratings, assurance opinion and verification of
    closure. AI-generated analysis, summaries and recommendations are working aids; they are not,

9. Advanced AI Security Requirements

This section establishes dedicated security and audit requirements for Generative AI, Retrieval-
Augmented Generation (RAG), Agentic AI, Multimodal AI, Multi-Agent AI and Autonomous/Cyber-
Physical AI systems. In addition to traditional generative-model risks such as hallucination and
prompt injection, these architectures introduce additional attack surfaces involving knowledge
retrieval, vector databases, tool invocation, persistent memory, autonomous actions, cross-agent
interactions and non-text input modalities. The requirements in this section supplement, but do
not replace, the core audit domains in Section 8.

9.5 Advanced AI Risk Escalation

Generative, RAG, agentic, multimodal, multi-agent and autonomous/cyber-physical AI systems
shall be evaluated using the risk factors defined in Section 5.3. The following capabilities or
deployment characteristics shall be considered as risk-escalation factors:

   i. external or unrestricted public exposure;
  ii. processing of sensitive or critical information;
  iii. use in consequential business, safety or operational decisions;
 iv. privileged access to production systems, data stores or administrative functions;
  v. ability to invoke tools, execute code, initiate transactions or communicate externally;
 vi. persistent memory capable of influencing future actions;
 vii. autonomous task planning or multi-step execution;
viii. delegation to other agents or dynamically selected tools;

                                                                                                                   Page 65 of 107
                                                                                                                                 AI Security Auditing Guidelines

 ix. operation in critical infrastructure or safety-critical environments;
  x. inability to reliably interrupt, rollback or place the system in a safe state;
 xi. opaque third-party model dependencies that materially restrict assurance; and
 xii. large-scale downstream deployment where a model-level failure may have systemic

         impact.

Where one or more such characteristics materially increase potential impact or adversarial
exposure, the assigned tier shall be escalated or enhanced audit requirements shall be applied
and the rationale shall be documented.

9.6 RAG and Vector Database Security

RAG-based systems shall implement controls covering knowledge-source provenance, secure
ingestion, document and chunk-level access control, tenant isolation, vector-store security,
indirect prompt injection, poisoned knowledge sources, retrieval relevance, stale content, deletion
propagation and retrieval logging.

Auditors shall verify that retrieved content is treated as untrusted input and cannot override higher-

priority system instructions or bypass user-level access restrictions.

9.7 Agentic AI Security

Agentic AI systems shall have documented identities, authorised objectives, approved tools,
defined permission boundaries and action limits. Agents shall operate under least privilege and
shall not automatically inherit permissions from users, tools or other agents.

Consequential actions shall use appropriate human approval gates. Agent execution metadata,
state transitions, tool calls, delegated tasks, approvals, external actions and outputs required to
reconstruct the execution chain shall be logged and protected against unauthorised modification.

High-risk agentic systems shall support emergency interruption, credential revocation, rollback
and prevention of uncontrolled automatic restart.

Agentic AI systems that interact with external tool servers, plugins, Model Context Protocol (MCP)
services, agent-to-agent communication mechanisms or dynamically discovered services shall
maintain an approved inventory of permitted connections. Tool/service identities shall be
authenticated, permissions shall follow least privilege, connection metadata and tool descriptions
shall be validated, and security-relevant tool calls shall be logged. Dynamic or newly introduced
tool connections shall undergo appropriate provenance, integrity and security assessment before
production use.

video, documents, metadata and other supported modalities.

injection, manipulated files, spoofed inputs, modality-specific adversarial examples and
inconsistent behaviour across equivalent inputs presented in different modalities.

11. Contestability and Human Decision Review

11.1 Purpose and Applicability

For the purpose of these Guidelines, contestability refers to the ability to subject an AI-generated
decision, recommendation or action to a defined process of review, validation, intervention, override,
correction or escalation where its accuracy, safety, reliability or appropriateness is questioned.

Contestability provides an additional assurance mechanism for AI systems whose outputs materially
influence operational, financial, safety-related or other consequential decisions. It enables incorrect,
anomalous, biased, low-confidence or unsafe AI outputs to be identified and reviewed by authorised
personnel before or after execution, depending on the system architecture and risk profile.

Section 8.8 defines the human-oversight controls that organisations are expected to implement. This
section supplements those requirements by defining the decision-review mechanisms and audit
procedures used to verify that such oversight operates effectively in practice.

Contestability and decision-review requirements shall be proportionate to the AI system’s risk tier,
autonomy, potential impact, reversibility of outcomes and level of human involvement.

    Auditors shall assess both the design and operating effectiveness of contestability and human
    decision-review mechanisms.

    Audit procedures should include, as applicable:
     inspect the documented criteria and triggers requiring human review;
     sample AI-generated decisions or actions and verify that required reviews were performed;
     verify that reviewers received sufficient information to make an informed assessment;
     test whether mandatory approval gates can be bypassed through alternate interfaces, APIs

        or workflows;
     verify that AI outputs can be rejected, corrected or overridden by authorised personnel;
     test escalation mechanisms for low-confidence, anomalous, disputed or high-impact AI

        outputs;
     inspect audit logs to verify traceability of AI outputs, human approvals, interventions and

        overrides;
     assess whether reviewer access and permissions are appropriate and segregated from

        unauthorised users;
     review training and competence records of personnel performing human review;
     analyse override frequency, reviewer agreement rates and recurring exceptions for indicators

        of ineffective oversight or model weakness;
     verify that repeated override patterns trigger investigation, model review or re-validation where

        appropriate; and
     for high-risk or critical systems, perform or observe practical testing of review, approval,

        override, interruption and recovery mechanisms.

Auditors should not conclude that human oversight is effective solely because a manual-review
procedure exists. The audit shall determine whether reviewers are technically capable, sufficiently
informed and operationally empowered to challenge or override AI outputs when necessary.

12.7 External Reporting and Escalation Requirements

Applicable external requirements shall be identified during audit scoping and incorporated into the
audit criteria and Statement of Applicability. This subsection addresses reporting or escalation
obligations triggered by audit findings, cybersecurity incidents or other reportable events.

During audit planning and reporting, the auditor and auditee shall identify applicable external
reporting requirements relevant to the system, organisation and identified finding or incident.

Where an audit identifies a condition that also constitutes a reportable cybersecurity incident or
other externally reportable event, the applicable reporting mechanism and prescribed timeline shall
be followed.

The audit report shall distinguish between:
     internal audit escalation;
     corrective-action reporting;
     security-incident reporting; and
     other applicable external reporting requirements.

The classification of an audit finding alone shall not automatically determine whether external
reporting is required.

     ✓ Previous audit findings have been formally tracked in a CAP register with assigned owners

     ✓ Critical and High findings from the previous audit have been remediated and independently
     verified.
     ✓ Evidence of corrective action completion is documented and sufficient to demonstrate
     effective remediation.
     ✓ Root causes have been addressed (not just symptoms) for recurring or systemic findings.
     ✓ Management review (ISO 42001 Clause 9.3) has considered audit results and CAP status.
     ✓ Continual improvement actions arising from audit findings are documented and in progress.
     ✓ Overdue findings have been appropriately escalated per the framework’s escalation
     requirements.
     ✓ Applicable external reporting and escalation obligations triggered by previous findings have

         been fulfilled.
     ✓ Audit report distribution has been controlled per confidentiality requirements.
     ✓ Trend analysis of findings across audit cycles has been performed and presented to the AI
     Governance Committee.

13. Maturity Level Model

This section defines a five-level organisational maturity model for AI governance capability. The
model enables organisations to benchmark their current state, identify gaps and chart a structured
progression path towards mature, resilient AI governance. It complements, but does not replace,
the audit planning, control assessment, technical testing, decision review, reporting and
remediation requirements in Sections 7–12.

The maturity model is informed by established capability maturity frameworks (CMMI, COBIT),
adapted for the specific requirements of AI governance, and aligned with the nine core audit
domains of this framework and the requirements of ISO/IEC 42001:2023.

14. Referenced Frameworks & Standards

    This section catalogues the international standards, regulatory frameworks, Indian legislation, and
    technical security resources that form the normative and informative foundation of the AI Security
    Auditing Guidelines. These references ensure global credibility, compliance interoperability, and
    alignment with both Indian regulatory requirements and international best practices.

    The standards, frameworks, advisories and technical references identified in this section provide
    normative or informative inputs to the AI Security Auditing Guidelines. Audit criteria shall primarily
    comprise the controls defined in these Guidelines together with applicable external requirements
    identified for the engagement. Informative frameworks and good-practice references shall be

   14.1 Purpose of Referenced Frameworks

    The inclusion of referenced frameworks serves five strategic functions:

      Reference Alignment: Providing recognised standards, frameworks, threat taxonomies and
      technical guidance against which framework controls may be mapped. A referenced document
      shall be treated as mandatory only where its applicability is established through these
      Guidelines or an applicable external requirement.

      Interoperability: Enabling Indian organisations to achieve cross-border regulatory recognition
      and international AI certification readiness.

      Audit credibility: Providing auditors with externally validated control objectives, risk categories,
      and assessment methodologies.

      Gap coverage: Ensuring no critical governance dimension is overlooked by synthesising
      complementary frameworks that address different aspects of AI risk.

      Future-proofing: Anchoring the framework to evolving international standards (ISO, NIST, EU)
      whose update cycles will inform periodic revisions of this document.

14.5 Applicability of External Requirements

External requirements relevant to an AI security audit may include applicable CERT-In Directions;
CERT-In advisories, guidelines and technical references; sector-specific cybersecurity
requirements; contractual security and assurance obligations; formally adopted AI management
or cybersecurity standards; and other requirements identified by the auditee or competent
authority.

Mandatory requirements shall be incorporated into the audit criteria where applicable. Informative
standards, advisories and good-practice references shall support risk assessment, threat
modelling, control assessment and technical testing based on their relevance to the in-scope AI
system.

Identification and mapping of applicable external requirements during an audit engagement shall
be performed in accordance with Section 7.3.

15. Integration with the India AI Governance Guidelines

In 2025, the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) published the India AI
Governance Guidelines—a national policy framework developed by a drafting committee
constituted by MeitY, with a foreword by the Secretary, MeitY, and a preface by the Principal
Scientific Adviser to the Government of India. The Guidelines represent India’s overarching policy
position on AI governance: a balanced, agile, flexible, and pro-innovation approach structured
around seven guiding principles (“sutras”), six policy pillars, a phased action plan, and practical
guidance for industry and regulators.

This section maps the India AI Governance Guidelines to the National AI Security Auditing
Guidelines, demonstrating how the two instruments complement each other and ensuring that
organisations adopting this audit framework are simultaneously aligned with the national
governance vision.

15.1 Relationship Between the Two Instruments

The India AI Governance Guidelines and this audit framework operate at different but
complementary levels of governance:

Table 42. Roles of the National Governance and Audit Guidelines

Instrument                   Role in AI Governance
India AI Governance
Guidelines                   Sets the national AI governance philosophy, policy direction, and
                             institutional architecture. Provides high-level principles,
AI Security Auditing         recommends policy and regulatory approaches, and establishes
Guidelines (This Framework)  institutional bodies (AI Governance Group, AI Safety Institute).
                             Scope is economy-wide and covers enablement, regulation, risk
                             mitigation, and institutional design.

                             Operationalises the governance vision into auditable controls.
                             Provides the technical audit methodology, risk tiering, domain-
                             specific control requirements, and audit & assurance mechanisms
                             that translate governance principles into verifiable compliance.
                             Scope is audit-specific, targeting organisations deploying AI
                             systems.

In essence, the India AI Governance Guidelines answer the question “what should India’s AI
governance look like?” while this audit framework answers “how do we verify that organisations
are actually doing it?” The two instruments are designed to be mutually reinforcing: compliance
with this audit framework demonstrates alignment with the national governance vision, and the
national governance vision provides the policy authority for the audit requirements.
