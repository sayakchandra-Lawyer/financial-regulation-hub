                                                JUDGEMENT
JUSTICE YOGESH KHANNA, MEMBER (JUDICIAL)

         The present four appeals being Company Appeal (AT)(Insolvency) Nos. 933,
934, 935 and 943 of 2026 have been filed by Mrs. Bindu Kapoor (Appellant) in
her capacity as suspended director of four corporate debtors. All the four appeals
have been filed against common impugned order dated 07.04.2026 passed by
Ld. NCLT, New Delhi Bench in CP(IB) Nos. 36, 32, 31 and 30 of 2026. These
four petitions were filed against four different Corporate Debtors, namely,
Imagine Estate Private Ltd (CP(IB) No.36 of 2026), Imagine Home Private Ltd.
(CP(IB) No.32 of 2026), Imagine Residence Private Ltd. (CP (IB) No.31 of 2026)
and Imagine Habitat Private Ltd. (CP(IB) No.30/2026). The common issues are
raised in these appeals and the facts of Company Appeal(AT)(Insolvency) No.933
of 2026 of Ld. NCLT, New Delhi Bench in CP(IB) No.36 of 2026 titled J.C. Flowers
Asset Reconstruction Pvt. Ltd Vs. Imagine Estate Private Limited are taken up
while deciding these appeals. The petition CP(IB) NO.36 of 2026 was filed by
M/s J.C. Flowers Asset Reconstruction Ltd (i.e. “Respondent No. 2 / JCF”)
seeking initiation of Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (“CIRP”) against
the Corporate Debtor (“CD”). It is argued Ld. NCLT has vide the Impugned Order
                                                               7

passed an entirely unreasoned order and proceeded to admit the Corporate
Debtor into CIRP, appointing Ms. Mamta Binani as an Interim Resolution
Professional (“IRP / Respondent No.1”) of the Corporate Debtor.

2. Section 7 Petition was filed by M/s JCF against the Corporate Debtor,
inter-alia, based on the following financial arrangements: (i) the CD’s
hypothecation against a loan of Rs. 105 crore availed by M/s Bliss House Private
Limited (“BHPL”) from M/s Indiabulls Housing Finance Limited (“IHFL”),
pursuant to a Loan Agreement dated 29.03.2019 (“Rs. 105 crore loan” and
“Hypothecation Deed”); and (ii) two loans worth Rs. 160 crore and Rs. 90 crore
availed by the CD from IHFL under Loan Agreements dated 05.09.2018,
aggregating to Rs. 250 crore (“Rs. 250 crore loans”), and; (iii) two loans worth Rs.
172 crore and Rs. 174 crore availed by the Corporate Debtor from Indiabulls
Commercial Credit Limited (“ICCL”) under Loan Agreements dated 07.10.2019,
aggregating to Rs. 346 crore (“Rs. 346 crore loans”, collectively referred to along
with the Rs. 250 crore loans as the “Rs. 596 crore loans”). It was alleged while
BHPL as well as the CD had duly complied with their obligations, on 09.03.2020,
IHFL issued the Loan Recall Notices (“2020 LRNs”), recalling the loan facilities
alleging an ‘Event of Default’ had occurred as a result of ‘Material Adverse Effect’
under the aforesaid Loan Agreements, without any justification or elaboration of
the alleged ‘Event of Default’.

3. Considering the dispute resolution scheme under the aforesaid loans,
arbitral proceedings were initiated by IHFL and it finally concluded on
                                                               8

08.05.2021, wherein, the Ld. Sole Arbitrator reserved the matter for the purpose
of passing the awards (“IHFL Arbitration”). After a significant delay of one (1)
year, nine (9) months and twenty (20) days, the Ld. Sole Arbitrator proceeded to
issue similarly – worded Awards on 28.02.2023. Aggrieved by the aforesaid
Awards rendered by the Ld. Sole Arbitrator in the IHFL Arbitration, BHPL as well
as the CD, preferred petitions under Section 34 of the Arbitration Act before the
Hon’ble High Court of Delhi in September 2023 (collectively referred to as
“Section 34 Petitions”)

4. On 29.03.2025, IHFL executed an Assignment Agreement in favour of M/s
JCF whereby all of IHFL’s rights in relation to the Rs. 105 crore loan as well as
the Rs. 596 crore loans were assigned to M/s JCF. It is alleged subsequently,
on 24.10.2025, M/s JCF, with an alleged malafide intent to circumvent the
aforesaid Section 34 Petitions, filed a Petition under Section 7 of IBC before the
Ld. NCLT, New Delhi seeking initiation of CIRP against BHPL which was admitted
by the Ld. NCLT, vide Order dated 21.01.2026 (“PL Admission Order”).
Thereafter, notwithstanding the fact the registered office of the CD is in Mumbai;
on 04.12.2025, M/s JCF filed the Section 7 Petition against the CD before Ld.
NCLT, New Delhi. JCF alleged the CD was an ‘obligor’ under the Rs. 105 crore
loan, and the Hypothecation Deed to secure Rs. 105 crore loan would amount to
a ‘guarantee’ enabling the Ld. NCLT to exercise its jurisdiction under Section
60(2) of IBC. Despite the CD’s vehement submissions on 07.04.2026, the Ld.
NCLT proceeded to pass the Impugned Order.
                                                               9

5. The learned senior counsel for the appellant rather admitted the CIRP

proceedings have been initiated against the principal borrower and the judgment

of the Ld. NCLT was confirmed till the Hon’ble Supreme Court. The impugned

order admits petitions against four different companies who had allegedly

hypothecated their assets for the loans granted to the principal borrower. It was

argued the facts of these cases are wholly different than that of the principal

borrower and the Ld. NCLT, New Delhi has no jurisdiction to deal with the

insolvency qua the guarantor(s).

6. It was argued (a) all these four companies on whose behalf the appeals

have been filed by the suspended director had its registered office(s) at Mumbai

and hence petition(s) ought to have been filed at Mumbai and (b) all these

companies are not the corporate guarantors of the loans granted to the principal

borrowers and at best these companies can be termed as hypothecators and thus

does not fall within ambit of the sub-section (2) of Section 60 of the IBC. Section

60(1) and (2) are as under: -

                 “60. Adjudicating Authority for corporate persons.
                 (1)The Adjudicating Authority, in relation to insolvency
                 resolution and liquidation for corporate persons
                 including corporate debtors and personal guarantors
                 thereof shall be the National Company Law Tribunal
                 having territorial jurisdiction over the place where the
                 registered office of the corporate person is located.
                 (2)Without prejudice to sub-section (1) and
                 notwithstanding anything to the contrary contained in
                 this Code, where a corporate insolvency resolution
                 process or liquidation proceeding of a corporate debtor
                 is pending before a National Company Law Tribunal,
                 an application relating to the insolvency resolution or
                                                              10

                 [liquidation or bankruptcy of a corporate guarantor
                 or personal guarantor, as the case may be, of such
                 corporate debtor] [Substituted 'bankruptcy of a
                 personal guarantor of such corporate debtor' by Act No.
                 26 of 2018, dated 17.8.2018.] shall be filed before
                 such National Company Law Tribunal.”

7. The main issue is whether these four companies are the corporate

guarantors of the principal borrower. It was argued the Respondent filed the

company petition, primarily, on the ground these four companies were the

Corporate Guarantors of the principal borrower as is shown in the pleadings.

Reference was made to the following paras of the company petition filed before

the Ld. NCLT:

                 repayment capacity of the RAB Promoters, SCL and its
                 subsidiary Indiabulls Commercial Creditor Limited

                 77. Clearly, the debt under the Subject Loan
                 Agreement forms part of both the Principal Borrower
                 Petition (as the Principal Borrower) and this Petition (as
                 the Corporte Guarantee).

                 78. Section 60(2) of the Code prescribes that any
                 proceeding against the corporate guarantor of a
                 corporate debtor shall be filed before the Adjudicating
                 Authority where the CIRP proceedings of the corporate
                 debtor is pending. In the facts of the present matter, (i)
                 the Principal Borrower Petition is pending adjudication
                 before this Hon’ble Tribunal; (ii) the Corporate Debtor
                 in this Petition is an Obligor of the Principal
                 Borrwer and thus a corporate guarantor (iii) the
                 debt underlying the Principal Borrower Petition
                 and the instant Petition are overlapping.
                 Therefore, in accordance with Section 60(2) of the Code
                 and to prevent conflicting decisions in respect of the
                 same debt, this Petition under Section 7 of the Code
                 has been instituted before this Hon’ble Tribunal.”

8. Thus it was argued the pleadings show these four companies were treated

as the Corporate Guarantors by the creditors but neither of these companies

have ever executed any Corporate Deed of Guarantee or Personal Deed of

Guarantee and despite the same, the impugned order was passed on the premise

the appellants were Corporate Guarantors as is evident from paras 21, 22 and

26 of the impugned order: -

                 “21. In the wake of the aforementioned, we are left
                 with no doubt that the Corporate Debtors before us
                 have the liability as Corporate Guarantors towards
                 the lender.

                 22. To meet the plea of jurisdiction of this Bench, raised
                 on behalf of the Corporate Debtors, the Petitioner
                 explained that in terms of the provisions of Section
                 60(2) of the Code, an application related with CIRP of
                 a Corporate Guarantor is required to be filed before the
                 same Tribunal before which the Principal Debtor is
                 undergoing CIRP.
                                                              13

                 26. Thus, we are left with no doubt that the captioned
                 petitions are amenable to the jurisdiction of this Bench
                 where CIRP qua the Principal Debtor viz. Bliss House
                 Pvt. Ltd., commenced in terms of order dated
                 21.01.2026 passed in CP (IB)-585/ND/2025 is
                 pending. One may say that in respect of the part of the
                 debt mentioned in the petitions, the Corporate Debtors
                 are also Principal Debtors, but the same would not be
                 a ground to take a view that the Corporate Debtors
                 before us are not Corporate Guarantors in respect of
                 part of the debt qua which the CIRP in respect of
                 Principal Debtor is pending before this Bench. Ergo,
                 the plea of territorial jurisdiction raised on
                 behalf of the Corporate Debtors is rejected.”

9. It was argued the above paras do show the plea on territorial jurisdiction

was rejected by the Ld. NCLT and the documents viz the loan agreements and

the hypothecation deeds were treated as the Corporate Guarantee(s). Reference

was made to Section 126 of the India Contract Act which defines Guarantee as

under: -

                  “126.'Contract of guarantee', 'surety', 'principal debtor'
                 and 'creditor'—
                 A 'contract of guarantee' is a contract to perform the
                 promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person in
                 case of his default. The person who gives the
                 guarantee is called the 'surety'; the person in respect
                 of whose default the guarantee is given is called the
                 'principal debtor', and the person to whom the
                 guarantee is given is called the 'creditor'. A guarantee
                 may be either oral or written.”

10. It was argued the first feature of the guarantee is it has to be a tripartite

agreement, which fact was missing in the present case and no examination on

this aspect was made by the Ld. NCLT.

11. It was the submission of the learned senior counsel for the appellant the

Ld. NCLT had relied upon para 1 of the Deed of Hypothecation but had ignored
                                                              14

other paras of the said Deed, hence holistic view of the documents was never

taken. It was argued the hypothecators had not signed the Loan Agreements and

whereas the borrowers had not signed the Deed of Hypothecation. It is submitted

the impugned order had relied upon China Development Banks case but it

overlooked that in the cited case the Deed of Hypothecation was rather a tripartite

agreement which met the requirements of Section 126 and 127 of the Contract

Act.

24. The facts do show BHPL was admitted into CIRP by the Ld. NCLT vide its

therefore, attained finality. This Appeal raises identical grounds as raised by

BHPL in earlier round and rejected by this Tribunal and also affirmed by the
                                                              22

Hon’ble Supreme Court. The only remaining issue is the validity of invocation of
Section 60(2) of the IBC.

25. The loan of Rs. 105 Crores to BHPL (the “BHPL Debt”) is governed by the
Loan Agreement dated 29 March 2019 (the “BHPL Loan Agreement”). Schedule I
undisputedly lists the Corporate Debtor as a “Hypothecator”.

26. Clause 1.1 of the BH Loan Agreement defines “Obligors” to include inter-
alia the Borrower and the Hypothecators i.e. the Corporate Debtor. Clause 2.1
makes all Obligors jointly and severally liable for the dues under the BH Loan
Agreement. Clause 12.2 empowers the Lender, upon an Event of Default, to recall
the BH Debt, whereupon the entire dues become immediately payable by the
Obligors, which term specifically includes Hypothecators.

27. Further, under Clause 2.2.4, the Obligors “are under an obligation to pay
from any source” upon a shortfall, payment default or Event of Default, so that
the entire dues under the BH Loan Agreement are recovered by the lender. The
Corporate Debtor admittedly executed the Deed of Hypothecation, whereby it
assumed all obligations stipulated under the BH Loan Agreement. The Corporate
Debtor executed the Deed of Hypothecation in furtherance of the BH Loan
Agreement. Under its recitals viz a) Recital A names the BH Loan Agreement in
Schedule III; b) Recital B contemplates the liability of the Obligors to repay the
dues under the BH Loan Agreement; and c) Recital D records that “Obligors”
includes the Hypothecator, a status the Corporate Debtor admits. The Corporate
                                                              23

Debtor thus consciously undertook obligations ascribed to it under the BH Loan
Agreement.

28. This is further confirmed by Clause 1 of the Deed of Hypothecation, which
reiterates the Corporate Debtor’s assumption of the obligations under the BH
Loan Agreement. Clause 5 requires the BH Loan Agreement to be read with the
Deed of Hypothecation and to prevail over it in case of inconsistency. The
obligations set out in the BH Loan Agreement establish joint and several liability
in addition to a covenant to pay any shortfall for the reasons set out below.

for the BH Debt.

32. Section 60(2) begins with a non-obstante provision which carves out an
exception to the general rule of jurisdiction under Section 60(1). It stipulates the
proceedings against the principal borrower and its guarantor are dealt with by
the same Tribunal, thereby avoiding conflicting orders and double realisation. In
this case, BHPL, the principal borrower, is undergoing CIRP before the Ld. NCLT,
New Delhi and its admission has attained finality up to the Hon’ble Supreme
Court. The CIRP of the Corporate Debtor, as a corporate guarantor, must
therefore proceed before the same Ld. Adjudicating Authority. This Tribunal by
judgement dated 1 August 2025, in S. Vasudevan v. IDBI Trusteeship Limited
CA (AT) (CH) (Ins) No.362/2023, upheld proceedings before the Ld. NCLT,
Chennai against a corporate guarantor whose registered office was in Bengaluru,
                                                              25

since the principal borrower’s proceedings were pending before the Ld. NCLT,
Chennai.

33. Moreover, the Corporate Debtor and BHPL are subsidiaries of RAB
Enterprises with common ownership and promoters, forming a single integrated
group. For this reason, the Ld. NCLT, by an Order dated 12 June 2026 has
consolidated their CIRPs.

34. Section 5(5A) of the IBC defines a “corporate guarantor” as a corporate
person who is the surety in a contract of guarantee to a corporate debtor. Section
126 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 defines a contract of guarantee as “a
contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person in
case of his default”, and provides that it “may be either oral or written”. Each
requirement is satisfied as BHPL is the principal borrower, IHFL (now
Respondent No. 2) is the creditor, and the Corporate Debtor, as an Obligor, is
the surety. Clauses 2.1 and 12.2 of the BH Loan Agreement make the Obligors,
including the Corporate Debtor, jointly and severally liable for the BH Debt, and
hence liable to pay upon BHPL’s default. A covenant to pay upon shortfall, such
as Clause 2.2.4, has been construed as a guarantee by the Hon’ble Supreme
Court in China Development Bank v. Doha Bank QPSC, (2025) 7 SCC 729. The
covenant under Clause 5(iii) of the Deed of Hypothecation considered in that case
is, in substance, identical to Clauses 2.1 and 2.2.4 of the BH Loan Agreement
read with Clause 1 of the Deed of Hypothecation. Thus the Ld. NCLT has
correctly applied China Development Bank judgement while interpreting Section
                                                              26

60(2). The Corporate Debtor’s liability is, therefore, not limited to the
hypothecated assets, Clause 2.2.4 obliges the Obligors to make good any
shortfall from any source.

35. The Appellant’s plea based on nomenclature is equally untenable. China
Development Bank (supra) reiterates the settled principle that the substance of
a document’s covenants, irrespective of its nomenclature, is decisive. Thus the
Corporate Debtor if not described as a “guarantor” is, therefore, immaterial. It is
settled law that a contract of guarantee can be recorded in multiple agreements,
so long as the intent to stand as guarantor is clearly discernible. The mere fact
that BHPL has not signed the Deed of Hypothecation is irrelevant, since the Deed
of Hypothecation itself evidences the assumption of all obligations under the BH
Loan Agreement. Read together, the BH Loan Agreement and the Deed of
Hypothecation evidence a tripartite arrangement between the principal borrower
(BHPL), the creditor (IHFL, now Respondent No. 2) and the surety (the Corporate
Debtor), constituting a contract of guarantee. The plea is all the more untenable
coming from the Appellant, who executed the BH Loan Agreement on behalf of
BHPL and the Deed of Hypothecation on behalf of the Corporate Debtor. As a
common director of both companies under RAB Enterprises, the Appellant
cannot now raise such contentions.

36. In identical circumstances, the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Punjab
National Bank v. Shri Vikram Cotton Mills, (1970) 1 SCC 60, a decision cited by
the Appellant itself, held the absence of all parties’ signatures on a single
                                                              27

document does not negate a guarantee. A guarantee can be inferred from conduct,
from an oral agreement or a combined reading of several agreements.

37. Thus independently of its liability as guarantor of BHPL, the Corporate
Debtor is liable for the Imagine Debt. As held by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in
Innoventive Industries Ltd. v. ICICI Bank, (2018) 1 SCC 407, and E.S.
Krishnamurthy v. Bharath HiTech Builders Pvt. Ltd., (2022) 3 SCC 161, once
the Adjudicating Authority is satisfied on the occurrence of debt and default, it
must admit the petition.

38. The Appellant has not denied the execution of the Imagine Loan
Agreements, the disbursal or the failure to repay.

39. Thus, the appeals are not maintainable on this count alone. The Impugned
Order suffers from no illegality and thus all above appeals are dismissed.
