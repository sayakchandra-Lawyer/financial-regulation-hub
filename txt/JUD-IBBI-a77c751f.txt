3. Ganesh Benzoplast Ltd., the appellant, is a company incorporated
in Maharashtra during the year 1986. It is involved in the manufacture and
storage of chemicals. Its infrastructure division provides storage tank
terminals for storage of petroleum products, oils, etc., at the ports in the
State. The appellant had business transactions with the STC to facilitate
supply of toluene and allied products to it in the year 2003 and for export
of its industrial lubricants during the years 2004 to 2006.
4. While so, the appellant sought reference under Section 15(1) of the
Sick Industrial Companies (Special Provisions) Act, 19854, before the
Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction (BIFR) due to erosion of
its net worth, as reflected in its audited balance sheet for the financial year
ended 31.03.2008. The reference was registered as Case No. 42 of 2009.
On 06.05.2010, the BIFR declared the appellant a sick industrial unit, in

terms of Section 3(1)(o) of the SICA and appointed an operating agency

for formulating a Draft Rehabilitation Scheme (DRS).

5. The DRS was circulated on 18.04.2013 with the cut-off date fixed as
31.03.2011. After statutory hearings were held, the DRS was modified as

directed and was submitted for approval to the BIFR. However, as the

BIFR ceased to function, owing to absence of a member, the appellant

approached the Delhi High Court by way of Writ Petition (C) No. 9320 of

2015 seeking approval of the DRS. The writ petition was allowed by a

Division Bench of the Delhi High Court on 04.12.2015. Significantly, the

DRS and the approved Rehabilitation Scheme (RS) provided for

unsecured liabilities being scaled down under Clause 11.11. This clause

reads as follows:-

         ‘11.11 Unsecured Liabilities
         a) The unsecured creditors would be paid at 25% of the
         principal dues in five equal annual instalment with an option
         not to accept the scaled down value of their dues and wait till
         the scheme of rehabilitation of the company has worked itself
         out/Seven years. M/s Pennar Paterson also will be treated at
         par with other unsecured creditors. Unsecured creditors also
         includes contingent liabilities, if crystallized.’

6. Annexure V appended to the DRS/RS furnished the details of the

unsecured creditors of the appellant as on 31.03.2011. The STC did not

figure in this list of 191 unsecured creditors. On the other hand, it was

shown in Annexure VI(a), titled ‘List of Contingent Liabilities of Ganesh

Benzoplast Ltd.’. The STC figured at Serial No. 6 in this list of ten

                                                        3
contingent liabilities and its claimed amount was shown as ₹11.35 crores.
Therefore, in terms of Clause 11.11 of the DRS/RS, till its liability stood
crystallized, STC could not attain the status of an ‘unsecured creditor’.
Despite it being shown as a ‘contingent liability’ in the DRS/RS, the STC
did not choose to challenge the same. The DRS had been published in
the newspapers on 09.05.2013 but despite the same, the STC never
objected to its status therein or the manner in which it was to be treated
even if it attained the status of an ‘unsecured creditor’. Even after approval
of the DRS by the Delhi High Court, vide order dated 04.12.2015, the STC
remained unmoved. The said order was challenged by a third party,
Avron Chemicals Pvt. Ltd., an unsecured creditor of the appellant, before
this Court, by way of SLP(C) No. 12096 of 2016. The SLP was dismissed
by this Court on 10.04.2018 observing that, given the facts of the case,
this Court was not inclined to interfere with the said order.
7. However, the STC deemed it fit to issue a demand notice on
20.03.2018 to the appellant under Section 8 of the IBC, claiming that a
sum of ₹24,26,44,490/- was due and payable to it. This sum comprised
₹17,00,05,876/- towards dues of export transactions; ₹3,68,62,534/-
towards domestic transactions; and ₹3,57,76,081/- for the appellant’s
failure in submitting ‘C’ Forms under the Central Sales Tax Act. The
appellant denied its liability, by reply dated 31.03.2018. Thereupon, the
STC filed Company Petition (IB) No. 1975/MB/2018 before the NCLT on

                                                        4
05.06.2018 under Section 9 of the IBC. During the pendency of the case,
the appellant handed over the requisite ‘C’ Forms to the STC on
30.01.2020.
8. In the order dated 13.02.2020 passed in the said company petition,
the NCLT noted that the STC had filed a rejoinder wherein it stated that,
as per the scheme sanctioned by the BIFR, it would be entitled to receive
25% of the principal dues in five equal instalments, treating it as
unsecured creditor. The NCLT also noted that the issue relating to
submission of ‘C’ Forms stood settled due to the submission of such forms
by the appellant on 30.01.2020. Further, the NCLT took note of the letter
dated 04.10.2019 addressed to the STC by the appellant, offering to settle
the issue by paying ₹1.75 crore, by way of compromise, in full and final
settlement of the STC’s claims. The NCLT observed that the said offer
was reasonable, as STC could only be treated as an ‘unsecured creditor’
in terms of the RS. Though the STC insisted on admission of its company
petition, the NCLT opined that payment of 25% of its dues, as offered by
the appellant, would meet the ends of justice and, accordingly, quantified
the amount payable as ₹2,18,88,421/-. Directing that this amount should
be paid within 30 days from the date of receipt of the order, the NCLT
disposed of the company petition.
9. This order was subjected to challenge by the STC before the
NCLAT, by way of Company Appeal (AT)(INS) No. 753/2021. However, in

                                                        5
the first instance, the NCLAT dismissed the appeal on the ground of
limitation, by judgment dated 20.04.2023. Aggrieved thereby, the STC
filed Civil Appeal No. 5477 of 2023 before this Court. The appeal was
allowed on 02.04.2025, holding that the STC’s appeal before the NCLAT
ought to have been treated as having been filed within time. The STC’s
appeal was restored to its original position and it was directed to be heard
and decided on merits. It was pursuant to this remand order, that the
NCLAT reheard the matter and allowed the appeal on 21.01.2026,
directing the STC’s company petition to be restored on the file of the NCLT
for decision afresh, leading to the filing of the present appeal.
10. Perusal of the impugned judgment dated 21.01.2026 reflects that
the NCLAT was of the opinion that the bone of contention between the
parties was with regard to treatment of the dues of the STC and observed
that, an unsecured creditor had two options under the RS, namely, to
accept 25% of its principal dues in instalments or, if it did not accept the
scaled-down value of its dues, wait till the rehabilitation scheme worked
itself out or seven years and agitate for its dues thereafter. The STC
contended that, if it was to be treated as an unsecured creditor, it had to
choose between the two options and the NCLT could not have forced it to
take the first option of accepting only 25% of its dues. It was brought to
the notice of the NCLAT that, pursuant to the order dated 13.02.2020
passed by the NCLT, the appellant had already paid ₹2,18,88,421/- to the

                                                        6
STC, which had accepted the same without prejudice. In that regard, the
STC contended that the appellant should have paid the 25% amount in
five annual instalments from 2015, as per Clause 11.11, but the appellant
paid a lumpsum amount only after the passing of the order dated
13.02.2020. The STC, therefore, argued that such payment was not in
keeping with Clause 11.11 and it could not be forced to accept the same.
Concurring with the STC on this point, the NCLAT observed that there was
no explanation as to why the STC’s dues were shown as a contingent
liability and why, when the appellant denied that liability altogether, the
NCLT had ordered payment of 25% of the principal dues. Holding so, the
NCLAT allowed the appeal and restored the company petition.
11. The reasoning of the NCLAT failed to take note of the fact that the
STC issued a demand notice on 20.03.2018, well within the seven year
period under the so-called second option in Clause 11.11 of the RS
sanctioned by the Delhi High Court on 04.12.2015. Further, the NCLAT
also lost sight of the fact that the options were available only to an
‘unsecured creditor’ and not to claimants under the category of ‘contingent
liabilities’. It was only upon crystallization of such a claim that a contingent
liability could be treated as an unsecured debt. Admittedly, there was no
crystallization of the STC’s claim, whereby it could call itself an ‘unsecured
creditor’. Therefore, be it viewed from any angle, at the point of time that
the STC issued a demand notice and even when it filed a company

                                                        7
petition, it did not act as per Clause 11.11 of the RS. Further, though the
NCLAT deemed it fit to wonder why the STC’s claim was treated as a
contingent liability, the inescapable fact remains that the STC itself
accepted such categorization and never challenged it. It was, therefore,
too late in the day for it to claim otherwise or raise that issue.
12. Insofar as the so-called second option under Clause 11.11 of the RS
is concerned, the NCLAT strangely refused to give effect to the decision
of this Court in Modi Rubber Limited vs. Continental Carbon India
Limited5. Therein, this Court noted that after amendment of the SICA in
1994, the BIFR was empowered to scale-down/vary the dues of creditors,
including decree holders, arbitration award holders and industrial award
holders. This Court held that, once a rehabilitation scheme is approved by
the BIFR, it would be binding on the unsecured creditors also, as there is
no difference between unsecured creditors and creditors. It was further
held that unsecured creditors did not have the option to not accept the
scaling-down of their dues as the rehabilitation scheme itself would be
rendered unworkable if this is permitted, thereby frustrating the very
objective of the SICA. This Court held that minority creditors and
unsecured creditors could not be permitted to stall rehabilitation of a sick
company by not accepting the scaled-down value of their dues as unless
and until there was a sacrifice by all concerned, including creditors,

5  (2023) 17 SCC 263

                      8
financial institutions, unsecured creditors and labourers, there could not
be revival of the sick industrial company.
13. In Fertilizer Corporation of India Limited and others vs.
Coromandel Sacks Private Limited6, this Court affirmed and applied the
ratio laid down in Modi Rubber Limited (supra). That was a case where
a holder of a decree against a sick company was not even part of the
rehabilitation scheme approved by the BIFR but, despite the same, this
Court held that, had it been part of the scheme, it would have had to settle
for the scaled-down value of its dues, as a similar approach had been
adopted in the scheme for settling the dues of other creditors and in such
a scenario, the decree-holder would not have had any other option but to
accept the scaled-down value and settle its dues.
14. In the light of the aforestated edicts, the option given under Clause
11.11 to unsecured creditors to wait till the scheme worked itself out or for
seven years and thereafter agitate their claim for their full dues was not
valid. Once the DRS was sanctioned, the approved RS was binding on all
concerned, i.e., the unsecured creditors and the unsecured creditors in
waiting, and there was no possibility of reopening the issue after the
scheme worked itself out or after seven years, thereby putting the
appellant, which stood rehabilitated, at the risk of becoming sick again.
That being so, when the STC was not even treated as an ‘unsecured

6  (2024) 8 SCC 172

                     9
creditor’ but remained a mere ‘contingent liability’ as on the date of
approval of the DRS by the Delhi High Court on 04.12.2015, the question
of it claiming a higher status than an ‘unsecured creditor’, whereby it could
agitate for the full dues claimed by it, did not arise.
15. The distinction sought to be drawn by the NCLAT, when faced with
the decision of this Court in Modi Rubber Limited (supra), on the ground
that the STC was not an ‘unsecured creditor’ but only a ‘contingent
liability’, defies logic as the STC could, at best, attain the status of an
‘unsecured creditor’, if its dues stood crystalized and upon such an event
materializing, Clause 11.11 of the RS only brought the STC on par with
the unsecured creditors listed in Annexure-V appended to the RS,
whereby it could only claim 25% of its principal dues and no more.
16. The STC’s contention that, as it was not paid 25% of its principal
dues in five annual instalments starting from the year 2015, it could not be
brought within the ambit of Clause 11.11 of the RS, is misconceived, as it
was only upon crystallization of its dues that the STC, which was shown
as a ‘contingent liability’ in Annexure VI(a), attained the status of an
‘unsecured creditor’ and was then entitled to payment of 25% of its dues
in five annual instalments. Admittedly, the STC did not initiate any steps
to have its dues crystalized and straightaway proceeded to issue a notice
under Section 8 of the IBC for its entire dues. The regime of the IBC does
not provide for determination of liability or quantification thereof, whereby

                                                       10
the STC could have sought crystallization of its dues thereunder.
Therefore, the STC was not entitled to seek payment of 25% of its dues
in five annual instalments from 2015 onwards. Despite the fact that the
STC still remained a contingent liability, the appellant addressed the letter
dated 04.10.2019 seeking closure, by offering to pay 25% of its principal
dues without the STC even resorting to crystallization of its dues. This fair
offer was acted upon by the NCLT, which directed payment of 25% of the
STC’s dues, quantified at ₹2,18,88,421/-.
17. Admittedly, the STC’s claim for non-submission of C-Forms stood
decimated by submission of such forms by the appellant on 30.01.2020.
Therefore, only the other two claims relating to export transactions and
domestic transactions remained and taking the same into account, the
NCLT quantified the amount at ₹2,18,88,421/-. No dispute has been
raised before us with regard to such quantification and the STC itself
accepted this amount on 20.03.2020, without prejudice. Viewed thus, it is
clear that nothing remained to be adjudicated in the company petition filed
under Section 9 of the IBC, as the maximum amount that the STC could
have realized, in terms of the approved RS, was already paid to it pursuant
to the NCLT’s order dated 13.02.2020. The NCLAT, therefore, erred in
overlooking this crucial fact and in allowing the STC’s appeal against the
said order and restoring the company petition for decision afresh though
there was nothing left to be adjudicated therein.
