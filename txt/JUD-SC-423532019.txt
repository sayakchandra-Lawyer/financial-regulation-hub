Issue I- Scope of compulsory third-party insurance under
the MV Act

8. The first question requires an examination of the
        statutory framework governing compulsory insurance

                                                                                          Page 8 of 43
        and the nature of the liability which an insurer is
        required to undertake under the MV Act.
9. Chapter XI of the MV Act deals with insurance of motor
        vehicles against third-party risks. Section 145(g) of the
        MV Act defines the expression “third party” in the
        following terms:

             “145 (g) ‘third party’ includes the Government.”

10. The definition is inclusive and does not exhaustively
        enumerate the persons who may qualify as third
        parties. However, it cannot be considered in isolation
        from the remaining provisions of Chapter XI. The
        question is not merely who may be described as a third
        party, but whether the particular risk sought to be
        enforced against the insurer falls within the compulsory
        coverage prescribed by the statute.

11. Moreover, Section 146(1) of the MV Act prohibits the use
        of a motor vehicle in a public place, subject to the
        statutory exceptions, unless a policy of insurance
        complying with Chapter XI is in force. The provision
        thus makes insurance compulsory, but the extent of the
        obligation is determined by Section 147 of the MV Act,
        which prescribes the requirements of such a policy.

12. Since the accident in the present case occurred on
        15.04.2012, the provisions of the MV Act as they stood
        on that date would govern the determination of the
        statutory liability of the Insurance Company. The

                                                                                          Page 9 of 43
        relevant portion of Section 147(1) of the MV Act, as

        applicable at the time, reads as follows:

             “147 (1) In order to comply with the requirements
             of this Chapter, a policy of insurance must be a
             policy which—
             (a) is issued by a person who is an authorised
             insurer; and
             (b) insures the person or classes of persons
             specified in the policy to the extent specified in
             sub-section (2)—
             (i) against any liability which may be incurred by
             him in respect of the death of or bodily injury to
             any person, including owner of the goods or his
             authorized representative carried in the vehicle or
             damage to any property of a third party caused by
             or arising out of the use of the vehicle in a public
             place;
             (ii) against the death of or bodily injury to any
             passenger of a public service vehicle caused by or
             arising out of the use of the vehicle in a public
             place:”

13. The statutory language requires careful consideration.

        Section 147(1)(b)(i) of the MV Act refers to liability

        arising from the death of or bodily injury to any person,

        including the owner of goods or his authorised

        representative carried in the vehicle. Section 147(1)(b)(ii)

        of the MV Act, on the other hand, specifically deals with

        liability arising from the death of or bodily injury to

        passengers travelling in a public service vehicle.

14. The legislature has thus distinguished between the

        general obligation to insure against third-party risks

        and the obligation to insure particular categories of

                                                                                        Page 10 of 43
        persons travelling in a vehicle. The express inclusion of
        the owner of goods or his authorised representative
        under Section 147(1)(b)(i), together with the separate
        provision concerning passengers of a public service
        vehicle, is significant.
15. If the expression “any person” were interpreted to
        include every individual travelling in any vehicle, the
        specific references to owners of goods and passengers of
        public service vehicles would lose their significance.
        Such an interpretation would render the legislative
        distinctions between classes of vehicles and passengers
        largely redundant.
16. The provisions of an enactment must be read
        harmoniously, giving effect to the language employed by
        the legislature without rendering any provision
        unnecessary or without purpose. An interpretation
        which treats every occupant or passenger as
        compulsorily insured merely because such person is
        neither the insured nor the insurer does not sufficiently
        account for the structure of Section 147 of the MV Act.
17. Section 147(1) of the MV Act also contains a proviso
        regulating the compulsory coverage of certain categories
        of employees, including persons engaged in driving
        vehicles, conductors of public service vehicles and
        employees carried in goods carriages. This further
        demonstrates that the legislature has identified
        particular liabilities required to be insured. The

                                                                                        Page 11 of 43
        statutory obligation cannot therefore be determined
        solely by reference to the general expression “any
        person”.
18. Section 147(2) of the MV Act, as it stood at the relevant
        time, prescribed the limits of liability under a policy
        complying with Section 147(1) of the MV Act. The
        relevant provision reads as follows:

             “147 (2) Subject to the proviso to sub-section (1), a
             policy of insurance referred to in sub-section (1),
             shall cover any liability incurred in respect of any
             accident, up to the following limits, namely:—
             (a) save as provided in clause (b), the amount of
             liability incurred;
             (b) in respect of damage to any property of a third
             party, a limit of rupees six thousand:”

19. The expression “any liability incurred” under Section
        147(2) of the MV Act must be understood with reference
        to the risks required to be insured under Section 147(1)
        of the MV Act. Sub-section (2) determines the extent of
        liability in respect of a risk falling within the statutory
        coverage. It does not independently enlarge the
        categories of persons or risks which must be insured.

20. The distinction between statutory coverage and
        contractual coverage is further reinforced by Section
        147(5) of the MV Act, which provides as follows:

             “147 (5) Notwithstanding anything contained in
             any law for the time being in force, an insurer
             issuing a policy of insurance under this section

                                                                                        Page 12 of 43
             shall be liable to indemnify the person or classes
             of persons specified in the policy in respect of any
             liability which the policy purports to cover in the
             case of that person or those classes of persons.”

21. The aforesaid provision recognises that an insurer may
        undertake liability under the terms of the insurance
        policy. Therefore, the absence of compulsory statutory
        coverage does not necessarily exclude liability arising
        under a wider contract of insurance. The liability of an
        insurer must consequently be determined by examining
        both the minimum coverage prescribed by the MV Act
        and any additional coverage undertaken through the
        policy.

22. Section 149(1) of the MV Act, as applicable at the
        relevant time, also bears upon this distinction. The
        provision imposed a duty upon the insurer to satisfy
        judgments and awards in respect of liability required to
        be covered under Section 147(1)(b), being a liability
        covered by the terms of the policy, subject to the
        conditions prescribed therein. The obligation under
        Section 149 of the MV Act must therefore be understood
        in conjunction with the scope of compulsory coverage
        under Section 147 of the MV Act.

23. At this stage, it is necessary to distinguish between two
        legally distinct situations. The first is where a risk falls
        within the statutory or contractual coverage, but the
        insured has committed a breach of the conditions

                                                                                        Page 13 of 43
        governing the policy. The second is where the risk itself
        is neither required to be insured under the statute nor
        covered by the contract of insurance.
24. In the former situation, the liability of the insurer and
        the consequences of breach must be examined with
        reference to Section 149 of the MV Act and the
        principles laid down by this Court. In the latter, the
        foundational obligation to indemnify the particular risk
        is absent. The mere existence of an insurance policy in
        respect of the vehicle does not establish that every
        liability arising from its use is covered.
25. In New India Assurance Co. Ltd. v. Asha Rani3, a
        three-Judge Bench of this Court considered whether the
        compulsory insurance contemplated under Section 147
        of the MV Act extended to gratuitous passengers
        travelling in a goods vehicle. The Court held that the
        expression “any person” could not be interpreted
        independently of the statutory context and concluded
        that compulsory coverage did not extend to such
        passengers. In doing so, it overruled the broader
        interpretation previously adopted in New India
        Assurance Co. Ltd. v. Satpal Singh4, which had
        treated the expression as extending to gratuitous
        passengers irrespective of the category of vehicle.

3 (2003) 2 SCC 223
4 (2000) 1 SCC 237

                    Page 14 of 43
26. The position was further clarified by another three-
        Judge Bench in National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Baljit
        Kaur5. The Court considered the effect of the
        amendment to Section 147 of the MV Act introduced in
        1994, whereby the owner of goods or his authorised
        representative carried in the vehicle was expressly
        included within compulsory insurance coverage. It held
        that the amendment did not extend such coverage to
        every passenger travelling in a goods vehicle. The
        reasoning in Baljit Kaur (supra) is significant. If every
        passenger were already included within the expression
        “any person”, the express legislative inclusion of the
        owner of goods or his authorised representative would
        serve no meaningful purpose. The decision thus
        reaffirmed that the scope of compulsory insurance must
        be determined from the statutory scheme.

27. In United India Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Tilak Singh6,
        this Court considered whether a statutory insurance
        policy covered the risk of death or bodily injury to a
        gratuitous pillion rider on a scooter. The Court
        examined the earlier decisions, including Asha Rani
        (supra), and held that the principle governing
        gratuitous passengers in goods vehicles was equally
        applicable to gratuitous passengers travelling in other
        categories of vehicles. Accordingly, the insurer was held

5 (2004) 2 SCC 1
6 (2006) 4 SCC 404

                    Page 15 of 43
        not liable for the death of the pillion rider, since the
        statutory policy did not cover that risk.
28. The question was considered more directly in Oriental
        Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Sudhakaran K.V.7. This Court
        examined whether a pillion rider could be treated as a
        third party for the purpose of fastening liability upon the
        insurer under Section 147. Upon consideration of the
        statutory provisions and the earlier decisions, the Court
        held that the insurer's liability under an Act Only Policy
        did not extend to the risk of a pillion rider unless the
        requisite premium had been paid. The Court further
        clarified that a pillion rider could not be treated as a
        third party for the purpose of imposing liability upon the
        insurer of the very vehicle on which the person was
        travelling, where the accident had occurred due to the
        negligent riding of that vehicle.
29. A further distinction between an Act Only Policy and a
        Comprehensive or Package Policy was explained in
        National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Balakrishnan8. This
        Court recognised that while an Act Only Policy provides
        the coverage required by the statute, a Comprehensive
        or Package Policy may extend to occupants of private
        vehicles in accordance with its terms and the applicable
        regulatory directions.

7 (2008) 7 SCC 428
8 (2013) 1 SCC 731

                    Page 16 of 43
30. The decisions discussed above establish that
        compulsory insurance under Section 147 of the MV Act
        and wider contractual coverage operate upon distinct
        legal foundations. The absence of compulsory coverage
        for a particular category of persons does not prevent an
        insurer from assuming that risk through an appropriate
        policy. Equally, liability cannot be imposed solely
        because the vehicle was insured against third-party
        risks.

31. We may now return to the question which prompted the
        reference. It cannot be answered merely by identifying
        every person other than the insured and the insurer as
        a third party entitled to compulsory coverage, without
        examining the nature of the risk insured.

32. In our considered view, the distinction lies between the
        status of a person and the character of the risk sought
        to be enforced against the insurer. A person may be a
        third party in the ordinary sense of being a stranger to
        the contract of insurance. However, that circumstance
        alone does not establish that every risk arising from the
        person's presence in the insured vehicle is required to
        be covered by an Act Only Policy.

33. It is equally important to clarify that an occupant of a
        private vehicle or a pillion rider does not cease to be a
        third party for every purpose merely because such
        person was travelling in a vehicle. Where the negligence
        of another vehicle gives rise to a claim, such person may

                                                                                        Page 17 of 43
        stand in the position of a third party in relation to that
        vehicle and its insurer. The present question concerns
        the liability of the insurer of the vehicle in which the
        person was travelling, where the risk is not covered
        under the applicable policy.
34. We are conscious that the MV Act is a beneficial
        legislation intended to provide compensation to victims
        of motor accidents and their dependants. Its provisions
        must receive an interpretation which advances that
        object. Nevertheless, the beneficial nature of a statute
        cannot justify an interpretation which disregards the
        distinctions expressly drawn by the legislature or
        imposes upon an insurer a liability which the statutory
        framework does not contemplate.
35. The interpretation adopted in Tilak Singh (supra) and
        Sudhakaran K.V. (supra) is consistent with the
        statutory scheme and the principles laid down by the
        larger Benches in Asha Rani (supra) and Baljit Kaur
        (supra). We find no reason to depart from the position
        that compulsory insurance under Section 147 of the MV
        Act does not automatically extend to the risk of injury
        or death of a gratuitous occupant of a private vehicle or
        a pillion rider on a two-wheeler under an Act Only
        Policy.
36. Accordingly, we answer the first question in the
        negative. We hold that a person does not become
        entitled to compulsory insurance coverage under

                                                                                        Page 18 of 43
        Section 147 merely because such person is neither the
        insured nor the insurer. The liability of an insurer under
        an Act Only Policy must be determined by reference to
        the risks which the MV Act requires to be insured.
        Where the risk falls outside such coverage, liability
        cannot be imposed unless the terms of the insurance
        policy otherwise provide for it.
37. This conclusion settles the extent of the insurer's
        statutory and contractual liability. It leaves open the
        distinct question whether this Court may, in exercise of
        its extraordinary jurisdiction under Article 142 of the
        Constitution, direct an insurer to first satisfy an award
        and thereafter recover the amount from the person
        legally liable.

Issue II- Scope and limitations of the power under Article
142 of the Constitution
38. We now turn to Article 142 of the Constitution and the

        limitations governing its exercise in motor accident
        compensation cases.
39. Article 142(1) of the Constitution empowers this Court
        to pass such decree or make such order as is necessary
        for doing complete justice in any cause or matter
        pending before it. The provision enables this Court to
        secure a just and effective resolution where the ordinary
        application of law may not adequately serve the ends of
        justice.

                                                                                        Page 19 of 43
40. The power under Article 142 of the Constitution is
        distinct from the jurisdiction conferred upon courts and
        tribunals by ordinary legislation. Its exercise is not
        necessarily confined to the reliefs expressly
        contemplated by a particular statute. Nevertheless, the
        amplitude of the power does not dispense with the
        requirement of judicial restraint. The pursuit of
        complete justice must remain consistent with the
        fundamental principles underlying the applicable legal
        framework.

41. In Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of
        India9, a Constitution Bench of this Court considered
        the scope and limitations of Article 142 of the
        Constitution. The Court recognised that the power is
        supplementary and curative in nature, intended to
        prevent injustice in a cause or matter pending before it.
        At the same time, it cautioned that such power cannot
        be employed to disregard substantive rights or to
        supplant the law applicable to the controversy.

42. The constitutional position has since been
        comprehensively examined by a Constitution Bench in
        Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan10. Upon
        considering the earlier decisions, including Union
        Carbide Corporation v. Union of India11, and

9 (1998) 4 SCC 409
10 (2023) 14 SCC 231
11 (1991) 4 SCC 584

                      Page 20 of 43
        Supreme Court Bar Association (supra), the Court
        explained that the power under Article 142 of the
        Constitution is not invariably restricted by the
        procedural or substantive requirements of ordinary
        legislation. However, its exercise must remain informed
        by considerations of fundamental general and specific
        public policy, including the underlying principles of the
        statutory framework. The Constitution Bench further
        emphasised that this Court cannot altogether ignore
        substantive legislative provisions. The power to do
        complete justice must be exercised with due regard to
        the competing rights and interests of the parties.
43. These principles assume particular significance in the
        context of compulsory motor vehicle insurance. The
        statutory scheme identifies the risks which an insurer
        is required to cover and permits the undertaking of
        wider liability through a contract of insurance. A general
        direction requiring insurers to satisfy awards in respect
        of risks falling outside both forms of coverage would
        undermine the distinctions recognised under Section
        147 of the MV Act.
44. However, a direction requiring an insurer to satisfy a
        particular award in the first instance, while preserving
        its right to recover the amount from the person legally
        liable, stands on a different footing. Such a direction
        does not necessarily alter the determination of liability
        under the statute or the insurance contract. Rather, it

                                                                                        Page 21 of 43
        concerns the manner in which an established liability
        may exceptionally be satisfied. This distinction does not
        render the interests of the insurer inconsequential.
        Recovery proceedings may involve considerable delay
        and expense, and the amount advanced may not
        ultimately be recovered in full.
45. Before examining the circumstances in which such
        intervention may be permissible, it is necessary to
        consider the judicial precedents concerning the
        principle of pay and recover. In National Insurance Co.
        Ltd. v. Swaran Singh12, this Court examined the
        consequences of a breach of policy conditions relating
        to the driving licence of the person operating the insured
        vehicle. The Court recognised that, in cases involving
        covered third-party risks, the insurer could be required
        to satisfy the award in the first instance and thereafter
        recover the amount from the insured.
46. The principle was subsequently applied in Shamanna
        v. Divisional Manager, Oriental Insurance Co.
        Ltd.13. However, the controversy in that case concerned
        the absence of a valid driving licence, which constituted
        a breach of the conditions of the policy. The decision
        must therefore be understood in the context of a risk
        otherwise falling within the statutory coverage. It does

12 (2004) 3 SCC 297
13 (2018) 9 SCC 650

                     Page 22 of 43
        not, by itself, determine the availability of pay-and-
        recover relief where the risk was never insured.
47. A different line of decisions concerns cases in which the
        insurer was found not to be liable for the particular risk,
        but was nevertheless directed to satisfy the
        compensation award. In Baljit Kaur (supra), this Court
        held that gratuitous passengers travelling in goods
        vehicles were not covered by the compulsory insurance
        requirements. Having clarified the legal position, the
        Court nevertheless directed the insurer to satisfy the
        award and recover the amount from the vehicle owner,
        having regard to the circumstances in which the claim
        had arisen and the earlier understanding of the law.
48. In National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Challa
        Bharathamma14, this Court similarly directed the
        insurer to satisfy the award despite finding that it was
        not legally liable to indemnify the insured. The Court
        also prescribed safeguards for recovery, including the
        entitlement of the insurer to initiate execution
        proceedings without instituting a separate suit and the
        furnishing of security by the vehicle owner. The decision
        recognises both the need to protect claimants and the
        importance of safeguarding the insurer's recovery
        rights.

14 (2004) 8 SCC 517

                     Page 23 of 43
49. The question arose again in National Insurance Co.
        Ltd. v. Saju P. Paul15. While holding that the insurer
        was not liable for injuries sustained by a gratuitous
        passenger travelling in a goods vehicle, this Court
        directed the insurer to satisfy the award and recover the
        amount from the owner. The Court placed particular
        emphasis upon the prolonged pendency of the
        proceedings, the permanent disability suffered by the
        claimant and the fact that compensation had remained
        unpaid for several years.

50. The approach adopted in Saju P. Paul (supra) was
        subsequently followed in Manuara Khatun v. Rajesh
        Kumar Singh16. This Court recognised that the insurer
        was not liable under the applicable policy but
        nevertheless directed payment and recovery,
        considering the circumstances of the claimants and the
        considerable time which had elapsed since the accident.
        Similarly, in Anu Bhanvara v. IFFCO Tokio General
        Insurance Co. Ltd.17, the Court invoked the principle
        having regard to the permanent disabilities suffered by
        the claimants, who were children at the time of the
        accident.

    51. The reservations expressed by the referring Bench
        in Parvathneni (supra) assume particular significance

15 (2013) 2 SCC 41
16 (2017) 4 SCC 796
17 (2020) 20 SCC 632

                      Page 24 of 43
   in the present context. The concern was whether an
   insurer, upon establishing that it had no liability under
   law, could nevertheless be compelled to satisfy an award
   merely because it was granted liberty to subsequently
   recover the amount from the vehicle owner. The Bench
   also recognised the practical difficulty that such
   recovery may take several years or may ultimately prove
   impossible.

52. These concerns raise a substantial question
   regarding the permissible limits of Article 142 of the
   Constitution. They require this Court to consider
   whether directing an insurer to satisfy an uninsured
   liability in exceptional circumstances would constitute
   an impermissible creation of liability, or whether such a
   direction may be justified as a measure for securing
   complete justice without disturbing the ultimate legal
   responsibility of the vehicle owner. We find that the
   objection in Parvathneni (supra) cannot be answered
   merely by observing that the insurer would be entitled
   to recover the amount. A right of recovery does not
   guarantee actual reimbursement. Equally, the absence
   of statutory or contractual liability cannot, by itself, be
   treated as an absolute prohibition upon the exercise of
   Article 142 of the Constitution. The question must be
   examined in the light of the constitutional principles

                                                                                   Page 25 of 43
        subsequently clarified by the Constitution Bench in
        Shilpa Sailesh (supra).
53. In our considered view, the distinction is between
        enlarging an insurer’s liability as a matter of general law
        and exceptionally directing it to satisfy an established
        compensation award in the first instance, while
        preserving the ultimate liability of the person legally
        responsible.
54. That distinction does not, by itself, justify such a
        direction. Even a temporary obligation to pay imposes a
        financial burden upon an insurer who is otherwise not
        liable. The exercise of Article 142 of the Constitution
        must account for that burden as well as the interests of
        the claimant.
55. The constitutional position explained in Shilpa Sailesh
        (supra) provides the appropriate framework for
        resolving this question. The exercise of Article 142 of the
        Constitution is not necessarily prohibited merely
        because the relief contemplated is not expressly
        provided under ordinary legislation. The relevant
        consideration is whether the proposed exercise would
        contravene a fundamental principle underlying
        substantive law or amount to an impermissible
        substitution of the statutory framework.
56. Section 147 of the MV Act defines the extent of
        compulsory insurance coverage. It does not purport to
        regulate or restrict the constitutional jurisdiction of this

                                                                                        Page 26 of 43
        Court under Article 142 of the Constitution.
        Nevertheless, the statutory allocation of insurance
        liability remains an important consideration in
        determining what constitutes complete justice. The
        power cannot be exercised in a manner which makes
        the distinction between insured and uninsured risks
        practically meaningless.
57. We may, in this context, turn to some recent decisions
        of this Court. In Sunita v. United India Insurance Co.
        Ltd.18, the Court directed the insurer to satisfy the
        compensation award and thereafter recover the amount
        from the owner, notwithstanding the absence of
        coverage for a gratuitous passenger under a Liability
        Only Policy. On the other hand, in Sri Ranga Naik v.
        United India Insurance Co. Ltd.19, this Court declined
        to apply the principle of pay and recover where the risk
        of the injured passenger fell outside the coverage of the
        policy. The Court distinguished a breach of policy
        conditions from a case in which the insurer had not
        undertaken the particular risk. The decision did not,
        however, undertake a separate examination of the
        circumstances in which the extraordinary jurisdiction
        under Article 142 may be exercised.
58. These decisions demonstrate that pay-and-recover relief
        has been granted in certain cases involving uninsured

18 2025 INSC 867
19 Civil Appeal 4281 of 2026

                              Page 27 of 43
        risks, but cannot follow automatically. The
        constitutional question requires a principled
        justification for exceptional intervention to secure
        complete justice.
59. The object of the MV Act is not merely to determine
        compensation, but also to secure meaningful relief to
        motor accident victims and their dependants. An award
        which remains unrealised for an unreasonable period
        may, depending upon the circumstances, fail to achieve
        that object.
60. In Anita Kushwaha v. Pushap Sudan20, a
        Constitution Bench of this Court recognised access to
        justice as an integral facet of the rights guaranteed
        under Articles 14 and 21 of the Constitution. The Court
        emphasised that access to justice encompasses not
        merely the availability of an adjudicatory forum, but
        also an effective, affordable and reasonably expeditious
        process for the enforcement of legal rights. The principle
        assumes particular importance where compensation
        has already been adjudicated, but the claimant
        continues to be deprived of its benefit through
        prolonged proceedings. The constitutional concern also
        extends to whether the remedy remains capable of
        providing meaningful relief to the person for whose
        benefit it exists.

20 (2016) 8 SCC 509

                     Page 28 of 43
61. At this juncture, we also consider it necessary to reflect
        upon the responsibility of insurance companies in the
        administration of motor accident compensation. In
        United India Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Lehru21, this
        Court expressed serious concern over the practice of
        insurers pursuing appeals on questions of law which
        stood settled, thereby depriving claimants of the benefit
        of compensation for several years. Such observations
        underline a larger concern regarding the manner in
        which insurance claims are contested. While insurance
        is undoubtedly a commercial enterprise, it also
        performs an important social function, particularly in
        the context of compulsory motor vehicle insurance. The
        victims of motor accidents and their dependants
        frequently approach the adjudicatory process in
        circumstances of financial distress and personal
        vulnerability. The pursuit of untenable defences or the
        unnecessary prolongation of litigation may, in such
        circumstances, render the eventual award of
        compensation inadequate to remedy the hardship
        already suffered. Insurance companies must therefore
        remain conscious that their right to contest claims
        carries with it a corresponding responsibility to act fairly
        and to avoid conduct which unnecessarily obstructs the
        timely administration of justice.

21 (2003) 3 SCC 338

                     Page 29 of 43
62. We must, however, clarify that an insurer cannot be
        deprived of its legitimate statutory or contractual
        defences merely because the MV Act is a beneficial
        legislation. Nor can the pursuit of a bona fide defence
        concerning the extent of insurance coverage, by itself,
        be regarded as an abuse of the judicial process. Indeed,
        in the present case, we have concluded that the
        Insurance Company bears no statutory or contractual
        liability for the risk in question. Our observations are
        therefore not intended to attribute any improper
        conduct to the Insurance Company herein. Rather, they
        emphasise that the commercial interests of insurers
        must be pursued consistently with the broader social
        purpose of motor accident compensation legislation.
        The extraordinary jurisdiction under Article 142 of the
        Constitution must ultimately be exercised upon the
        constitutional principles discussed above, and not upon
        any general presumption against insurance companies.

63. A related principle of common law is embodied in the
        maxim actus curiae neminem gravabit, which means
        that an act of the Court shall prejudice no one. In A.R.
        Antulay v. R.S. Nayak22, this Court recognised the
        principle that a litigant should not suffer injustice on
        account of an act of the judicial process. The maxim
        reflects the broader obligation of courts to ensure that

22 (1988) 2 SCC 602

                     Page 30 of 43
        the administration of justice does not itself become a
        source of avoidable prejudice.
64. The maxim does not authorise the displacement of
        substantive rights or the imposition of liability upon
        another person merely because litigation has caused
        delay. Its relevance arises particularly where judicial
        orders have materially impeded the realisation of an
        established entitlement for a considerable period.
65. These considerations do not independently create
        liability against the insurer. They inform whether
        exceptional intervention under Article 142 of the
        Constitution is necessary and the permissible extent of
        such intervention.
66. We are therefore of the view that a direction requiring
        an insurer to first satisfy an award, despite the absence
        of statutory or contractual liability, must be governed
        by a structured and sufficiently demanding threshold.
        Such a direction should ordinarily be considered only
        where the following four requirements are satisfied.
67. First, there must be an established and enforceable
        obligation to compensate. The liability of the vehicle
        owner or other responsible person must have been
        judicially determined, and the compensation payable
        must be ascertainable. The absence of liability on the
        part of the insurer must also be established. Article 142
        cannot be invoked to dispense with the ordinary
        adjudication of liability or to compel an insurer to pay

                                                                                        Page 31 of 43
        merely because a claim for compensation has been
        instituted.
68. Second, there must exist an exceptional necessity to
        prevent substantial injustice. The Court must be
        satisfied, on the basis of the record, that requiring the
        claimant to pursue the ordinary course of recovery
        would result in hardship of an exceptional character.
        Relevant considerations may include prolonged non-
        payment of compensation, serious financial or physical
        vulnerability, permanent disability, the circumstances
        of dependent family members and the effect of judicial
        proceedings upon the claimant's ability to obtain timely
        relief.
69. No single circumstance should ordinarily be treated as
        conclusive. The fact that an accident has resulted in
        death or injury cannot, by itself, justify exceptional
        intervention, since such consequences form the very
        foundation of claims under the MV Act. Equally, the
        passage of time, without regard to its causes and
        consequences, cannot automatically warrant relief. The
        circumstances, viewed cumulatively, must disclose a
        substantial injustice beyond the ordinary difficulties of
        enforcing a compensation award.
70. Third, the proposed direction must satisfy the
        requirements of necessity and proportionality. The
        Court must consider whether an equally effective and
        less burdensome remedy is reasonably available. It

                                                                                        Page 32 of 43
        must further examine whether directing the insurer to
        satisfy the award would materially advance the
        claimant's access to compensation and whether that
        benefit justifies the financial and procedural burden
        imposed upon the insurer.
71. Proportionality further requires consideration of the
        amount involved, the duration of non-payment, sums
        already deposited or received and the practical
        prospects of recovery from the person legally liable. The
        financial position of the insurer, by itself, cannot justify
        imposing an uninsured liability.
72. Fourth, the direction must preserve procedural fairness
        and provide appropriate safeguards for recovery. The
        insurer and the person ultimately liable must be
        afforded an opportunity to be heard. Any direction
        requiring payment must expressly preserve the
        insurer's entitlement to recover the amount from the
        legally responsible person and provide an effective
        mechanism for enforcing that entitlement.
73. The requirement of effective recovery safeguards also
        finds support in the equitable doctrine of subrogation
        and the related principle of restitution. The underlying
        object of these principles is to ensure that where a
        person discharges an obligation which legally rests
        upon another, the person ultimately responsible does
        not obtain an unjust benefit at the expense of the
        person making payment. In Krishna Pillai

                                                                                        Page 33 of 43
        Rajasekharan Nair v. Padmanabha Pillai23, this
        Court recognised that the doctrine of subrogation is
        founded upon principles of equity and natural justice,
        and not merely upon contractual relationships. The
        Court also referred to the principle of reimbursement
        embodied in Section 69 of the Indian Contract Act,
        1872, under which a person interested in making a
        payment legally due from another may, upon satisfying
        the prescribed conditions, be entitled to
        reimbursement.
74. The equitable rationale underlying these principles
        assumes relevance in the present context. Where an
        insurer is directed to satisfy a compensation award
        despite the absence of statutory or contractual liability,
        the payment is made for the immediate benefit of the
        claimant, while the underlying obligation continues to
        rest upon the vehicle owner. The object of such a
        direction is not to relieve the owner of liability, but to
        ensure that the claimant is not deprived of meaningful
        compensation in circumstances warranting
        extraordinary intervention. If the owner were permitted
        to obtain the benefit of the payment without remaining
        answerable to the insurer, the relief intended to prevent
        injustice to one party could itself occasion injustice to
        another. Equity requires that the ultimate financial

23 (2004) 12 SCC 754

                      Page 34 of 43
        burden should, as far as reasonably practicable, remain
        upon the person legally responsible for the
        compensation.
75. We must, however, clarify that the doctrine of
        subrogation does not, by itself, authorise this Court to
        compel an insurer to discharge an uninsured liability,
        nor does Section 69 automatically govern every payment
        made pursuant to a judicial direction. The source of
        such extraordinary intervention remains Article 142 of
        the Constitution. The principles of subrogation and
        restitution furnish an additional equitable justification
        for preserving the insurer's right of reimbursement once
        such intervention is found necessary. It is therefore
        incumbent upon this Court to ensure that a direction to
        pay and recover is accompanied by an effective
        mechanism through which the insurer may enforce its
        entitlement against the person ultimately liable. In this
        manner, the exercise of Article 142 of the Constitution
        may secure timely relief to the claimant without
        unnecessarily disturbing the substantive allocation of
        financial responsibility under the MV Act.
76. The adequacy of the recovery mechanism must receive
        genuine consideration. The mere preservation of a right
        to recover cannot be regarded as sufficient where its
        enforcement is demonstrably impracticable. Equally,
        the safeguards contemplated for the insurer must not
        be so onerous as to defeat the purpose of granting

                                                                                        Page 35 of 43
        exceptional relief to the claimant. The Court must
        therefore ensure that the mechanism of recovery is both
        effective and proportionate, maintaining a reasonable
        balance between the competing interests of the parties.
77. These four requirements must be considered together,
        rather than as independent or automatic grounds for
        relief.
78. Even where these requirements are met, Article 142 of
        the Constitution remains extraordinary and
        discretionary. Its exercise must rest upon reasons
        specific to the case, demonstrating why constitutional
        intervention is necessary to secure complete justice.
79. The power cannot be exercised so frequently that an
        exceptional constitutional remedy becomes an
        alternative system of compulsory insurance, thereby
        undermining the statutory distinctions between
        different classes of insurance policies.
80. This restraint protects not merely the commercial
        interests of insurers, but also legal certainty in the
        allocation of responsibility. The beneficial object of the
        MV Act must be pursued consistently with its statutory
        framework and the rights of all affected parties.
81. It is also necessary to clarify that Article 142 of the
        Constitution confers a constitutional power upon this
        Court alone. The principles discussed above do not
        authorise Tribunals or High Courts to invoke an
        equivalent extraordinary jurisdiction where no statutory

                                                                                        Page 36 of 43
        or contractual liability exists. This does not affect their
        power to direct payment and recovery where such relief
        is otherwise permissible under the MV Act and the
        binding precedents governing breaches of policy
        conditions.
82. We accordingly answer the second question by holding
        that the absence of statutory or contractual liability
        does not constitute an absolute bar to the exercise of
        Article 142 of the Constitution. In exceptional cases,
        where an established compensation award remains
        unrealised and the requirements of exceptional
        necessity, proportionality and procedural fairness are
        satisfied, this Court may direct an insurer to first satisfy
        the award and thereafter recover the amount from the
        person legally liable.
83. Such a direction does not enlarge insurance coverage or
        alter the ultimate liability under the MV Act. It remains
        an exceptional constitutional measure and does not
        establish a general entitlement to payment from
        insurers in respect of uninsured risks.

Application of the aforesaid principles to the present case

84. At the outset, we find that the risk of the deceased, who
        was travelling as an occupant of a private vehicle
        insured under an Act Only Policy, was not covered
        under the statutory insurance requirements. The
        Tribunal had also recorded that no additional premium

                                                                                        Page 37 of 43
        had been paid towards coverage of passengers. The High
        Court was therefore justified in holding that the
        Insurance Company was not liable to indemnify the
        owner of the vehicle.
85. As regards the first requirement, the liability to
        compensate the appellants stands judicially
        determined. The Tribunal held that the accident
        occurred due to the rash and negligent driving of
        respondent No. 2 and awarded compensation of
        ₹7,79,800/- (Rupees Seven Lakh Seventy-Nine
        Thousand Eight Hundred only), together with interest
        at the rate of 7.5 per cent per annum from the date of
        filing of the claim petition until realisation. Neither the
        finding of negligence nor the quantum of compensation
        was disturbed by the High Court. There thus exists an
        established and enforceable obligation upon the owner
        of the vehicle.
86. Turning to the second requirement, the prolonged
        pendency of the proceedings is of particular
        significance. The accident occurred on 15.04.2012 and
        the Tribunal passed its award on 28.08.2017. More
        than fourteen years have thus elapsed since the
        accident.
87. The High Court stayed execution of the award on
        18.01.2018, subject to deposit of the awarded amount
        with accrued interest. Subsequently, it exonerated the
        Insurance Company and directed the return of any

                                                                                        Page 38 of 43
        amount deposited. The appellants were consequently
        left to pursue recovery from the owner.
88. In Interlocutory Application No. 273720 of 2025 filed
        before this Court, the first appellant has stated that she
        is a widow without any independent source of income
        and is suffering from medical infirmities associated with
        her advanced age. The appellants have further asserted
        that they have not received compensation under the
        award. While the precise position regarding payment
        requires verification, these circumstances, considered
        alongside the prolonged litigation, disclose a substantial
        concern regarding the effective realisation of
        compensation.
89. Although the appellants retain the remedy of executing
        the award against the owner, that remedy must be
        considered in light of the prolonged litigation and the
        hardship which further delay may occasion.
        Exceptional intervention is therefore warranted to
        ensure that compensation does not remain merely an
        enforceable right without meaningful relief.
90. As regards the third requirement, directing the
        Insurance Company to first satisfy only the outstanding
        compensation and accrued interest, after adjusting
        amounts already received, would facilitate payment of
        an adjudicated liability without altering the statutory
        allocation of responsibility.

                                                                                        Page 39 of 43
91. Such a direction would impose a financial burden upon
        the Insurance Company despite the absence of
        coverage. Nevertheless, having regard to the prolonged
        proceedings, the established liability of the owner and
        the circumstances of the appellants, we consider
        exceptional relief proportionate, provided that the right
        of recovery is effectively protected.

92. The fourth requirement concerning procedural fairness
        and recovery safeguards must also be satisfied. The
        Insurance Company shall retain its right to recover the
        amount paid from respondent No. 2, who remains
        legally responsible for compensation. The mechanism
        for such recovery, including recourse to execution
        proceedings before the Tribunal without instituting a
        separate suit, shall be specified in the operative
        directions.

93. On a cumulative consideration of these circumstances,
        we are satisfied that the present case warrants
        exceptional relief under Article 142 of the Constitution,
        subject to verification of any amounts already paid or
        disbursed and appropriate recovery safeguards. Such
        relief does not impose statutory or contractual liability
        upon the Insurance Company or establish a general
        entitlement in cases involving uninsured risks.

Conclusion and operative directions

94. In view of the foregoing discussion, we answer the first
        question by holding that compulsory insurance under
        Section 147 of the MV Act does not automatically extend
        to gratuitous occupants of private vehicles or pillion
        riders under an Act Only Policy. As regards the second
        question, we hold that the absence of statutory or
        contractual liability does not constitute an absolute bar
        to the exercise of the extraordinary jurisdiction of this
        Court under Article 142 of the Constitution.

95. However, the exercise of such jurisdiction must remain
        exceptional and must ordinarily satisfy the following
        four requirements:

  I. Established liability. There must be an established and
        enforceable obligation to compensate, with the liability
        of the person responsible having been judicially
        determined.

 II. Exceptional necessity. The circumstances must
        disclose a compelling necessity to prevent substantial
        injustice which cannot adequately be remedied through
        the ordinary process of enforcement.

III. Necessity and proportionality. The proposed direction
        must be necessary and proportionate to the injustice
        sought to be remedied, having due regard to the rights
        and interests of the insurer.

IV. Procedural fairness and recovery safeguards. The
        affected parties must be afforded an opportunity of being
        heard, and appropriate safeguards must be provided to
        secure recovery from the person ultimately liable.

                                                                                        Page 41 of 43
96. Satisfaction of these requirements does not confer an

        automatic entitlement to relief under Article 142. The

        power must remain exceptional and cannot become a

        substitute for the statutory framework governing

        insurance liability.

97. The established liability of the owner, the prolonged

        proceedings and the hardship pleaded by the first

        appellant justify exceptional intervention, subject to

        verification of the outstanding amount and preservation

        of the Insurance Company’s recovery rights.

98. Accordingly, we affirm the finding of the High Court that

        the Insurance Company bears no statutory or

        contractual liability. However, in exercise of our

        jurisdiction under Article 142 of the Constitution, we

        modify the impugned judgment dated 05.08.2019 and

        issue the following directions:

 (i) The Insurance Company shall satisfy the outstanding
         compensation awarded by the Tribunal on 28.08.2017,
         together with interest at the rate of 7.5 per cent per
         annum from the date of filing of the claim petition until
         realisation, after adjusting any amount already
         received by the appellants.

 (ii) The Tribunal shall ascertain the outstanding amount
         within four weeks from receipt of this judgment. The
         Insurance Company shall deposit the amount so
         determined within eight weeks thereafter for
         disbursement to the appellants in accordance with the
         award.

 (iii) The Insurance Company shall be entitled to recover the
         amount paid from respondent No. 2, being the owner of
         the offending vehicle, through execution proceedings

         before the Tribunal without instituting a separate suit.
         The Tribunal shall afford the owner an opportunity of
