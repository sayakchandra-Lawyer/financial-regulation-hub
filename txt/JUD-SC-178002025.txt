      THE IMPUGNED JUDGMENTS

12. The High Court decided the writ petitions by two separate
      judgments delivered on the same date, that is, 07.03.2025. The
      High Court held as follows, (i) the Charity Commissioner failed to
      properly scrutinize the valuation report particularly the reduction
      in the value of the lands to the extent of 80% in the Ready
      Reckoner value in the case of the Vadavali land and to the extent
      of 50% in the case of the Owale land, (ii) the reduction of the area
      adopted as saleable, i.e., 94,860 sq. mtrs. as against 1,78,140 sq.
      mtrs. in the case of the Vadavali land, was not examined, (iii) the
      unexplained rationale behind assigning negative weightages of
      60% and 20% in the case of the Vadavali land, and of 30% and

                                                                 7
      20% in the case of the Owale land, in the valuation report should
      have been rigorously examined, and the basis of valuation of the
      TDR was unclear, (iv) the alleged topographical difficulties,
      encroachments and pending litigation in respect of subject lands
      were not subjected to a probing and evidence based enquiry, (v)
      the orders granting sanction failed to establish bona fide and
      compelling necessity justifying alienation of lands belonging to
      Trust, and (vi) the process did not demonstrate transparent and
      objective determination of fair market value. Accordingly, by both
      the impugned judgments, the orders of sanction were quashed
      and in W.P. No. 1915 of 2025 as a ‘necessary corollary’, the
      conveyance deed dated 06.09.2024 in respect of the Vadavali land
      was declared void ab initio and annulled. The proceedings under
      Section 36(1)(a) of the Act, namely, Application Nos. 51 and 50 of
      2024, were remitted to the Charity Commissioner for a de novo
      consideration, with a direction to decide them within six months.
      Hence, these appeals.

           15
20. This Court in several decisions has dealt with the scope and ambit
      of Section 36 of the Act. It has been held that while exercising
      power under Section 36(1)(a) of the Act, the Charity Commissioner
      has to consider three classic requirements, namely, the interest,
      benefit and protection of the trust. The Charity Commissioner has
      to be objectively satisfied that there is necessity to dispose of the
      property in the interest of the public trust. It is also open to the
      Charity Commissioner to revoke the sanction in exercise of power
      under Section 36(2) of the Act on the ground that sanction has
      been obtained by plain fraud or misrepresentation or material
      facts have been suppressed while obtaining sanction. The power
      of revocation has been conferred on the Charity Commissioner
      with an object to subserve the interest, benefit and protection of
      the trust and its property. However, the power of revocation is
      circumscribed, in case a conveyance deed is executed. In such a
      case, the sanction can only be revoked on the ground of fraud.
      The Trustees hold the property for the benefit of the beneficiaries
      and in case the Charity Commissioner accepts the necessity of
      alienating the trust property, the beneficiaries cannot insist that
      property should be sold to a person of their choice6.

whose tender was the highest and compliant, deposited earnest

undertaking of the purchaser to remove the encroachers and to
bear the cost of litigation, the absence of any relationship between
the trustees and the purchaser, the development plan remarks
and the applicability of the coastal zone regulations, and relied on
two decisions of the Bombay High Court which permit sale of
encroached trust property to a purchaser who takes over the
responsibility for the encroachments.

      SCOPE AND AMBIT OF THE POWERS UNDER ARTICLE 227
      OF THE CONSTITUTION OF INDIA
29. The scope and ambit of exercise of power under Article 227 of the
      Constitution of India has been examined and explained in a
      number of decisions of this Court. A Constitution Bench of this
      Court7 has held that power of superintendence conferred by
      Article 227 is to be exercised most sparingly and only in
      appropriate cases in order to keep the subordinate courts within
      the bounds of their authority and not for correcting mere errors.

  7 Waryam Singh & Anr. v. Amarnath & Anr., (1954) 1 SCC 51

                                                                22
    A two-Judge Bench of this Court8 has held that the exercise of
    power under Article 227 is restricted to cases of serious
    dereliction of duty and flagrant violation of principles of law of
    justice or where if the High Court does not interfere, a grave
    injustice remains uncorrected. It is trite that while acting under
    Article 227, the High Court does not exercise its power as an
    appellate court or substitute its own judgment in place of that of
    the subordinate court to correct an error which is not apparent
    on the face of record. The High Court can set aside the findings of
    an inferior court or tribunal if they are based on no evidence or
    the finding is so perverse that no reasonable person can possibly
    come to such a conclusion which the Court or Tribunal has
    arrived at. Another two-Judge Bench of this Court9 again
    considered the scope and ambit of Article 227 and summarised
    the principles on exercise of jurisdiction under Article 227.
    Similar view has been reiterated by another two-Judge Bench of
    this Court10 and it has been held that reasoned decision taken on
    an elaborate consideration of relevant facts cannot be interfered
    with in exercise of supervisory jurisdiction under Article 227 of
    the Constitution. The principles with regard to scope of exercise

8 Estralla Rubber v. Dass Estate (P) Ltd., (2001) 8 SCC 97
9 Shalini Shyam Shetty v. Rajendra Shankar Patil, (2010) 8 SCC 329
10 Garment Craft (supra)

                                                              23
      of power under Article 227 of the Constitution again have been
      recently summarised by a two-Judge Bench of this Court.11
30. In the backdrop of aforesaid well-settled scope of exercise of power
      under Article 227, we may advert to the impugned judgments. The
      High Court has failed to appreciate that a reasoned decision was
      taken on an elaborate consideration of relevant facts by the
      Charity Commissioner which did not call for any interference in
      exercise of supervisory jurisdiction under Article 227 of the
      Constitution. The High Court has virtually acted like an Appellate
      Court while examining the validity of the decision of the Charity
      Commissioner.
31. The High Court has clearly exceeded its powers under Article 227
      and the judgments passed by it suffer from the following
      infirmities: (i) it did not refer to the process adopted by the Trust
      to sell the property, namely, a well-considered decision by its
      trustees to alienate the properties, issuance of public notices, the
      multiple bids received and the valuation report obtained by the
      Trust to ascertain the market value of the properties, while
      recording a finding that the sale ought to have been conducted
      through a transparent and competitive process. The finding that
      a transparent process by public notice was not followed is

  11 Nandi Infrastructure Corridor Enterprises Ltd. (supra)

                                                                24
      contrary to the record. (ii) The High Court failed to take into
      consideration that the negative weightages applied by the valuers
      were on account of the reservation, the coastal regulation zone,
      the green zone and the forest zone, which are matters within the
      domain of experts. (iii) Insofar as the Owale land is concerned, the
      High Court proceeded on the footing that negative weightages of
      30% for green zone and 20% for litigation were applied, whereas
      the valuation report applies a negative weightage of 50% to the
      plots in green zone and of 75% to the plots in forest zone. The
      finding, therefore, does not even conform to the record. (iv) The
      High Court itself accepted that the topography of the land, the
      encroachments and the expenses of litigation might constitute
      valid considerations for sale, yet faulted the Charity
      Commissioner for not holding a probing inquiry. (v) The finding
      that the basis of valuation of TDR is unclear, is factually incorrect,
      as both the valuers have specifically valued the TDR potential of
      the land. (vi) The High Court ought to have appreciated that
      Section 36 does not contemplate a trial and the Charity
      Commissioner is required to arrive at his satisfaction on the
      material placed before him.
32. The High Court ought to have appreciated that it is neither
      exercising original jurisdiction nor an appellate jurisdiction but

                                                                25
      jurisdiction of superintendence. The High Court erred in not
      appreciating that the orders of the Charity Commissioner were
      passed by taking into account the interest of the Trust and the
      Charity Commissioner had objectively satisfied himself that there
      is a necessity to dispose of the property in the interest of the Trust.
      Thus, the High Court, in our opinion has clearly exceeded the
      scope of its jurisdiction under Article 227 of the Constitution.
33. The High Court ought to have also appreciated that the registered
      conveyance deed with respect to Vadavali land was acted upon
      and the purchaser had already transferred a part of the land to
      the Corporation which was not impleaded as a respondent in the
      writ petitions. The High Court failed to appreciate that under the
      proviso to Section 36(2) of the Act, after the execution of the
      conveyance deed, a sanction cannot be revoked except on the
      ground of fraud practised on the Charity Commissioner. The
      respondent no. 1 had not taken any such plea and therefore, the
      High Court ought not to have set at naught the conveyance deed.
      The High Court ought to have appreciated that the respondent no.
      1 had a remedy under Section 36(2) to approach the Charity
      Commissioner for seeking revocation of sanction, which he did
      not avail. On this ground alone, the High Court ought not to have
      entertained the writ petitions.
