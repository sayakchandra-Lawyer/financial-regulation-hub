NARESH SALECHA, MEMBER (TECHNICAL)
1. The present appeal has been filed by the Appellant i.e., Gaurav Katiyar,
who is the Insolvency Professional registered with the Insolvency & Bankruptcy
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

Board of India (‘IBBI’) with registration no. IBBIIIPA-001/IPP00209/2017-
18/10409. The instant appeal has been filed by the Appellant under Section 220
(7) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (‘Code’) against the Order
dated 20.08.2026 ("Impugned Order") passed by the Disciplinary Committee
of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India Bearing No.
IBBI/DC/340/2026.

       Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (“IBBI”) is the Respondent
herein.
2. The Appellant submitted that the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process
(“CIRP”) of M/s. Revital Reality Private Limited (“Corporate Debtor”)
commenced pursuant to the order dated 04.06.2024 passed by the National
Company Law Tribunal (“Adjudicating Authority”), New Delhi Bench,
whereupon he was appointed as the Interim Resolution Professional (IRP) and
subsequently confirmed as the Resolution Professional (RP).
3. The Appellant submitted that, upon receiving a complaint concerning his
conduct during the CIRP, the Respondent sought his explanation by email dated
02.04.2026, which he furnished through emails dated 04.04.2026, 15.04.2026 and
20.04.2026. Thereafter, the Respondent issued a show-cause notice dated
18.05.2026 under Section 219 of the Code, to which the Appellant submitted a
detailed reply on 01.06.2026. However, the Disciplinary Committee of the IBBI,
by the impugned order dated 20.08.2026, suspended his registration as an
Insolvency Professional for two years with effect from 21.08.2026, restrained him

                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

from continuing in his existing assignments and directed the respective
Committees of Creditors (CoCs) to replace him with another IRP/RP.
4. The Appellant submitted that all three Prospective Resolution Applicants
(PRAs) had furnished their responses to the queries raised during the CoC
meeting held on 23.03.2026. Upon examining the responses, he found those
submitted by two PRAs to be complete, whereas the response of the third PRA
was vague and incomplete for the purposes of vetting and proofreading the
proposed amendments to its resolution plan. Accordingly, by email dated
28.03.2026, the Appellant requested the third PRA to rectify the deficiencies,
make the necessary revisions and submit the requisite documents by 30.03.2026.
He contended that the communication was intended to facilitate proper
examination of the proposed amendments, minimise clerical errors and enable the
CoC to consider the resolution plans in a time-bound manner, without permitting
changes to their commercial aspects.
5. The Appellant submitted that the email dated 28.03.2026 specifically
identified the deficiencies requiring rectification, including the absence of an
indicative timeline for handing over units to homebuyers, a profit and loss
statement and quarter-wise cash-flow projections. He further pointed out that
several queries had been answered merely with the word “Ok”, while certain
queries remained unanswered. The third PRA was also reminded of the applicable
legal requirements concerning the declaration under Section 32A of the code and
informed that, according to the Information Memorandum, the assets and

                                                 Page 3 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

liabilities were required to be stated as on the insolvency commencement date
and not as on 31.03.2023. The Appellant contended that these deficiencies
necessitated clarification and that the email expressly prohibited amendments to
the commercial aspects of the resolution plan.
6. The Appellant contended that the email dated 28.03.2026 was marked to
all CoC members and that the matter was placed before the CoC at its meeting
held on 01.04.2026, without any member raising an objection. He submitted that
the communication was transparent and undertaken in the discharge of his
professional responsibilities, without concealment, undisclosed negotiations or
any mala fide intention to favour the third PRA. He further contended that the
three-day period afforded for rectification was procedural in nature and did not
confer any undue commercial advantage upon the concerned PRA.
7. The Appellant submitted that the subsequent conduct of the CoC supported
his contention that the extension was neither objectionable nor prejudicial to the
CIRP. In its 23rd meeting held on 01.04.2026, the CoC granted further time to all
three PRAs to furnish their responses to the queries and submit amended
resolution plans by 07.04.2026. Although the minutes recorded the requirement
to submit the responses and amended plans, the date of 07.04.2026 was
inadvertently omitted. The Appellant stated that he had preserved the electronic
recording of the meeting in accordance with Regulation 23(3)(d) of the IBBI
(CIRP) Regulations, 2016. He further relied upon paragraphs 2.1.18 and 2.1.19
of the impugned order, wherein the Respondent itself acknowledged that the CoC

                                                 Page 4 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

had subsequently granted time to all three PRAs to submit their amended
resolution plans.
8. The Appellant further submitted that, at its 26th meeting held on
27.05.2026, the CoC granted the third PRA an additional three days to rectify the
discrepancies identified by him. He contended that the CoC did not reject the
plans on the ground of delayed submission but instead examined and discussed
them before proceeding to vote on a resolution plan with the requisite majority.
According to the Appellant, the CoC’s decision to consider the plans rather than
reject them as belated demonstrated its acceptance of the extended timeline and
its assessment that the plans warranted consideration on their merits.
9. The Appellant contended that, even assuming that the initial extension
required prior approval of the CoC, its subsequent conduct amounted to
ratification of the extension. He submitted that the CoC had the opportunity to
reject plans received beyond the stipulated timeline but instead accepted,
examined and voted upon the plans submitted during the extended period. He
argued that the Disciplinary Committee failed to appreciate the legal effect of this
subsequent approval. In support, the Appellant relied upon the judgment of the
Hon’ble Supreme Court in Delhi Technological University v. B.S. Rawat, 2026
INSC 797, wherein ratification was explained as the confirmation or adoption, by
a person or body possessing the requisite authority, of an act previously
undertaken without authority or irregularly, thereby treating it as valid from its
inception.

10. The Appellant submitted that the Disciplinary Committee had erroneously
applied Regulation 36B (6) of the IBBI (CIRP) Regulations, 2016, without
distinguishing it from Regulation 36B (2). He pointed out that Regulation 36B
(6) contemplates extension of the timeline for submission of resolution plans with
the approval of the CoC, whereas Regulation 36B (2) requires the Request for
Resolution Plans (RFRP) to specify the steps in the process, the manner and
purpose of interaction between the RP and the PRAs, and the corresponding
timelines. The Appellant relied upon Clause 2.1.5 of the approved RFRP, which
expressly permitted the CoC to negotiate with PRAs having compliant resolution
plans, require revised proposals based on such discussions and determine or
communicate the timelines for submission of revised proposals at a later stage, if
necessary.
11. The Appellant contended that the relevant stage of the CIRP was governed
by Regulation 36B (2), read with the approved RFRP, and not Regulation 36B
(6). He submitted that the CoC had extended the timeline for receipt of resolution
plans until 06.02.2026, that all three plans were received on that date and that the
plans were placed before the CoC on 13.02.2026. Thereafter, at its 22nd meeting
held on 06.03.2026, the CoC directed all PRAs to respond to each query and
submit proposed changes to their resolution plans. Accordingly, when the email
dated 28.03.2026 was issued, the process had moved beyond the initial
submission of resolution plans and had entered the stage of post-submission
clarification, vetting, revision and negotiations. The Appellant therefore

                                                 Page 6 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

contended that the three-day period related to the rectification and resubmission
of revised proposals contemplated by the approved RFRP and could not be treated
as an impermissible extension of the original timeline for submission of
resolution plans.
12. The Appellant further submitted that Regulation 39(1B) of the CIRP
Regulations prohibits the CoC from considering a resolution plan received after
the timeline specified by it under Regulation 36B. He contended that the said
provision could not, in the circumstances of the present case, be construed as an
independent violation by the RP merely because he facilitated the submission or
consideration of revised proposals pursuant to the CoC’s decisions. He
accordingly maintained that the finding of contravention was vitiated by the
failure to appreciate the distinction between the initial submission of resolution
plans and subsequent revisions undertaken during the negotiation stage.
13. The Appellant also contended that the allegation of selectively extending
time to the third PRA, as raised in paragraph 41(iv) of the show cause notice, had
not culminated in any adverse finding by the Disciplinary Committee. He
submitted that the email dated 28.03.2026 was circulated to all CoC members,
the matter was placed before the CoC, and the CoC subsequently extended the
timeline for all three PRAs at its meeting dated 01.04.2026. He therefore
contended that no violation of Regulation 7(2)(a) or Regulation 7(2)(h) of the
IBBI (Insolvency Professionals) Regulations, read with Clause 3 of the Code of
Conduct, had been established.

14. The Appellant submitted that the disciplinary jurisdiction under Sections
218 and 220 of the code could be exercised only in accordance with the statutory
framework and upon establishing a sustainable contravention of the Code, the
rules or regulations made thereunder, or directions issued by the Board. He
contended that, in the absence of a properly established violation of the applicable
provisions, the finding concerning the extension of time and the consequential
suspension could not be sustained.
15. The Appellant submitted that the eligibility criteria for PRAs were initially
approved by the CoC in its 3rd meeting held on 20.08.2024. Under the original
criteria, a PRA was required, inter alia, to possess a net worth of at least Rs. 50
crores as on 31.03.2024, based on the applicable financial statements, with a
provision permitting submission of unaudited financial statements for FY 2023–
24 where the audit had not been completed, subject to the prescribed undertaking
and deadline.
16. The Appellant submitted that an appeal filed by an ex-Director/Promoter
had resulted in a stay on publication of Form G, which was ultimately vacated
when the appeal was dismissed by this Appellate Tribunal in August 2025. He
contended that, by then, the verification of expressions of interest was expected
to take place during August, September or October 2025, rendering the earlier
reference to financial statements as on 31.03.2024 outdated for the contemplated
verification process. He further submitted that the revised criteria were proposed
to ensure clarity and reduce the possibility of competing interpretations and

                                                 Page 8 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

subsequent litigation, and that these reasons were recorded in the relevant CoC
minutes.
17. The Appellant contended that, in view of the changed circumstances, he
placed Agenda Item No. 9 before the CoC at its 13th meeting, held on 04.09.2025,
proposing a fresh set of eligibility criteria. He relied upon the notice and agenda
for the meeting, the minutes thereof and the comparative statement of the earlier
and revised criteria to submit that the proposal was not confined to deleting the
five-year delivery requirement but constituted a comprehensive revision of the
eligibility framework, which was considered and approved by the CoC.
18. The Appellant submitted that the original criteria required a PRA to have
delivered at least five lakh square feet of super area in group-housing real estate
projects during FY 2019–20 to FY 2023–24, with delivery evidenced by an
Occupancy Certificate or Completion Certificate issued by the competent
authority. The earlier criteria also prescribed an average annual turnover of at
least Rs. 50 crores from group-housing real estate project business during the
preceding five financial years, based on the latest audited financial statements,
subject to the provision concerning unaudited financial statements for FY 2023–
24.
19. The Appellant further submitted that the revised criteria altered the basis
for assessing project experience by requiring a PRA, as a promoter, to have
successfully completed real estate projects aggregating at least five lakh square
feet and obtained the requisite Occupancy Certificate or Completion Certificate.

The revised criteria excluded areas claimed on the basis of deemed completion
and adopted the meanings of “real estate project” and “promoter” under Section
2 of the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016. The Appellant
contended that the change replaced the earlier requirement of delivery within a
specified five-year period with a requirement based on successful completion of
qualifying projects, subject to the prescribed certification conditions.
20. The Appellant submitted that the revised criteria deleted the earlier
requirement of an average annual turnover of Rs. 50 crores from group-housing
real estate project business. The minimum net-worth requirement of Rs. 50 crores
were retained but was linked to the latest audited financial statements for FY
2023–24 or FY 2024–25, instead of the earlier criteria referring to net worth as
on 31.03.2023 and 31.03.2024. The revised criteria also retained the eligibility
route for homebuyer cooperatives, societies or associations satisfying the
stipulated requirement of at least 300 members and admitted claims aggregating
to not less than 33% of the claims collated for homebuyers at the time of issuance
of Form G. Such entities were exempted from the ordinary net-worth and
experience qualifications. The requirement of a security deposit of Rs. 10 lakhs,
payable by demand draft in the name of the Corporate Debtor or by transfer into
its bank account, was also retained.
21. The Appellant contended that the Disciplinary Committee erred in treating
the revision as the deletion of an isolated eligibility condition without
contemporaneous justification. He submitted that the comparative criteria and the

                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

records of the 13th CoC meeting demonstrated that an entirely revised eligibility
framework had been proposed and approved. According to the Appellant, the
proposal had to be assessed as a whole, having regard to the recorded reasons for
revising the criteria, rather than by examining the deletion of the five-year
delivery condition in isolation.
22. The Appellant further submitted that, under Section 25(2)(h) of the code,
the eligibility criteria for PRAs require the approval of the CoC. He contended
that placing a proposal before the CoC is a procedural and administrative function
of the Resolution Professional, enabling the competent decision-making body to
consider and determine the matter, and does not by itself constitute a substantive
decision within the meaning of Clause 16 of the Code of Conduct for Insolvency
Professionals. The Appellant maintained that he had merely placed the proposed
criteria before the CoC for its consideration and approval and that the ultimate
decision rested with the CoC.
23. The Appellant submitted that the Disciplinary Committee had
acknowledged, in paragraph 2.2.16 of the impugned order, the rationale
underlying the proposed substitution of the eligibility criteria. He contended that
the proposal was supported by recorded reasons and that no mala fide intention
had been established. He further submitted that the impugned order failed to
identify any prejudice caused to the Corporate Debtor or its stakeholders by the
revision of the eligibility criteria and that the act of placing Agenda Item No. 9
before the CoC could not, in itself, justify disciplinary action.

24. The Appellant challenged paragraph 3.4 of the impugned order, whereby
the Disciplinary Committee directed him not to continue in his existing
assignments and required the respective CoCs to replace him with another
Insolvency Professional. He submitted that Regulation 13(7) of the IBBI
(Inspection and Investigation) Regulations, 2017 provides for communication of
the relevant order to the CoCs of the insolvency resolution processes in which the
concerned professional is acting as IRP/RP. According to the Appellant, the
power to communicate the disciplinary order must be distinguished from the
power to direct the CoCs to replace the professional in their respective
proceedings.
25. The Appellant contended that the Disciplinary Committee, constituted
under Section 220(1) of the code, exercises only such powers as are conferred
upon it by the statutory framework, including Section 220(2). He submitted that
the CoCs of other corporate debtors are not service providers within the meaning
of Section 3(31A) of the code and that the Disciplinary Committee had no
jurisdiction to issue binding directions to them requiring replacement of the
Appellant in their respective assignments. He further contended that the
mandatory replacement direction impermissibly pre-empted the independent
consideration of the respective CoCs.
26. In support of his submission, the Appellant relied upon the interim order
passed by this Appellate Tribunal in Sachin Navin Sinha v. IBBI, Company
Appeal (AT) (Ins.) No. 1165 of 2026. In that matter, the Tribunal suspended the

contended that the same approach ought to be adopted in the present case and that

40. The Respondent submitted that the disciplinary proceedings arose from the
Appellant’s conduct as the Resolution Professional in the Corporate Insolvency
Resolution Process of M/s Revital Reality Private Limited. Upon examination of
the material on record, the Disciplinary Committee found two contraventions,
namely, (i) extension of the timeline for submission and revision of a resolution
plan beyond the timeline approved by the CoC without obtaining its prior
approval; and (ii) modification of the eligibility criteria applicable to PRAs by
deleting the requirement relating to delivery or completion of a real estate project
during the preceding five financial years, without maintaining an adequate record
of the basis and rationale for such material modification.
41. The Respondent contended that the findings of the Disciplinary Committee
were based on the relevant material and the applicable statutory and regulatory

78. We first deal with the width of our review. We have noted the pleading of
the Respondent/IBBI that the Appellant only seeks a second look at the same
material and that our role is limited to finding any perversity, breach of natural
justice or a wrong reading of the law. It relies on Union of India v. Parma Nanda,
(1989) 2 SCC 177; B.C. Chaturvedi v. Union of India, (1995) 6 SCC 749;
Lucknow Kshetriya Gramin Bank v. Rajendra Singh, (2013) 12 SCC 372; State
of Karnataka v. N. Gangaraj, (2020) 3 SCC 423; and State of Karnataka v.
Umesh, (2022) 6 SCC 563. We accept the main teaching of these cases.

       While generally speaking, it seems appropriate that a reviewing forum
should not re-weigh evidence only because another view is possible. The
Appellate Court may seek to understand whether the process was fair, whether
the finding rests on evidence, whether the rules were correctly applied, whether
the finding is perverse, and whether the penalty is out of proportion to the proved
misconduct. We have seen that Umesh (supra) lists these very heads, and
proportionality of penalty is one of them. Misconduct of this kind is judged on a
preponderance of probabilities and not on proof beyond reasonable doubt. We

                                                 Page 34 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

further note that the case of B.C. Chaturvedi (supra) also recognises that where
a punishment shocks the conscience, the authority may be asked to reconsider it
and, in sporadic cases, depending upon facts and circumstances of each case, the
court / Appellate Tribunal however may substitute a lesser penalty with reasons.
Thus, we are of the considered view that the Board’s authorities do not shut out
review of the Appellate Tribunal including on the measures.
79. We take into account and are conscious of the fact that the DC is the expert
body of the regulator, and its view of what professional duty requires in a live
insolvency process deserves respect but it does not mean that the Appellate
tribunal should accept what the Board has recorded in the Impugned order. We
hold that the Appellate forum needs to intervene where an order treats an
uncharged matter as a ground, or gives no reason for the sanction. Regulation
13(1) of the IBBI (Inspection and Investigation) Regulations, 2017 requires a
reasoned order. That applies to the finding and also to the measure as applicable
to the Board. We find that in the present case the reasons given for the measure
are short, and a fuller statement would have served the Board better, as we say at
the end of these findings. We are conscious that a short order need not to be
considered as an unreasoned one, when the reasons for the measure can be read
from the findings on which it rests. We therefore proceed to examine whether the
findings and the record support the measure, and we do not intend to set the
Impugned Order aside for brevity alone.

                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

80. Before turning to the two charges against the Appellant, we state the

standard against which the conduct of a resolution professional has to be

measured. In Manish Motilal Jaju v. Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of

India, Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1363 of 2026, this Appellate Tribunal

described resolution under the Code as the business of trust (para 21 of the order)

            “21. Highlighting some of the guidelines of the Code of
            Conduct, it is submitted that the nature of the powers and
            duties which have been entrusted to the Resolution
            Professional by the Code is such that any lapse committed
            knowingly or unknowingly by him may bring disrepute to the
            whole institution of the insolvency professionals. It is a
            business of trust and once the trust is gone it would be very
            difficult for the stakeholders of an insolvency process to
            believe in the functioning of the resolution of the CD within
            the framework of the IBC. Hence, whatever decision would
            be taken with regard to the instant appeal would convey a
            message to the whole of the body of insolvency professionals.
            Therefore, it should not reflect that any misconduct
            committed by a Resolution Professional would be ignored by
            the guardians of law and therefore, having regard to the
            nature of misconduct committed by the appellant, he is not
            entitled to any interim relief.”

                                                            (Emphasis Supplied)

81. We adopt that description. It needs to be spelled out, because the number

of matters now coming before the Disciplinary Committee and before us shows

that it is not always understood. We recognise that the resolution professional

holds the corporate debtor and its estate in his custody from the first day. The

Resolution Professional controls its accounts and records, collates and verifies

                                                 Page 36 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

claims, frames the criteria for applicants, runs the invitation and evaluation of
plans, convenes the CoC and keeps its minutes, reports to the Adjudicating
Authority, and places the plan before the creditors. It may be stated that the RP is
the one person who sees the whole picture. Everybody else sees only what he
reports and records, the creditors decide on his information, the Adjudicating
Authority approves his application, and the Board learns of a lapse only if it is on
paper. The Honourable Supreme Court has repeatedly stipulated that commercial
wisdom belongs to the CoC [K. Sashidhar v. Indian Overseas Bank, (2019) 12
SCC 150; Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel India Ltd. v. Satish Kumar
Gupta, (2020) 8 SCC 531]. However, it needs to be appreciated that wisdom is
only as good as the information and the process that reaches it.
82. The other side of the rule is that the resolution professional must not take
for himself a decision that the Code or the regulations have given to the CoC, and
must keep a record that lets the CoC and every later reader see why he acted as
he did.
83. We would like to further state that the weight of this trust grows with the
scale of the process. In real estate cases the persons who rely on the professional
like the Appellant, are homebuyers, in thousands, who put their life long savings
into a home and now sit as financial creditors. The homebuyers act through an
authorised representative and cannot watch each step. The homebuyers depend
on the resolution professional to run an even process and to record what he does.
The processes now before this Appellate Tribunal involve thousands of crores of

                                                 Page 37 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

rupees and thousands of such families. In view of these facts, we find that
misconduct in such a process, whether a small departure that is not recorded or a
large one that is concealed, is not a private matter between a professional and his
regulator. It falls on those who can least bear it, and it weakens the faith of all
users in the whole insolvency system.
84. We find that in this manner, the Code and the regulations leave little room
for improvisation by the resolution professional. The creditors take the
commercial decisions, the Adjudicating Authority takes the legal ones, and the
professional runs the process and keeps the record. When the resolution
professional thinks a departure would help, such as an extra day, a relaxed
condition or a different course, the proper path for the resolution professional is
just to follow the correct path as laid down in the code and the regulations. The
Resolution professional must seek the permission of the CoC, or the Adjudicating
Authority if the matter is beyond the CoC, for decisions not contemplated in his
day-to-day and ordinary jurisdiction. We are of the opinion that a deviation made
quietly, however well meant, takes the decision away from those who own it,
makes the process uneven among applicants, and leaves everyone else to find and
repair the problem later, when it is costly to undo. A good purpose is not an
answer. The law asks for authority first and a record always.
85. This Appellate Tribunal has said the same in recent matters concerning
professionals. In Manish Motilal Jaju (Supra), order on the interim application
dated 18.08.2026, arising from the order of the DC dated 08.07.2026, this

                                                 Page 38 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

Appellate Tribunal declined to protect a professional who was found to have
stepped outside the role the Code gives him. The facts there were more serious,
but the principle is the same. In the main proceedings in that case, this Appellate
Tribunal had observed that a resolution professional collates claims and does not
sit as an adjudicator, and that if a professional could overrule what the CoC or the
Adjudicating Authority has settled, the whole process could be undone.
86. We would like to recall the case of Anshul Gupta v. Insolvency and
Bankruptcy Board of India, Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1139 of 2026 & IA 4426
of 2026, order dated 07.09.2026, where this Appellate Tribunal refused interim
relief against a two-year suspension, it was held that registration is not
assignment-specific, that it is the gateway to acting as a professional in any
process under the Code, and that a suspension of registration under Section 220(2)
disentitles the professional from acting as interim resolution professional or
resolution professional in any other process. The Hon’ble Supreme Court issued
notice on 21.09.2026 (Diary No. 57176/2026), but did not stay the order, and
asked this Appellate Tribunal to decide the appeal in the meantime without being
prejudiced by its observations. Similarly, in Kannan Tiruvengadam v.
Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India, Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No.
1814/2026, order dated 14.10.2026, this Appellate Tribunal refused to stay a two-
year suspension and said that once a professional is found tainted there is no place
for him in other processes, because the faith of stakeholders in the mechanism of
the Code has to be kept.

87. We also take note of our own decision in the companion appeal arising
from the same Revital Reality process, Deepak Kumar Goyal v. Insolvency and
Bankruptcy Board of India, Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1736 of 2026. The
authorised representative there was held to have concurred in extensions of the

That decision shows that the process in this very CIRP was run with a series of
timeline extensions and thin records, and that this Appellate Tribunal looks at the
contravention and at the measure separately.
88. We acknowledge that neither charge in this appeal alleges directly that the
Appellant was involved in any financial wrongdoing for his own personal gain.
Both charges are to determine whether the Appellant stayed within the lines that
the regulations draw around his role. A resolution professional is trusted precisely
because he does so without being watched. A lapse of process by such a person
is therefore not a small matter, and the answer that nobody objected or that no
loss followed does not meet the charge.

                            Contravention No. 1

89. We turn to the first contravention. Regulation 36B (6) of the CIRP
Regulations stipulates that the resolution professional may, with the approval of
the CoC, extend the timeline for submission of resolution plans. Regulation
39(1B) stipulate that the CoC shall not consider a plan received after the time

                                                 Page 40 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

specified under Regulation 36B. Regulation 7(2) of the IP Regulations makes
registration subject to the conditions that the professional shall at all times abide
by the Code, the rules, the regulations and the guidelines (clause (a)) and shall
abide by the Code of Conduct (clause (h)). We understand that the timeline for
plans belongs to the CoC and the resolution professional runs the process, but he
can widen the time the CoC has set only with its approval. Regulation 39(1B)
shows why a plan that comes after the CoC's date cannot even be considered, so
a later date decides who can compete and on what terms.
90. The Appellant's main legal answer is that by 28.03.2026 the plans were
already with the CoC, so that what was going on was clarification, vetting and
negotiation, governed by Regulation 36B (2) and clause 2.1.5 of the approved
Request for Resolution Plans (RFRP), and not by Regulation 36B (6). We do not
accept this, for the following reasons:

   a) Regulation 36B (2) state what the RFRP must contain, namely each step of
       the process, the manner and purpose of interaction between the resolution
       professional and the PRA, and the timelines. It tells the professional what
       to put in the RFRP. It gives no power to extend a timeline once fixed. That
       power lies in Regulation 36B(6) alone and is given with a condition.

   b) On the Appellant's own description, clause 2.1.5 lets the CoC negotiate
       with PRAs whose plans are compliant, ask for revised proposals, and
       determine or communicate the timelines for those proposals. The clause
       speaks of the CoC fixing the dates. Nothing in it lets the resolution

                                                 Page 41 of 67
                                Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

   professional enlarge a date the CoC has already set. A clause that leaves
   dates to be fixed later does not take the CoC out of the picture. It keeps the
   CoC in.
c) The Appellant himself acted as if the CoC fixed these dates. At the 22nd
   meeting the CoC fixed 17.03.2026. The extension to 19.03.2026 came after
   consulting members. The extension to 23.03.2026 came on his own
   recommendation and with the concurrence of the AR holding 91%. We are
   of clear opinion that a professional like the Appellant, who asks for
   approval on 19.03.2026 cannot say on 28.03.2026 that approval was not
   needed.
d) The Consortium's submission on 23.03.2026 was a revised resolution plan.
   The e-mail of 28.03.2026 asked it to revise that plan again and to submit
   pending documents. That is a further submission of plan material after the
   date set by the CoC. Regulations 36B (6) and 39(1B) deal with exactly that,
   whatever name is given to the step.
e) The argument that Regulation 36B(6) speaks of submission of plans while
   the date at issue was for rectification is too fine. Once the CoC has fixed a
   last date for revised plans and a PRA is given more time to revise, the PRA
   is being given more time to submit. The plan on which the CoC will vote
   is not final until that later date, and the mischief the regulation addresses,
   namely uneven treatment of PRAs through shifting dates, applies fully.

                                            Page 42 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

   f) Calling the changes clerical does not help. Handover timelines for
       homebuyers, a profit and loss statement and cash flow projections go to the
       content and credibility of a plan. Whether a change is small enough to be
       allowed after the date is for the CoC to judge when asked. The resolution
       professional cannot decide that for himself and then go ahead.

91. The Appellant also argued that Regulation 39(1B) binds the CoC and not
the resolution professional. In its words that is right. We therefore do not treat
Regulation 39(1B) as a separate duty that the Appellant broke and read it only as
showing the purpose of Regulation 36B (6). The contravention is of Regulation
36B (6), and to that extent we examine the Impugned order.
92. It is an undisputed fact that the Approval was not taken. The Appellant's
case is that the e-mail was copied to all CoC members, that the matter was placed
before the CoC on 01.04.2026, and that no member objected. We consider that
was not approved by the CoC. It needs to be appreciated that approval needs a
proposal put to the CoC and a decision taken by it. Nothing was put to the CoC
before the extension was given. The CoC was told of a step already taken. The
AR with 91% of the voting share was not asked, though he had been asked on
19.03.2026. The silence of members who were only copied cannot be turned into
consent. We hold that the DC was right on this point.
93. It also appears that there was no urgency that prevented the Appellant from
asking. The CoC's date had passed five days earlier. On 17.03.2026 and
19.03.2026, the Appellant had obtained the members' view or the AR's

                                                 Page 43 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

concurrence within a day or two. The same short consultation, or a circulation to
the AR, was available on 28.03.2026 and was not used. Therefore, it is clear that
the Appellant knew the rule and had followed it twice in the same fortnight. That
makes the departure on 28.03.2026 more serious and not less. We accept that his
purpose was to help the CoC examine the plans in a time-bound way and that
copying the CoC counts in his favour on motive. It answers an allegation of
concealment, which was never made. It does not answer the charge that approval
was not taken. Purpose is a separate matter from authority.
94. We hold that prior approval is not a formality, and we say so for three
reasons that go beyond the words of the regulation. First, equal treatment. In a
competitive process the date by which each PRA must put its plan in final form
is part of the terms on which all of them compete. When the date is moved for
one PRA, the others are entitled to know and to ask for the same, and the way to
secure that is to take the decision to the body that represents all creditors. Here
the e-mail went to the Consortium alone, while in the Appellant's own case two
of the three PRAs had already given complete replies. Second, the record. A
decision of the CoC is minute and can be seen by all, while a decision taken by
the professional and reported later appears on the record only as an after-event.
Third, protection of the professional himself. One who seeks approval first is
protected by it. One who acts first has only the argument that the CoC would have
agreed, which can be made only after the event.

                                                 Page 44 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

95. We also don’t hesitate to record that none of this means that every
exchange between the resolution professional and a PRA needs a prior resolution
of the CoC. The Appellant may ask a PRA to clarify a point, to fix an obvious
clerical error, or to supply a document already promised, without moving any
date. The difference here is that the e-mail sets a fresh date, after the date fixed
by the CoC, for further revision of a plan and for pending documents. It is the
fixing of that date, and not the asking of questions, that needed the CoC.
96. We come to the finding on Clause 3 of the Code of Conduct. The show
cause notice raised a prima facie view that the extension appeared to have been
granted selectively to the Consortium. The DC did not record a finding of
selectivity, bias, conflict of interest or influence in its own findings, and the record
before us does not show that any other PRA asked for time and was refused. We
therefore make no finding of bias or favour.

       But Clause 3 asks for objectivity, and that is not confined to the absence of
a bad motive. A resolution professional who gives one PRA extra time after the
CoC's date, without telling the others and without the CoC's decision, falls short
of the evenness that Clause 3 expects, whatever his reason. To that limited extent,
and without any finding of bias, we hold that Regulation 7(2)(h) read with Clause
3 is also attracted. The contravention does not depend on Clause 3 in any case. A
breach of Regulation 36B (6) is by itself a breach of the condition in Regulation
7(2)(a) that the professional shall abide by the regulations at all times.

                                                 Page 45 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

97. We also note that the DC remarked that the Appellant should have checked
the AR's instructions before the extension to 23.03.2026 and called this a serious
lapse of due diligence. But the DC itself treated that extension as approved by the
CoC and the charge was confined to the extension beyond 23.03.2026. The
remark is therefore not a finding of contravention, and any weight given to it in
the measure of punishment is set aside.
98. The Appellant's other main answer to this charge is ratification. His case is
that the CoC gave all three PRAs further time on 01.04.2026, gave the
Consortium three more days on 27.05.2026, did not reject any plan as late, and
then voted on a plan by the required majority. The Appellant relies on Delhi
Technological University v. B.S. Rawat, 2026 INSC 797, where the Honourable
Supreme Court explained ratification as the confirmation or adoption, by a person
or body having the authority, of an act earlier done without authority or
irregularly. The DC said that later approval cannot retrospectively confer
authority. We find the argument of the Board as a general statement that is too
wide, since ratification is part of our law and Regulation 36B (6) does not forbid
it. We proceed on the assumption that a CoC could in principle ratify an extension
given without prior approval. But ratification needs knowledge of the material
facts and adoption of the act as the body's own, and that is not shown here.
99. We can accept that the CoC knew of the e-mail, because it was copied to
members and mentioned on 01.04.2026. Adoption is not shown. The DC found
that the minutes of 01.04.2026 show only that the CoC was informed, and that no

                                                 Page 46 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

approval was sought or obtained. The Appellant pleaded that an electronic
recording was kept under Regulation 23(3)(d) of the CIRP Regulations and can
be produced if directed, but he did not place it before the DC or with the appeal,
and it would add nothing even if it shows 07.04.2026 was announced. What the
CoC did on 01.04.2026 was to give time to all PRAs from that day, after the date
of 30.03.2026 had already passed. A fresh decision about the future is not the
approval of a past act done for one PRA, and the papers do not show that the CoC
was asked to do the second. The grant of three days on 27.05.2026 came after the
Board began its enquiry on 02.04.2026 and after the show cause notice. It is
another fresh decision about a later period and shows nothing about what the CoC
thought of the e-mail when it was sent. Voting on a plan shows that the CoC
valued the plan. It does not show that it adopted the extension granted by the
Appellant, and a CoC does not give up its power under Regulation 36B(6) by
considering the plans placed before it.
100. We are of the view that even if ratification were accepted, it would not
decide a disciplinary question. Regulation 36B(6) speaks of approval of the
committee, and the natural reading is approval at the time of the extension,
because the whole point is that the CoC controls the date. Ratification in the sense
settles whether an act binds the body that adopts it. A disciplinary proceeding
asks whether the professional did what the regulations required of him when he
acted. A professional who goes ahead without authority and is later excused by
his principal has still not obtained authority first. We need not decide whether

                                                 Page 47 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

ratification could cure a breach of Regulation 36B(6) for other purposes, such as
the validity of a plan. For a disciplinary purpose, the later conduct of the CoC
goes to how serious the lapse was and not to whether it happened. We take it into
account in that way below. Contravention 1 is proved.

                             Contravention no. 2

101. We turn to the second contravention. Under Section 25(2)(h) of the Code,
the criteria for PRAs are laid down by the resolution professional with the
approval of the CoC, as both sides accept and the DC recorded Clause 16 of the
Code of Conduct requires an insolvency professional to maintain written
contemporaneous records of any decision taken, the reasons for taking it, and the
information and evidence in support. The charge was that no written
contemporaneous record was kept of the reasons for omitting the requirement of
delivery or completion in the preceding five financial years. The Board is right
that the charge was never that the criteria were changed. The question is narrow:
was there a written reason, made at the time, for dropping that one condition?
102. The Appellant argued that placing an agenda item before the CoC is a
procedural and administrative function, that the real decision is the CoC's
approval under Section 25(2)(h), and that Clause 16 is therefore not engaged. We
do not agree. We are of the opinion that the resolution professional frames the
criteria and the CoC approves or rejects what is placed before it. What goes into
the proposal is his own choice. He chose to put up a text without the five-year
window. That is a decision, and Clause 16 asks for a written record of the reasons

                                                 Page 48 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

for it. The CoC's approval is a separate step and does not replace the professional's
own record. We are of the view that an agenda item that proposes to drop a
safeguard is a decision of the person who proposes it, even if it is the CoC that
gives effect to it. The CoC can decide only on what it is shown, and this is why
the law asks the professional to keep his reasons.
103. We have also considered the fact that the reasons recorded for Agenda Item
9 were two i.e. provisions on unaudited financial statements and similar changes
had to be brought in, and that the criteria should be made more straightforward
so as to avoid different readings and later objections. The DC accepted that the
first reason explains the change to the net worth condition. We agree, and we also
accept that the stay on publication of Form G, which ended only in August 2025,
made the references to 31.03.2024 outdated. But neither reason explains why the
recency requirement was dropped. We find that a time-bound condition is no
harder to read than one without a time limit. The staleness of the dates is a reason
to move the window forward, for example to the five years ending 2024-25. It is
not a reason to remove the window. The Appellant argued that the criteria were
revised afresh, but that describes what he did and not why. Before the DC and in
this appeal, the Appellant has given no reason, recorded or otherwise, for
dropping the five-year window. We find it quite significant in the present
situation.
104. The Appellant relies on the notice and agenda of the 13th meeting and on
a comparative statement of old and new criteria which he says was circulated. But

                                                 Page 49 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

even if it was circulated, Clause 16 asks for reasons and not just for text. A before-
and-after statement shows what changed. It does not show why. The minutes
record the two general reasons and are silent on this limb. The CoC approved the
revised criteria by e-voting, and the complainant also voted in favour. That does
not answer a duty to keep records. We are of clear view that the duty is the
professional's own, and it exists so that the Board, a later reader, the competing
PRAs and the Adjudicating Authority can see why he did what he did. The
approval of the CoC is relevant to how serious the lapse was, and we deal with it
below.
105. On the face of it, we find that the change was not minor. A recency
requirement is a safeguard for homebuyers. It shows that the PRA has delivered
projects in recent years and can deliver now. Without it, an old completion
qualifies. The comparative statement also shows other relaxations that were not
part of the charge and on which the DC made no finding: the turnover condition
was deleted, the experience condition was recast by reference to the Real Estate
(Regulation and Development) Act, 2016, and net worth was moved to the latest
audited statements. We make no finding on those. We only note that a package
of relaxations of this kind is where written reasons matter most.
106. We find that the DC faulted the Appellant for not specifically highlighting
the omission to the CoC and the AR and for not explaining it at the meeting. The
charge was the failure to keep written records. We hold Contravention II proved

                                                 Page 50 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

on the Clause 16 charge. Through Clause 16 the breach of Regulation 7(2)(a) and
(h) of the IP Regulations follows.
107. The Appellant submitted that the impugned order does not identify any
prejudice to the Corporate Debtor or its stakeholders from the revision. That is
correct as to the order, but Clause 16 is a duty of conduct and is not made out
only when loss follows. In our considered view, a professional who fails to record
the reasons for a decision breaks the clause whether or not the decision later
proves harmful. If it were otherwise the clause could be tested only after a loss,
and by then the record that the clause is meant to create would not exist.
108. We will also test the argument of the Appellant that no prejudice was
shown. That argument assumes that the removal of the five-year window was a
harmless drafting change. If the applicant that finally came out successful is one
that gained from the relaxation, then the removal had a real effect on who could
compete and who won. The point is that nobody can now tell, from the
Appellant's own record, why a relaxation with that effect was made. A CoC that
voted on the revised text, a PRA that was kept out by the old window, or the
Adjudicating Authority reading the file, cannot find a reason. When a relaxation
turns out to benefit the winning applicant, the absence of a recorded reason is the
very thing that leaves a doubt open that a record would have closed. We do not
say the doubt is well founded. We say the Appellant's failure left it open, and that
this is why Clause 16 exists.

109. The Appellant asks us to treat Contravention No. 2 as a mere record-
keeping lapse without consequence. On the facts as we are told, that is not so.
The gap in the record sits at the point where the process opened up to an applicant
who later succeeded. In a process in which the AR holding 91% of the votes was,
in the same period, being asked for concurrence in successive timeline extensions,
a resolution professional had a particular reason to keep careful paper on every
relaxation.
110. In view of the above discussion, we hold that the contravention No. 2 is
proved.

                       Proportionality of Penalty

111. We now come to the issue of measure or proportionality of penalty, and we
begin with the question whether a suspension of two years is in proportion. We
note that under Section 220(2), the DC, if satisfied that sufficient cause exists,
may impose a penalty, or suspend or cancel registration. We also note that
Regulation 13(3) of the Inspection and Investigation Regulations lets it close the
matter, issue a warning, suspend or cancel the authorisation for assignment, take
action under Section 220(2) to (4), refer the matter to the Board, or give any other
direction. So, the DC had a range of measures. In Ranjit Thakur v. Union of
India, (1987) 4 SCC 611, the Hon’ble Supreme Court held that a punishment so
disproportionate as to shock the conscience can be set aside. In Chairman-cum-
Managing Director, Coal India Ltd. v. Mukul Kumar Choudhuri, (2009) 15 SCC
620, the Hon’ble Supreme Court held that proportionality is part of judicial

                                                 Page 52 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

review of punishment, one test being whether any reasonable employer would
have imposed such a punishment. The Board's own authority, Umesh (supra)
lists disproportion among the grounds on which an outcome may be examined.
The test is a demanding one, and a measure within the range open to the expert
regulator is not to be disturbed because another authority might have chosen a
lighter one.
112. We note that the Hon’ble Delhi High Court in the case of Sandeep Kumar
Bhatt v. Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India, 2025: DHC:2252-DB
(L.P.A. No. 1054 of 2024, decided on 03.04.2025), reduced a two-year
suspension imposed on 01.11.2023 to the period already served, a little over one
year and four months. We note that the Hon’ble Delhi High Court did so because
the DC had overlooked material aspects of the case and the penalty was not
proportionate on that footing. In view of this ratio as well in ratio of our own
earlier case like Manish Motila Jajoo (supra), it can be concluded that this
Appellate Tribunal can interfere in these aspects of penalty, if so warranted in the
circumstances of the case.
113. We take into consideration that in the present case, the DC considered the
Appellant's explanation, his replies and his written submissions, found both
contraventions on the record, and has not overlooked any material aspect of the
charges. The Board also relies on the view that the disciplinary authority should
ordinarily fix the punishment and that a court should not substitute its own. That
view supports the Board here. We do not decide this appeal by comparing it with

                                                 Page 53 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

other orders. However, the Appellant has given a list of earlier DC orders to
show the range of measures used, and we have not verified it against the orders
themselves. We do not rest on it, but we say something about it later.
114. We begin with the order. Paras 3.1 and 3.2 of the Impugned Order repeat
the findings, and para 3.3 of the Impugned Order then suspends registration for
two years. The Impugned order does not say in so many words why two years, or
why a lesser measure was not enough, and it does not refer to the Appellant's
record. We find that the Regulation 13(1) requires a reasoned order, and a fuller
statement on the measure would have been better. But the reasons for the measure
can be read from the findings that precede it: two contraventions of different
kinds, a known rule not followed, and a gap in the record that touched the very
applicant that later succeeded. We therefore examine the measure on the record
before us, and we take into account the matters the order does not mention, to see
whether they would change the result.
115. We are of the opinion that the seriousness of what is proved weighs
heavily. There are two contraventions, and they are of different kinds. One is an
extension of time taken without the CoC that owns the date. The other
contravention is a relaxation of a safeguard for homebuyers made without a
written reason. In both, the Appellant did what only the CoC could do, or did
what the CoC was entitled to see explained, and left no paper. A pattern is visible,
namely that decisions that should have been the CoC's, or should have been
recorded for the CoC, were taken informally in a process where the AR

                                                 Page 54 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

representing homebuyers with 91% of the vote was in frequent contact and where
several extensions were being given. We hold that the Appellant knew the rule
on the extension, because he had followed it twice shortly before. On the second
contravention, on the facts as we are told, the gap in the record has a real bearing
on who benefited from the relaxation. The role of a resolution professional is, as
we have said, one of high trust, and lapses of process are the way in which that
trust is lost. A warning or a money penalty alone would not mark these lapses.
116. Against this stand the mitigating facts. We are aware that the DC found no
dishonesty, concealment, personal gain or loss to the estate, and none is found
here. We take into consideration the submissions of the Appellant that he was
enrolled on 27.06.2017, and has worked on 22 assignments as IRP, RP or
liquidator, and has had one inspection and one investigation of his work and a
complaint to his agency in 2023, with no violation found and no disciplinary
action in nine years. The Board does not deny this, and we take it as
uncontroverted. We reiterate that the AFA of the Appellant has been suspended
since 18.05.2026, so that he has taken no new assignment for more than five
months. The CoC took no exception to the e-mail, did not reject any plan as late,
and approved the revised criteria by a large majority. We state that the Impugned
order does not mention any of them, but we have weighed them. However, we
are of the view that these do not erase the contraventions, and the CoC's approval
in particular cannot take the place of the professional's own duty. These facts
also do not lead us to a lesser measure than the one chosen. The DC stopped short

                                                 Page 55 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

of cancellation, which was open to it, and that itself shows that it saw no
dishonesty or gain. A suspension of two years, in the case of two contraventions
in insolvency case of this magnitude involving thousands of homebuyers with a
real bearing on the outcome of a large homebuyer process, is a measured use of
a range that runs up to cancellation.
117. Recent orders of this Appellate Tribunal, in which two-year or longer
suspensions were not interfered with at the interim stage, involved professionals
found to have failed to disclose assets of very large value in a liquidation report,
to have sold a major asset privately without the consultation the regulations
require, or to have refused to admit a claim that the Adjudicating Authority and
this Appellate Tribunal had directed him to admit. In those cases, there was either
a large sum at stake or a refusal to follow judicial orders. The present case does
not carry either feature. It is a case of process lapses with no loss found and no
disobedience of an order. That is a fact that was properly weighed in choosing
suspension and not cancellation, and in stopping at two years. It is not a ground
for treating the lapses lightly, because the same trust is at stake in each case. The
period here is at the lower end of the periods this Appellate Tribunal has left
undisturbed in those cases.
118. Taking all this together, we find that suspension of registration is the right
kind of measure and that a warning or a money penalty alone would not mark
these lapses. A suspension of two years is within the range open to the DC and is
not so extreme as to shock the conscience. On one side are two contraventions of

                                                 Page 56 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

different kinds, the first done knowingly, the second with a real bearing on the
applicant that finally succeeded, in a process involving thousands of homebuyers
and by a professional of long standing who should have known better. On the
other side are the absence of dishonesty, gain, loss or disobedience of an order, a
clean record of nine years, the conduct of the CoC, and the restraint already
suffered. The first side calls for a long period. The second side explains why the
DC chose suspension and not cancellation, and it does not call for a period shorter
than two years. We therefore find the measure proportionate and decline to
interfere with it. We make this finding on the record, and it does not depend on
the brevity of the reasons in the Impugned order.
119. We have also considered whether the matter should go back to the DC to
record fuller reasons on the measure, and we do not think it should. The record is
complete, and the findings on which the measure rests are now confirmed by this
Appellate Tribunal with reasons. A remand would serve no purpose except delay.
This Appellate Tribunal gave no interim protection, and the Appellant has been
out of new work since 18.05.2026 and out of existing work since the order took
effect. A remand of this kind also invites every professional against whom the
Board has acted to ask for a second round on the measure. The Code runs on a
time-bound process and cannot afford that. In Deepak Kumar Goyal (supra),
we remitted a cancellation, which is final and where lesser measures had to be
considered afresh. We note that in contrast, in the present case, the DC has already

                                                 Page 57 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

chosen a lesser measure than cancellation, the record is complete, and the choice
is supported. The cases are therefore different.
120. We wish to make it clear that Lapses of process in a resolution are not
small. When a professional takes a decision that belongs to the CoC, or leaves no
record of why he changed a safeguard for homebuyers, he puts the whole process
at risk, and a suspension of two years is a fair response even where no loss or
dishonesty is found. Where lapses lead to harm, as in misappropriation of funds,
conflict of interest, concealment, disobedience of the orders of this Appellate
Tribunal or the Adjudicating Authority, or loss to large numbers of homebuyers
or creditors, longer suspension and cancellation are called for, and neither length
of service nor past record will stand in the way. A professional who has been
found to have contravened, and who has been given a reasoned measure within
the range, should not expect a second round on the measure.
121. We turn to paras 3.4 and 3.5 of the Impugned order. Para 3.4 of the
Impugned order says that, in terms of Section 206, the Appellant shall not be
eligible to continue in his existing assignments and directs the CoC of each
corporate debtor in which he is serving to replace him under Section 27. Para 3.5
of the Impugned order deals with when the order takes effect. Section 206 says
that no person shall render services as an insolvency professional under the Code
without being enrolled with an insolvency professional agency and registered
with the Board. As we read Regulation 13(6) of the Inspection and Investigation
Regulations, the professional must discharge his pending obligations and may

                                                 Page 58 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

continue only as long as is needed for the stakeholders to move to another
professional, and under Regulation 13(7) the Board must send the order to the
members of the CoC of every process in which he is acting and to the
Adjudicating Authority.
122. It is the case of the Appellant that the power to communicate the order
under Regulation 13(7) is different from a power to direct the CoCs to replace
him, that the CoCs of other corporate debtors are not before the DC, and that the
direction takes away the right of each CoC to decide whether to continue with
him. We note that the Appellant relied on an interim order of the Coordinate
Bench of this Appellate Tribunal, in case of Sachin Naveen Sinha v. Insolvency
and Bankruptcy Board of India, I.A. No. 4537 of 2026 in Company Appeal (AT)
(Ins) No. 1165 of 2026, in which the suspension was stayed in so far as it
concerned the professional's other assignments, leaving each CoC to decide
whether to continue with him.
123. That order was made at the interim stage on a prima facie view. Since then
this Appellate Tribunal, in Anshul Gupta (supra) and in Kannan
Tiruvengadam (supra), has taken the view, again at the interim stage, that a
suspension of registration under Section 220(2) disentitles the professional from
acting in any other process under the Code, that the Board must inform the CoCs
of the other assignments and the Adjudicating Authority, and that such intimation
and any resulting replacement are distinct from the CoC's power of replacement
under Section 27. The Hon’ble Supreme Court vide order dated 21.09.2026

                                                 Page 59 of 67
                                     Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1703 of 2026

passed in Civil Appeal No. 12985/ 2026 has also directed this Appellate Tribunal

to decide this appeal without being influenced by any observation made in that

only. We follow the view of the later orders of this Appellate Tribunal. As

recorded in our earlier judgments, we are of the firm view that the professional

whose registration stands suspended is not in a position to act as resolution

professional in other processes, and the stakeholders in those processes have to

be told and given the chance to move to another professional in an orderly way.

That is the purpose of Regulations 13(6) and 13(7).

128. We would like to reemphasise that where a CoC has fixed a date for plans
or revised proposals, any change to that date must be put to the CoC, or to its
authorised representative where the CoC has so authorised, before it is given. A

A relaxation is a decision that can change who competes and who wins. Third, a
resolution professional is not to decide for himself that a departure is minor. If he

unauthorised step into an authorised one for the purposes of professional conduct.
Professionals who run these processes are fully answerable for the way they run
them, and the Board and this Appellate Tribunal will expect strict adherence in
every process, large or small.

They are made in the interest of the resolution professionals and of the Board's
own orders, and they do not affect the outcome of this appeal. We suggest that an
order imposing a penalty should:

   a) set out, under separate heads, each contravention found and the factors that
       weigh against the professional as well as the factors in his favour, and the
       penalty suggested with reasons for the proposed period of penalty;

   b) state clearly the size of the CD in the CIRP of which misconduct has been

       creditors affected, and whether there was loss, gain, concealment or
       disobedience of an order, so that the reader can see why a case is grave or
       is not;

   c) record the insolvency professional's (RP’s/ IRPs) own history, including
       his earlier assignments and any earlier inspection or action which has been
       taken against him;

that, as he describes them, included use of the corporate debtor's resources for a
private purpose, appointment of a related person without disclosure, a large
undisclosed arbitral award, a professional negligence charge and large fees. We
have not checked the list against the orders and we do not rely on it. Even on his
description, the period in each case turns on its own facts, earlier orders do not
bind the DC, and we do not intend to reduce a measure that is within the range on
the strength of an unverified list. But, we agree to the proposition that a
professional who reads such a list will need to understand as to why his own case
has been treated differently, and the Board is best placed to answer that before it
is asked. A short comparison in the order serves consistency and is the best
protection of the Board's order on appeal. In the same way, a severe order in a

grave case should say plainly what made the case grave, so that it is clear to the
Resolution Professional (RP/IRP) why that case stands apart from a lapse of
process. Orders recorded in this way will also lessen the need for any second
round of measure/ penalty, either before the Board or before this Appellate
Tribunal.
