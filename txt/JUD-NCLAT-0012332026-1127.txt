Per: Justice N. Seshasayee, Member (Judicial)
       These instant Company appeals have been filed by Mr. Debashis Bose

and Ms. Pragya Jhunjhunwala, both of who are the suspended directors

of the corporate debtor, M/s. India Power Corporation Limited

(hereinafter would be referred to as IPCL). IPCL has provided corporate

guarantee for the financial facilities availed by M/s. Meenakshi Energy

Ltd., the principal borrower herein MEL), and it challenges the Order of

the Adjudicating Authority (NCLT), Hyderabad, dated 15.05.2026 in

                                              3
       CP/IB/205/2021, admitting IPCL to CIRP under Sec.7 IBC in a petition
       which the first respondent SBI had instituted.

1.2 This is the second innings this case is undergoing. Earlier, when the
       matter was before the Adjudicating Authority, and the pleadings were
       required to be completed, SBI had filed its rejoinder belatedly, and it filed
       I.A.1547 of 2022 to take its rejoinder on record. The Adjudicating
       Authority allowed it partially and had held that while it would take the
       rejoinder on record, it still would not consider it. This was challenged
       both by SBI and IPCL before this tribunal in Company Appeal (Ins) 87 of
       2023 and (Ins) 69 of 2023 and vide its Order dated 04.10.2023 both were
       dismissed. SBI chose to challenge the judgement in Civil Appeal No. 8178
       of 2023 before the Hon’ble Supreme Court. In the meantime, the
       Adjudicating Authority dismissed the very company petition filed under
       Sec.7 IBC, vide its Order dated 30.10.2023, on the ground that the debt
       claimed is not payable since the deed of corporate guarantee is void
       under Sec.23 of the Contract Act,1872. This order was challenged by
       the SBI in Company Appeal (Ins) 53 of 2024. While it was pending, the
       C.A.8178 of 2023 came up before the Hon’ble Supreme Court, and vide
       its judgment dated 14.02.2025 it was disposed of the same as below:

               “13. As we have set aside the order passed by the NCLAT, the
               matter should now go back to the NCLT for fresh consideration of
               Section 7 application.
               14. In this regard, the NCLAT shall pass appropriate order in the
               pending appeal filed by the State Bank of India keeping in mind
               our observations in this order.
               15. However, we clarify that we have otherwise not expressed
               any opinion on the merits of the case.

                                                                                                                          4
               16. The Section 7 application of the bank shall now be decided
               by the NCLT afresh strictly in accordance with law on its own
               merits.”
       In view of the same, this tribunal vide its Order dated 24.02.2025, closed
       the Company Appeal (Ins) 53 of 2024 filed against the Order dismissing
       the petition under Sec.7 IBC. Thereafter, the Adjudicating Authority
       heard the matter de novo, and this time, it chose to allow the petition
       and initiated CIRP against the corporate debtor. It is this Order which
       is now in challenge before us.

1.3 The quintessential point raised by IPCL is as to the validity of the deed of
       corporate guarantee. While it carried conviction with the Adjudicating
       Authority in the first instance, post remand, it took the opposite view.

2. A brief narration of material facts that provide the context for this appeal
       is provided as below:

       a) IPCL is an electricity distribution licensee in the State of West Bengal
          since 1920s and is engaged in its business for over a century. It is now
          governed by the Electricity Act, 2003, and the Regulations framed by
          the West Bengal Electricity Regulatory Commission (Licensing and
          conditions of License) Regulations, 2013 (hereinafter referred to as
          WBERC).

       b) Be that as it may, a certain Meenakshi Energy Private Limited (MEL),
          came to be promoted by M/s Engie Global Developments B.V (Engie)
          a Dutch subsidiary of a French company, and incorporated for
          engaging in the business of thermal electricity generation. It was
          proposed to be done in two phases.

3. The line of reasoning of the Adjudicating Authority in admitting IPCL to
       CIRP is that:

          a) The Corporate Guarantee was consciously executed by IPCL as part
              of the commercial arrangement and the same cannot be

                                                                                                                        11
   subsequently avoided because the transaction resulted in financial
   liability.
b) the absence of prior approval under Regulation 5.13.2 of the
   WBERC Regulations by itself will not render the Corporate
   Guarantee void or unenforceable under Section 23 of the Indian
   Contract Act, 1872 since neither the provisions of the Electricity Act
   nor the WBERC Regulation has expressly or impliedly render the
   deed guarantee as void merely because it was executed without the
   prior approval of WBERC. Also, such non regulatory compliances do
   not render the whole contract void.
c) IPCL had earlier challenged the validity of the Corporate Guarantee
   in Commercial O.S No. 266 of 2017, which was withdrawn without
   liberty to institute fresh proceedings. Further, its conduct and
   admissions in earlier proceedings acknowledged the subsistence of
   the debt, validity of the Guarantee and its liability as Corporate
   Guarantor, and therefore the IPCL is estopped.
d) The invocation and transfer of pledged shares did not, by
   themselves, extinguish the underlying debt by placing reliance on
   PTC India Financial Services Ltd. vs Venkateswarlu Kari and
   another [(2022) 9 SCC 704]. The debt and default of MEL stood
   established. The Corporate guarantee had been duly invoked,
   demand notices had been issued and IPCL had failed to discharge
   its liability and the existence of financial debt and default stood
   established.
e) Turning to limitation, by reckoning the period of limitation from the
   date of the failure to comply with the demand notice dated

                                                                                                             12
              20.12.2017 by IPCL, the petition filed by the SBI under Sec.7 IBC
              on 26.02.2020 was within the prescribed period of limitation. The
              requirements of existence of financial debt, occurrence of default
              and completeness of the application under Section 7 of the IBC were
              therefore satisfied.

Arguments

4. The learned counsel for the appellants argued that:
       a) the deed of Corporate Guarantee dated 23.09.2016 which IPCL in
          favour of the lenders of MEL is void and unenforceable for want of prior
          written consent of the WBERC, as mandatorily required under
          Regulation 5.13.2 of the WBERC (Licensing and Conditions of License)
          Regulations, 2013 under Sec. 23 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872,
          since its object or consideration is forbidden by law or would defeat
          the provisions of law. Consequently, no enforceable debt could arise
          against IPCL within the meaning of Sec. 3(11) and 3(12) of the IBC,
          and thus the essential requirement for admission of an application
          under Sec. 7 would remain unsatisfied. Reliance was placed on
          Innoventive Industries Ltd. Vs ICICI Bank [(2018) 1 SCC 407]; M.
          Suresh Kumar Reddy Vs Canara Bank, [(2023) 8 SCC 387]; and
          Power Trust Vs Hiranmaye Energy Ltd., [(2026) 5 SCC 38]. This
          apart admission of MEL into CIRP could establish only MEL’s liability
          and could not, by itself, establish the validity or enforceability of IPCL’s
          Corporate Guarantee.
       b) WBERC had, through its orders and subsequent proceedings, affirmed
          the requirement of prior consent for execution of the Corporate

                                                                                                                        13
   Guarantee. The lenders did not challenge the said determination
   before the appropriate appellate forum under the Electricity Act, 2003.
   Therefore, the determination had attained finality and could not be
   collaterally questioned before the Adjudicating Authority under Sec.
   60(5) of the IBC. Reliance was placed on Embassy Property
   Developments Pvt. Ltd. vs. State of Karnataka, [(2020) 13 SCC
   308]; Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam Ltd. vs. Amit Gupta, [(2021) 7 SCC
   209]; Tata Consultancy Services Ltd. vs. Vishal Ghisulal Jain,
   [(2022) 2 SCC 583]; State Bank of India vs. Union of India , [2026
   SCC OnLine SC 202]
c) the Electricity Act, 2003, together with the regulations framed
   thereunder, constitutes a special statutory framework governing
   electricity distribution licensee. The regulatory safeguards and prior-
   consent requirements applicable to IPCL cannot be disregarded merely
   by invoking the overriding provision under Section 238 of the IBC.
   Indeed, there is no irreconcilable conflict between the two enactments
   warranting the application of the overriding clause and that both
   statutes ought to be harmoniously construed. While the Adjudicating
   Authority relied on the decisions in Paschimanchal Vidyut Vitran
   Nigam Ltd., vs. Raman Ispat (P) Ltd., [(2023) 10 SCC 60], and
   Duncans Industries Ltd., vs. A.J. Agrochem, [(2019) 9 SCC 725],
   the same are distinguishable on facts and the context in which those
   decisions were rendered.
d) the affidavit furnished by IPCL’s Director, the representations made
   during negotiations, or any alleged admission on the part of IPCL
   cannot validate an agreement which is otherwise prohibited by law.

                                                                                                                 14
   The affidavit dated 23.09.2016, the Indemnity Deed and Clause 2.4 of
   the guarantee deed thereof, cannot cure the absence of mandatory
   statutory consent and make the Corporate Guarantee enforceable
   contrary to Sec. 23 of the Contract Act. Where a party aware of a
   statutory prohibition cannot seek the aid of the Court to enforce a
   contract made in its violation. Reliance was placed on Loop Telecom
   and Trading Ltd. vs. Union of India, [(2022) 6 SCC 762].
e) the withdrawal of Commercial Suit No. 266 of 2017, without
   adjudication on merits, cannot operate as a bar against IPCL
   questioning the validity of the Guarantee.
f) The Appellants contended that the requirement of prior approval
   under Regulation 5.13.2, being mandatory and backed by statutory
   penalties, amounts to a prohibition. Reliance was placed on Asha
   John Divianathan Vs Vikram Malhotra, [AIR 2021 SC 2932] and
   Mannalal Khetan Vs Kedar Nath Khetan, [(1977) 2 SCC 424]. Any
   contract entered into in breach of such statutory prohibition is void or
   unenforceable, even in the absence of an express provision declaring
   it so. Consequently, the Corporate Guarantee executed without the
   requisite approval cannot be enforced in law.
g) Alternatively, and at any rate, without prejudice to their primary
   contention regarding the invalidity of the Corporate Guarantee, Clause
   2.7 of the deed of Guarantee itself confines the contracted obligation
   of IPCL to its non-regulated assets. Indeed, the contract was drafted
   by the lenders or their legal team, and hence in terms of doctrine of
   contra proferentem, it binds the lenders. So far as the non-regulated
   assets are concerned, they were transferred by IPCL to its wholly

                                                                                                                 15
   owned subsidiary IPCL Power Ltd vide BTA dated 2.6.2025 during the
   pendency of CIRP. Reliance was placed on State of Maharashtra vs.
   M.N. Kaul, [1967 SCC OnLine SC 372], Syndicate Bank vs.
   Channaveerappa Beleri, [(2006) 11 SCC 506].
h) Even assuming the Guarantee to be enforceable, its limited recourse
   cannot justify initiation of CIRP against the entirety of IPCL, including
   its regulated electricity distribution undertaking and assets. It was
   contended that the IBC does not provide for a CIRP confined to
   specified assets and that admission of the Section 7 application would
   consequently expose assets and revenues which were never intended
   to secure the lenders’ facilities. The Resolution Professional however,
   had already assumed control over the licensed undertaking and that
   a sector-specific and modulated approach was necessary, having
   regard to the public utility character of IPCL’s business. Reliance was
   placed on S. Elangovan vs. ASREC (India) Ltd., [2022 SCC OnLine
   NCLAT 2859]; Mansi Brar Fernandes vs. Shubha Sharma, [2025
   INSC 1110]; and Gagan Tandon vs. IL&FS Financial Services Ltd.,
   [2026 SCC OnLine NCLAT 16].
i) the IBC is a mechanism for insolvency resolution and not a substitute
   for ordinary recovery proceedings. Where the very validity and
   enforceability of the Corporate Guarantee are seriously disputed and
   require adjudication, the insolvency process cannot be invoked merely
   as a means of recovery. Reliance was placed on Dhanlaxmi Bank Ltd.
   vs. Mohammed Javed Sultan, [2026 SCC OnLine SC 820], Swiss
   Ribbons (P) Ltd. vs. Union of India, [(2019) 4 SCC 17].

                                                                                                                 16

Discussion & Decision

6.1 The long narration of facts and the arguments built on them concern
       themselves principally with addressing a solitary issue: whether the deed
       of guarantee which IPCL had executed on 23.09.2016 is valid and
       establish the subsistence of an undisputed contractual relationship
       between IPCL and the SBI for holding the existence of a financial debt for
       the purposes of Sec.7 IBC, and the extent to which it creates a binding
       relationship. Looking just at this issue superficially, we do not consider
       that it is either novel or unfamiliar to this tribunal.

6.2 But this case is slightly different, for the defence which the corporate
       debtor has raised for defending the SBI’s petition for initiating CIRP
       against IPCL is alleged to be founded on a void agreement of guarantee
       under Sec.24 of the Contract Act, as its object is unlawful and opposed
       to public policy within the meaning of Sec.23 thereof. The pointed
       contention of IPCL is that it being an electricity distribution licensee, it
       is bound by the Electricity Act, 2003, and WBERC Regulations, and even
       though Regulation 5.13.2 requires prior consent of the Commission for
       executing the deed of guarantee, it has not been obtained, and hence it
       is unlawful. This leads to the next issue: how far should this tribunal

                                                                                                                        22
       engage itself with this issue, more so when the proceedings under the
       tribunal are summary in character?

6.3 This tribunal’s jurisdiction is well trimmed to fit in with the settled and
       well entrenched legal position: to ascertain whether the defence is
       bonafide and genuine, or is it a fakery. A more than a strong prima facie
       view to be obtained through the rule of preponderating probability. See
       the ratio in Deepak Raheja and another Vs Omkara Assets
       Reconstruction Private Ltd. [2026 SCC OnLine SC 2184]; Hardeep
       Singh Sawhney Vs Sawhney Builders Pvt., Ltd., [(2019)217
       Comp.Cases 506 (NCLAT)]; Progfin Pvt., Ltd., Vs Ganesh Benzoplast
       [(2026) ibclaw.in 786 (NCLAT)]. After all, in a summary proceeding for
       ascertaining the existence of debt and default, the twin criteria necessary
       for admitting a corporate debtor to CIRP, the document establishing
       contractual relationship between the corporate debtor and the financial
       creditor should either be beyond dispute, at least free of any probable
       dispute. Where the very document involved requires serious examination
       for its construction, then tribunal may not be the forum to approach.
       This therefore, leads to the next question: Whether this tribunal has
       authority to declare that the deed of guarantee which IPCL has
       indisputably executed, which SBI relies on fastening a liability – the
       financial debt, as void under Sec.24 read with Sec.23 of the Contract
       Act? This tribunal is conscious that in Umesh Kumar Vs Narendra
       Kumar Sharma [2024 SCC OnLine NCLAT 226], this tribunal has taken
       a view that it cannot go into the question of tenability or validity of a
       contractual agreement.

                                                                                                                        23
6.4 And there is an ancillary issue on limitation. But given the intricacies of
       the issue raised, this has taken the back seat.

7. As outlined earlier, the thrust of the arguments on either side is whether
       the deed of corporate guarantee is void. And the appellant also comes
       out with an alternative argument and contends that even if the deed of
       guarantee which IPCL has executed is considered valid, then its
       operation must be limited to separate business, and that it should not
       be construed as involving the entire business of the corporate debtor.

8.1. The deed of corporate guarantee came to be executed because when IPCL
       contemplated the takeover of MEL, and the lenders of MEL had set it as
       a precondition to enable it happen. IPCL did resist this idea, and on
       29.07.2016 it did inform the lenders that providing a corporate guarantee
       is prohibited by WBERC Regulations, but the lenders provided IPCL with
       a set of three legal opinions, all of which are to the effect that the
       Regulations only bar providing guarantee in respect of the ‘Regulated
       assets’ of IPCL and its ‘surplus amount’. The expressions ‘regulated
       asset’, or ‘surplus amount’ however, are coinage of those who have
       offered the legal opinion or drafted the deed of guarantee, whereas the
       WBERC Regulation employs the expressions such as Core, Non-Core,
       Other and Separate businesses or activities (to which pointed reference
       will be made in subsequent paragraphs). Here, we need to make few
       statements to the parties:

          a) We give no credence to the legal opinions which might have provided
              a context for IPCL to execute the guarantee deed, for IPCL is no child
              to complain that it was induced into executing it. It was keen on a

                                                                                                                        24
   commercial venture of acquiring MEL, and if it had felt that the pre-
   condition which the lenders of MEL had set would involve
   contravention of the Regulations and might invite complications to
   its core-business, it was free to disengage itself from its proposed
   commercial venture. It was justly ambitious with the keenness to
   expand its business, yielded to the demands of the lenders of MEL,
   and it cannot complain that it was induced by the lenders.
b) The second aspect is that, SBI also cannot plead innocence, as it
   knows before hand – before the deed of guarantee was executed that
   execution of the deed of guarantee as demanded by it would involve
   the need to obtain prior consent of WBERC before executing the
   guarantee deed.
c) Now that it is a bilateral document, both the parties have invested
   their creative intelligence or expertise to outmanoeuvre, if not to
   outsmart the provisions of Electricity Act and the statutory
   Regulations made thereunder through the backdoor usage of
   certain non-statutory expressions. Like IPCL, SBI also had its
   options: (i) either to withdraw the precondition of executing the deed
   of guarantee; or (ii) denying approval for IPCL’s takeover of MEL; or
   (iii) to wait for WBERC to apply its mind in terms of the Regulations.
   It did neither. In essence, both the parties were overdriven by
   commercial ambitions to pursue, and got into a contractual
   relationship through a consciously contrived drafting (of the
   agreement) to hoodwink the Electricity Act, its Regulations and the
   Regulator. And it is they who call themselves angel and the other
   the devil before us.

       a) Keeping the controversial deed of guarantee aside, somewhere in the

            through an affidavit dated 23.09.2016, executed for IPCL which was
            to the effect that executing a deed of corporate guarantee would not
            contravene the Regulations. This is least appealing for when the
            Regulations assert themselves on the strength of its statutory origin,
            it is not given to those whom the Regulations govern to stand against
            them. The affidavit lacks the strength to substitute the effect of the
            Regulations.
       b) Turning to the appellant, it has contended that when the Hon’ble
            Supreme Court in its judgement in C.A.8178 of 2023,

            argument also is a distraction, since the very judgement of the
            Hon’ble Supreme Court (whose operative portion is captured in
            paragraph 1.2 above) belies it.

11. In our endeavour, we consider it appropriate not just to confine our

a) Non-Regulated assets are negatively defined in Clause 1.1 of the
   guarantee-deed as those which are not Regulated assets.

   revenues and receivable of the business and operations of the
   Dishergarh Electric License. Therefore, ‘Regulated assets’, according
   to this definition, is mere trade receivables and other revenues of IPCL,
   and does not include the existing tangible assets with which IPCL is
   carrying its electricity distribution business.

c) This would now imply all the fixed and tangible assets of IPCL which
   are necessary for working the distribution licence has been made part
   of the definition of Non-Regulated Assets. Indeed, if the definition is
   closely read it does not exclude even the electricity distribution-license
   from the purview of Non-Regulated assets.

   available after deducting few heads of expenditure from the revenue
   and receivables (which constitute Regulated assets) obtained from
   working the distribution licence.

       provisions of the Act throw light on it.

15. The scheme of the Electricity Act indicates a legislative idea that one may
       walk into become an electricity distribution licencee with its ‘utility’
       (assets), but once it commences its business, it neither has absolute
       control over the profits it makes, nor any control over its utility which the
                                                                                                                        33
       right of ownership ordinarily vests in the owner of assets, nor will it have
       control over its assets even on quitting the business, as they now become
       liable for acquisition. It is therefore, mandatory that both the one who is
       a licensee, and the one who chooses to deal with it, ought to be aware
       how the scheme of Electricity Act works and the purpose behind it. As in
       the working of every statute, there is an internal logic about how
       Electricity Act is structured for its effective and integrated working of the
       different aspects it deals in.

16. Regulation 5.13.2 therefore has to be understood in the backdrop of the
       statutory provisions and in the context of the legislative anxiety to
       preserve the assets of the distribution business, which in terms of the
       definitions in the Regulations is the basis for the Core, Non-Core and
       Other businesses of the distribution licensee plus the income generated
       therefrom. This signifies the necessity for obtaining prior consent of the
       WBERC under Regulation 5.13.2 before providing any guarantee.

17. Now, if the terms of the agreement of guarantee are tested on the plane of
       the legislative intent, the ingenuity and the intent of the contracting
       parties to carefully sidestep Regulation 5.13.2 through a bilateral
       agreement becomes glaringly evident. In the guise of Non-Regulated
       assets and Surplus Amounts, the deed of guarantee has brought within
       its folds even the tangible and other fixed assets of IPCL and the surplus
       income which a distribution licensee may generate from its business.

18. The moment there is legislative space of granting consent implies that
       Regulation 5.13.2 does not prohibit offering a guarantee by a distribution
       licensee. After all, to ‘consent’ is not a synonym for to ‘bar’. It may not

                                                                                                                        34
       be difficult to fathom that there is considerable public interest involved
       in Regulation 5.13.2, as it only reflects the legislative intent to secure the
       assets of the distribution licensee from encumbrance, as could be
       gathered from the tenor of Sec.17(3), 20(1) and Proviso to Sec.51. Set in
       the context, it may be said that the need for insistence on prior consent
       of WBERC is aimed at balancing the public interest involved in
       distribution of electricity to public, with the commercial necessity of a
       distribution licensee to encumber its assets. The object behind this
       appears not to expose the public utility involved in the distribution of
       electricity to the consumers, which an electricity distribution licensee has
       undertaken, to the peril of being disrupted by the creditors of the licensee.
       It is hence Regulation 5.13.2 does not bar providing guarantee, but only
       insists that prior permission of the Commission be obtained before it is
       so given.

19.1 Pausing here, we now turn to the point on the validity and enforceability
       of the deed of guarantee in the context of its ability to feed a petition
       under Sec.7 of the Code, the appellant would come out with two
       arguments:

       a) that the deed of guarantee is void under Sec.23 of the Contract Act, as
          it is violative of public policy involved in Regulation 5.13.2; and

       b) at any rate, when the Commission in its third Order dated 22.12.2021,
          had held it to be so, this tribunal may not sit in appeal over the same.
          In other words, according to the appellant, it falls within the exclusive
          jurisdiction of the WBERC to test the deed of guarantee on the
          touchstone of Regulation 5.13.2 which it is empowered and duty bound

                                                                                                                        35
          to administer, and when once it has exercised its exclusive jurisdiction,
          it is not given to this tribunal, which is constituted for a limited
          purpose of resolving the insolvency of the corporate debtors, to
          evaluate the correctness of the decision of the WBERC. In plain terms,
          its contention is that this tribunal cannot overrule the finding/decision
          of the WBERC as an appellate tribunal over the latter not withstanding
          Sec.238 of the Code.

19.2 The respondent however, would contend:

       a) that only civil court has jurisdiction to declare that whether the deed
          of guarantee is void, and it is neither given to the WBERC nor this
          tribunal to pronounce on its voidness. Indeed, the appellant itself has
          approached the civil court twice, first with Commercial O.S.266 of
          2017 and then with Money suit 1 of 2022. While the former was
          withdrawn, the latter is still pending. And so far as the present case
          is concerned, inasmuch as the appellate tribunal is not vested with the
          power to declare the invalidity of the deed of guarantee under Sec.23
          of the Contract Act, it has to act on it.

       b) Regulation 5.13.2 on its face does not bar a licensee under the
          Electricity Act to give corporate guarantee, but only requires it to
          obtain prior permission of the Commission. And, in terms of Sec.142
          of the Electricity Act,2003, the consequence that would visit any
          breach of Regulations is penal action merely. Nowhere, either in the
          Act or in the Regulation is there a legislative intent indicated to hold
          that violation of Regulation 5.13.2 would render the deed of guarantee
          void.

                                                                                                                        36
        c) At any rate, Sec.238 of the Code will have overriding effect on all
            provisions to the contrary.

 20. Here two aspects arise: (a) whether the contract of guarantee is void under
       Sec.23 of the Contract Act on which the appellant laid considerable
       emphasis; and (b) effect of the Sec.238 of the Code.

21. First to the efficacy of Sec.238 of the Code to save the deed of guarantee.
       Macquarie Bank Ltd. Vs Shilpi Cable Technologies Ltd., [(2018) 2 SCC
       674, the Hon’ble Supreme Court has held that the non obstante clause in
       section 238 will operate only when there is an inconsistency between the
       IBC and any other enactment. In the instant case, both the Electricity Act
       and the Code operates in different and distinct spheres, and there is hardly
       any conflict in their working. Secondly, for invoking the Code, there need
       to exist a creditor-debtor relations which is traceable only to a contract.
       However, formation of contract consistent with the special requirement of
       a special statute (such as in the instant case where prior consent of
       WBERC is required for creating a creditor-guarantor relationship) cannot
       be considered to be inconsistent with the Code, for the Code will have
       relevance post the formation of the contract and requires the existence of
       rights that flow from the contract. Thirdly, and specific to the facts of this
       case, here is a scenario where the parties have contrived a strategy to
       hijack the criterion prescribed Regulation 5.13.2 for the formation of
       contract of guarantee through the special terms of the contract which they
       have chosen through crafty drafting, should the same be saved under
       Sec.238 of the Code? It appears to be more than a strong probability that
       tend to indicate that the parties to the deed of guarantee apparently have

                                                                                                                          37
     attempted to play fraud on the Electricity Act and the Regulations framed
     thereunder. It is not avoidance of a statutory procedure but evading it by
     subterfuge. And, to us, Sec.238 neither textually nor in spirit encourage
     fraud on statute.

22.1 Turning to the issue as to whether the deed of guarantee is unlawful or
       opposed to public policy under Sec.24 of the Contract Act, this has to be
       read in conjunction with Sec.23 of the Contract Act that defines what
       constitutes unlawful objective or consideration.

               “24. Agreements void, if considerations and objects unlawful in
               part.—If any part of a single consideration for one or more objects,
               or any one or any part of any one of several considerations for a
               single object, is unlawful, the agreement is void”
         Sec.23 of the Act reads:

               “23. What considerations and objects are lawful, and what
               not.—The consideration or object of an agreement is lawful,
               unless—
               it is forbidden by law;
               or is of such a nature that if permitted, it would defeat the
               provisions of any law; or
               is fraudulent; or
               involves or implies injury to the person or property of another; or
               the Court regards it as immoral, or opposed to public policy.
               In each of these cases, the consideration or object of an
               agreement is said to be unlawful. Every agreement of which the
               object or consideration is unlawful is void.”
       For a contract to be rendered void, as stated, its object or consideration
       must be unlawful. However, if Regulation 5.13.2 is analysed to ascertain

                                                                                                                        38
       the same, as explained it has not barred the grant of guarantee but only
       insists that it may be done but only with the prior consent.

22.2 Here the SBI would contend that nowhere in the Electricity Act or in the
       Regulations it is declared that the breach of Regulation 5.13.2 would
       render the execution of guarantee sans the prior consent of WBERC void,
       and that it only invites penalty under Sec.142 of the Electricity Act.
       Sec.142 provides that the Appropriate Authority (the Central or the State
       Government, as the case may be) can impose a penalty not exceeding
       ₹1.0 lakh for each contravention, and in case of continuing contravention
       ₹6,000/- for every day of such contravention. And this is in addition to
       any other penal action to which the party breaching the Act or the
       Regulation may be otherwise exposed to. Does it not then imply that the
       respondent, by its own statement attempt to legitimise that which is
       forbidden by law. And, a contract whose object or consideration is
       forbidden by law is equally void under Sec.24 read with Sec.23 of the
       Contract Act.

22.3 In the context the following passage in paragraph 26 of the judgement of
       the three Judges bench of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Asha John
       Divianathan Vs Vikram Malhotra [(2021)19 SCC 629 : AIR 2021 SC
       2932 ] is apposite. It reads:

               “It is well established that a contract is void if prohibited by a
               statute under a penalty, even without express declaration that
               the contract is void, because such a penalty implies a prohibition.
               Further, it is settled that prohibition and negative words can
               rarely be directory…. although it may be a case of seeking
               previous permission it is in the nature of prohibition as
               observed by a three-Judge Bench of this Court in Mannalal

                                                                                                                        39
               Khetan v. Kedar Nath Khetan. In every case where a statute
               imposes a penalty for doing an act, though, the act not prohibited,
               yet the thing is unlawful because it is not intended that a statute
               would impose a penalty for a lawful act. When penalty is imposed
               by statute for the purpose of preventing something from being
               done on some ground of public policy, the thing prohibited, if
               done, will be treated as void, even though the penalty if imposed
               is not enforceable.”
       This law has been candidly declared. We therefore, need not now delve
       on the extent of our authority to hold the deed of guarantee void, but in
       the context of the law declared by the Hon’ble Supreme Court, can we
       blindfold ourselves and continue to treat the same to be valid? Thus even
       though the debt, and default in repayment are otherwise established, yet
       in the instant case the enforceability of the agreement of guarantee has
       come seriously under the scanner. It is not the public interest involved in
       the core, non-core businesses of IPCL, but the over-ambitious attempt to
       outsmart the law and the Regulators that it constituted through a
       customised designer-agreement of guarantee that has spoiled the fortune
       for the SBI in this appeal.

23. It is precisely for this reason, the enforceability of the deed of guarantee
       as regards the application of ‘surplus amount’ also comes under the
       forensic scanner. If the definition of Non-Regulated assets of IPCL in the
       guarantee-deed creates an unassailable predicament for it to feed a
       contractual relationship between IPCL and the SBI vis-à-vis the former’s
       core, non-core and other business, then as a corollary, even the income
       that are generated from those assets may not answer the claim of SBI,
       more so when they also come under the purview of Regulators in tariff-
       fixation.
                                                                                                                        40
24. Now, we propose to address the other extreme view or argument which
       the appellants have made. It is on the effect of the Order of the WBERC,
       dated 22.12.2021, the Third Order. If it is perused, the Regulatory
       Commission has only focused on the need to obtain prior permission
       under Regulation 5.13.2 but without considering the express terms of the
       deed of guarantee limiting its scope. And it has not chosen to explain how
       Separate Business or the Surplus Amount from Other Business and
       Separate Business of IPCL will fall within its purview of its jurisdiction
       under Regulation 5.15.1. Interestingly enough the appellant after losing
       twice successively in its attempt to obtain ex-post facto permission under
       Regulation 5.13.2 (and without disclosing about the execution of the deed
       of guarantee) has moved the WBERC on the third occasion only under
       Regulation 5.15.1. It reads:

               “Regulation 5.15.1 The licensee shall refer all disputes under the
               licence relating to interpretation of the licence and the terms and
               conditions thereof to the Commission for their determination.”
       Under the aforesaid Regulation, WBERC’s jurisdiction may be invoked
       only for interpreting the license when a dispute involving the
       interpretation of the licence arises. Given the facts of this case, there is
       neither a dispute over the interpretation of the licence involved, nor the
       WBERC has the authority to interpret any other document other than a
       licence. Very obviously the observation of WBERC on the validity of the
       deed of guarantee is without jurisdiction. At any rate it cannot affect the
       Separate Business or Income from Other and Separate businesses of
       IPCL. The conflict of jurisdiction between the WBERC and this tribunal
       may arise only where WBERC has acted within its jurisdiction. Therefore,

                                                                                                                        41
       the need to consider whether it requires a suit to declare that the deed of
       guarantee is void or to ascertain how far Sec.238 of the Code will impact
       this case will only have academic relevance, and we avoid dealing the
       issue as it is plainly unnecessary.

25. The respondent has put forth another contention, when it argued that
       IPCL has executed a deed of indemnity dated 23.09.2016. This however,
       is seen to have been executed only in favour of Rural Electrification
       Corporation Ltd., (REC) and not the SBI. Secondly, it is doubtful if this
       indemnity document without any qualification can affect the core-
       business of IPCL, for that might interfere with the Regulations. At any
       rate this issue has to be left open as it does not merit consideration in
       this appeal. Therefore, even the argument based on the Indemnity Deed
       dated 23.09.2016 is of no avail to the respondent.

26. To sum up, since the extent of our authority to state the most obvious
       can be doubted or debated, led by the rule of preponderating probability,
       we merely hold that there is more than a strong prima facie case to
       conclude that there is considerable merit in the contention of the
       appellants that we decide that it will not be safe to hold that the deed of
       guarantee dated 23.09.2016 has created a legal relationship between
       IPCL and the SBI, for the latter to invoke Sec.7 IBC. As stated earlier, the
       SBI knew it beforehand when IPCL alerted it that offering guarantee
       might have to negotiate Regulation 5.13.2. While both IPCL and SBI
       joined to execute the deed of guarantee, today SBI might feel stranded for
       losing an advantage, which it might have thought that it had secured
       through the deed of corporate guarantee for realising its debts of MEL,

                                                                                                                        42
       but is it not true in pari delicto potior est conditio defendentis ? Law
       asserts, and it shall at all times, if the rule of law should prevail. And, the
       law serves best those who serve it best.

27. Having stated thus, all is not lost entirely for SBI yet, for it has still the
       Separate Business of IPCL, its assets and income therefor, to secure the
       debt, for they do not come under the purview of the Regulators under the
       Electricity Act. Indeed, the IPCL itself has listed a list of assets of its
       separate business which according to it, it has transferred to its cent
       percent subsidiary, IPCL Power Limited’ (formerly known as – Parmeshi
       Energy Limited). And the assets read as below: (i) 25 MW wind power
       plant in Gujarat; (ii) Smart metering project in Madhya Pradesh; (iii)133
       MW solar project in Maharashtra; (iv)150 KW distributed solar project for
       large consumers; and (v) 50% stake in a JV company with Uniper SE.

28. There is one last aspect which requires to be addressed. It is on Limitation.
       The appellant submitted that the respondent had stated different dates
       of default at different places. In Part IV of the application, SBI has merely
       stated “default as on 31.01.2020” without expressly mentioning the date
       of default, whereas in the list of dates it was mentioned as 28.12.2017.
       In the rejoinder, SBI states that the date of default to be the 7th day from
       the date of second demand notice issued on 07.02.2020. The
       Adjudicating Authority however reckoned the date of default from the
       date of failure to comply with the first demand notice dated 20.12.2017.
       In this instant case, the Sec.7 application was filed on 26.02.2020, it is
       within time irrespective of which of the three dates - 28.12.2017,
       31.01.2020 or 15.02.2020 that could be gathered from the records. The

                                                                                                                        43
       appellant herein is not a stranger to the loan transaction and he knows
       and at all times ought to know the facts pertaining to the loan
       transaction. Hence a petition filed without specifying the date of default
       ipso facto does not feed a plea of bar of limitation for instituting creditor’s
       petition under Sec.7 of the Code. This precisely has been the view taken
       in Manmohan Singh Jain Vs State Bank of India [(2021) SCC OnLine
       NCLAT 5983], and few other authorities on the aspect. The plea founded
       on bar of limitation which the appellant has raised, thus fails.

29. Now inasmuch as the deed of guarantee executed by IPCL is partially
       valid, and the default in repayment of debt by the principal borrower MEL
       is not disputed, IPCL cannot escape its obligation to repay the debts of
       the MEL as its corporate guarantor but that will be limited only to the
       extent of the assets of its separate business and surplus amount from its
       other and separate businesses.

30. We therefore have little hesitation in holding that the Order of the
       Adjudicating Authority directing initiation of CIRP of IPCL does not
       require to be interfered with. But the game still has not ended, and as
       we are still required to provide necessary directions to the resolution
       professional as to the nature of assets of IPCL which she is under a
       statutory duty to manage. This has become necessary since despite the
       fact that the deed of guarantee as provided by IPCL is limited, she has
       taken control IPCL in its entirety, for which we do not find any
       justification. We therefore, do not consider it to be safe to leave to her
       judgement as to the specific assets that she may bring under her
       management any longer.

                                                                                                                        44
31. In conclusion, we hold:

       a) The deed of guarantee, dated 23.09.2016, to the extent it relates
          to the separate business of IPCL, is enforceable in law and hence
          it can be acted upon to that extent it has created a legal
          relationship between IPCL and SBI for sustaining the finding of
          the Adjudicating Authority in its judgement, dated 15.05.2026,
          about the existence of financial debt and its default.

       b) Only the separate business and income from the separate
          business will alone be liable to answer the obligations created by
          the deed of guarantee dated 23.09.2016.

       c) As to the liability of the surplus income generated from the core,
          non-core and other businesses of IPCL, the Resolution
          Professional is directed to approach the WBERC, for the WBERC
          retains control over the profits of these businesses.

       d) The resolution professional is directed to take charge of the assets
          of the separate business of IPCL, if it has not already taken plus
          income therefrom, which are now held by its wholly owned
          subsidiary, ‘IPCL Power Limited’ (formerly known as – Parmeshi
          Energy Limited).

       e) If the resolution professional, has materials with him to show any
          omission on the part of IPCL to transfer any of its specific asset of
          its separate business, she is permitted to apply to the
          Adjudicating Authority for obtaining the same. And, if any such
          application is moved, the Adjudicating Authority is required to

                                                                                                                        45
       pass appropriate orders on the same but only after granting IPCL
       an opportunity of being heard in the matter.

   f) The resolution professional is directed to handover the core, non-
       core and other business (as defined by the WBERC Regulation) of
       IPCL to its erstwhile management immediately after 30 days from
       today. And she will preserve the present status quo till then,
       except keeping the IPCL as a going concern.

   g) Anything which the resolution professional might have
       undertaken to affect the assets of the core, non-core and other
       businesses (as defined by the WBERC Regulation) of the IPCL
       pursuant to the impugned judgment of the Adjudicating Authority
       shall stand set aside.

The appeal is disposed of in the manner indicated above. No costs.
